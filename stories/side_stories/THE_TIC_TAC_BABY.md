## THE LIVING ROOM, EVENING

The Kovač living room in November of 2004 smelled like the inside of a bread bag and the ghost of Ante's cigarettes, the ones he'd quit in the spring and still smoked twice a month on the back step when he thought no one could smell it on his flannel. Everyone could. Monique never said anything. Some kindnesses are just silences you decide to keep.

The TV was a Sony Trinitron the size of a filing cabinet, and it threw a blue-gray light across the carpet that made the room feel colder than it was. The heat hadn't kicked on yet — Ante rationed the thermostat like a man defusing a bomb, one degree at a time — so Monique had a blanket over her knees and Marko, two years old and furious about bedtime, was fighting sleep in her lap with the doomed determination of the very small.

And Zara, three, sat on the floor exactly eighteen inches from the screen, where she was not supposed to sit, in the cross-legged stillness that her mother had long ago stopped finding cute and started finding *strange*.

Because Zara did not watch television the way children watch television. Other kids at the daycare — she had studied them, the way you'd study a foreign film without subtitles — leaned and lolled and narrated and asked *why* forty times a minute. Zara watched like a technician monitoring an instrument. Motionless. Complete. Her small face doing the specific nothing that made adults, without knowing why, lower their voices around her.

Tonight the news was on. Monique wasn't really watching it; it was just the sound that filled a house at that hour, the anchor's voice like furniture. A story about the election, still fresh and raw that month. A story about gas prices. A story, near the end, in the slot they reserved for the strange and the harmless, about *Navy pilots and a mystery off the coast of California.*

Zara's spine changed.

Monique noticed it the way you notice a fridge stop humming — not the sound of something starting, but the sudden absence of the small motion that had been there. Her daughter, who was already still, became *more* still. A held-breath stillness. The stillness of an animal that has heard, under all the ordinary noises of the world, the one specific sound it was built to fear or love.

## THE THING ON THE SCREEN

There was almost nothing to see, which is the part Monique would remember later, when she remembered it at all, which was almost never. The news had no real footage — this was 2004, the tape that would eventually leak was still locked in a vault, still years from being a thing the whole world argued about. So the screen just showed a stock photo of a fighter jet, and a graphic of the California coastline, and the anchor saying words like *unidentified* and *radar* and *the pilots reported an object that moved in ways they could not explain* in the mildly amused tone news anchors use for stories they've been told not to take seriously.

An object, the anchor said, shaped *like a Tic Tac.* Like the little white breath mint. He smiled when he said it. That was the point of the shape-comparison, though no one in the room knew it: to make a forty-foot craft that outflew the United States Navy sound like a piece of candy. To make you smile. To make it small.

Zara did not smile.

Zara lifted her hand — her whole arm, slow, deliberate, a gesture with none of the flailing overshoot of a toddler's reach — and pointed one finger at the screen. Not at the jet. Not at the map. At the empty gray space *beside* the stock photo, the blank corner of the frame where nothing was, where the real thing would have been if the camera had been pointed the right way.

And she made a sound.

Not a word. Not babble. A single, low, sustained tone, held on one breath, so quiet that Monique felt it in her sternum before she consciously heard it, a note under the note of the room. It lasted maybe four seconds. And in Marko's lap-sleep it must have carried, because the baby — who had finally, blessedly, gone limp against his mother's arm — stirred and whimpered and pressed his face harder into her, as if hiding.

Monique said, "Zara? *Dušo?*" — *sweetheart* — and the sound stopped, and Zara lowered her arm, and turned her head, and looked at her mother with an expression that a three-year-old's face is not supposed to be able to make. Not fear. Not excitement. Something worse, because it was so much older than the face wearing it:

*Recognition.* And under the recognition, grief.

## WHAT SHE KNEW (AND COULDN'T SAY)

Here is what Zara understood, sitting eighteen inches from a Trinitron in a cold living room in 2004, that she had no mouth capable of saying and no listener capable of hearing:

She knew the flight signature. Not the shape — the shape was a lie the news told, a breath-mint cartoon laid over the truth. She knew the *movement.* The way the anchor's own words described it — dropping tens of thousands of feet in an instant, no sonic boom, no exhaust, changing direction without an arc — she knew that. She had *ridden* things that moved like that. She had built the roads such things traveled on, in a life the color of home, before she was folded into a screaming pink infant and set down in a country called America in a body that couldn't point straight until this year.

The Navy had found a Shepherd craft. One of the maintenance vessels. Off the coast of California, going about the ancient patient business of tending a well beneath the ocean, and a carrier group had blundered into it and chased it and lost, and now a smiling man on television was calling it a *Tic Tac* so that everyone watching would file it under *funny* and go to bed.

And Zara — who remembered everything, who had woken every day of her three years into the full unbearable clarity of an exile — Zara looked at the gray corner of the screen where her own kind had just been and were now already gone, unreachable, uncontactable, four hundred miles away and might as well have been in another octave, and she felt the specific loneliness that had no bottom: *they were RIGHT THERE. And they don't know I'm here. And I can't tell them. And no one in this room will ever understand that the funny breath-mint story is the closest I have come, in three years, to seeing home.*

She was three. She could say *juice* and *no* and *Mama* and *Marko.* She could not say any of that.

So she pointed. And she hummed the four-second tone — which was, though no human would ever decode it, a *hail.* The oldest hail her kind had. A note that meant, roughly: *I am here. I see you. Come back.* Sent at a craft that had already left, on a frequency no television could carry, by a toddler on a carpet, to no one.

## THE PART MONIQUE KEPT

Monique turned the TV off after that. Not because of Zara — or not consciously because of Zara. She just decided, the way you decide a hundred small things a night, that Marko needed to go down and the news was depressing and the room was cold. She scooped the baby. She held out her free hand. "Bed, *mišu.* Both of you."

Zara stood. She did the thing she always did — she performed *tired toddler*, rubbing one eye with a small fist, letting her mother lead her by the hand down the hall past the framed photos, past the thermostat Ante guarded, into the small warm dark of the room she'd share with Marko until they were older. She let Monique tuck the blanket. She let Monique kiss her forehead. She said "night night" in the small round voice she'd engineered specifically to sound like every other three-year-old on Earth.

And when the door was almost closed, when the strip of hall light had narrowed to a blade across the carpet, Monique paused. She would not have been able to tell you why. She looked back at her daughter — a shape in a small bed, eyes open in the dark, catching the last of the light — and something moved through her that she had no name for and would forget by morning. A cold. A largeness. The sense, gone as soon as it came, that she had just tucked in something that was pretending, very kindly, to be her little girl.

"I love you," Monique said, into the dark, to fill it.

"Love you," said the small voice.

And it was true — that was the thing that would have broken Monique's heart if she'd understood it. It *was* true. Za-Rah, thousand-year exile, frequency-highway builder, three years into a life of unbearable solitude, did love this tired kind woman in the doorway. Loved her the way you love the warm strangers who take you in when you have washed up on the far shore of everything. Not the love the body was built to give a mother. A vaster, sadder, more grateful love, aimed at a person who could never know its true size.

The door closed. The hall light went out.

And in the dark, three-year-old Zara lay still, and did not cry, because crying was a performance and there was no one left to perform for, and she listened — down, and down, and down, below the furnace and the pipes and Marko's breathing — for a four-second tone coming back across four hundred miles of night.

None came. None would. Not for twenty-two more years.

But she listened anyway. She would always listen.

That was the whole of it. A baby watched the news. Nothing happened. A craft off California, a smiling anchor, a breath-mint joke, a mother's forgotten shiver, a toddler pointing at an empty gray corner of a screen and humming a hail to no one.

The most ordinary evening. The loneliest thing in the world.

*In November 2004, the USS Nimitz carrier group tracked and pursued an object off the California coast that moved in ways no aircraft could — the "Tic Tac." The public wouldn't see the footage for over a decade; at the time it was a light human-interest oddity on the evening news, the breath-mint nickname doing the cover's work for free. Signal Decay truth: it was a Shepherd craft tending an undersea well — and four hundred miles inland, a three-year-old who remembered building such craft's roads watched the news go still, and hailed her own kind, and was not heard.*

*She was two years and some months from being able to run. She had already been alone longer than the country she lived in had existed.*
