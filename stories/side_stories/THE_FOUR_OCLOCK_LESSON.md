# THE FOUR O'CLOCK LESSON

*Life & Times Side-Quest · Mira · a weekday afternoon, present day (2026)*
*Register: slice-of-life — a teaching hour, a gentle undertone*
*Created: 2026-09-11*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. A guitar teacher gives a lesson to an eight-year-old. That's it. It might be the most quietly hopeful thing in this whole universe.*

---

## 3:55 PM

Mira Chen taught private guitar lessons out of the back room of a music store called Fret Not, which was a pun so bad she'd grown to love it, and her four o'clock on Tuesdays was a kid named Theo who was eight years old and had been playing for exactly six weeks.

Theo was, by any measure, not good yet. This is the honest truth about teaching a beginner: for the first while, it is mostly noise. Buzzing strings. Fingers in the wrong place. The heartbreaking little pause before every chord change while the whole hand reorganizes itself from scratch. A person learning guitar at eight sounds, most of the time, like a person *not* learning guitar, and the entire job of the teacher is to hear the thing that isn't there yet and love it anyway, out loud, so the kid keeps coming back.

Mira was extraordinarily good at this, and the reason was a little strange, and she never told anyone.

Because Mira didn't just *hear* Theo play. She *saw* it.

She'd seen color in sound since before she could remember — she was born with it, the entities were the first faces she saw, the whole world came to her layered and chromatic and constant. For most of her life this had been a curse: a thing that got her misdiagnosed at twelve, a thing she learned to never mention, a thing that made her *the crazy girl.* But in the back room of Fret Not, on a Tuesday at four o'clock, with an eight-year-old butchering a G chord, the chromatic sight was the best teaching tool that has ever existed, and no one knew she had it.

She could see Theo's sound. A clean note came off his cheap little three-quarter-size guitar as a clear color — a specific warm amber for the open G string, when he hit it true. A buzzed, half-fretted note came off muddy, a brown-gray smear. And a note played with actual *feeling* — rare, in a six-week beginner, but it happened — came off brighter and deeper than the note had any right to, a color with light inside it.

So Mira didn't have to guess whether Theo was improving. She could see it. And today, at four o'clock, she saw something she'd been waiting six weeks for.

---

## THE CHORD

"Okay," Mira said. "G to C. Slow. I don't care about slow. Slow is the whole game."

Theo set his small fingers, tongue between his teeth in concentration, and made the G. Amber. Clean-ish. Then the terrible pause — the whole hand lifting off and reassembling — and then the C.

And the C *rang.*

It wasn't perfect. His pinky buzzed a little. But he'd changed chords without stopping the strum, for the first time, the two shapes connected by an actual strummed beat instead of a five-second silence, and to Mira's eye the sound came off the guitar in a color she'd been watching him fail to make for six weeks — a clean bright thing with light in the middle of it, the color of a kid who just did the thing.

Theo didn't notice. That was the beautiful part. Theo was eight and still frowning at his own pinky, already convinced he'd messed it up. He looked up at Mira, bracing for the correction.

And Mira said, with the specific joy of a teacher who has just watched the exact thing she lives for: "*Theo.* Do that again."

"It buzzed—"

"Do it again. Don't fix the buzz. Do the change again, the same way."

He did it again. G to C, connected, the color coming off bright. Then a third time. And on the third time Mira watched it land in his face — the moment the kid *heard* what he'd done, heard the two chords become a piece of music instead of two separate problems, and his whole eight-year-old face opened up into the particular astonishment of *I did that. That was me. I made that sound.*

"That," Mira said, "is guitar. Everything else is just more of that. You just played guitar, Theo."

He grinned so wide it looked painful. He immediately did it four more times, worse each time because now he was excited and rushing, and Mira let him, because you let them. You never, ever step on the moment a kid discovers they can do the thing. That moment is the whole reason to teach. Everything else is scales.

---

## WHAT SHE DIDN'T SAY

Here is what Mira Chen thought, and would never say, watching an eight-year-old rush through a G-to-C change with light coming off it:

She thought about being eight herself. She thought about the psychiatrist's office and her father crying in the car and the decision she'd made in the back seat — *never tell them anything again.* She thought about a whole childhood of being told that the thing she saw wasn't real, that she was sick, that the colors and the shapes and the faces in the corners of rooms were symptoms to be monitored. She thought about how long it had taken her to stop being afraid of her own sight, and how she still hadn't fully, and how she probably never would.

And she thought: *but it lets me do THIS.* It lets me see, before the kid himself can hear it, the exact moment he becomes a musician. It lets me catch the bright color coming off a buzzed C and tell him *do it again* at precisely the right second. The thing they told me was broken is the thing that makes me the best teacher this kid will ever have, and he'll never know, and that's okay, because he doesn't need to know. He just needs someone who can see him do the thing and say *that was you* at the exact right moment. And I can do that. Because of the thing they said was wrong with me.

She didn't say any of that. She said, "Okay, hotshot, before you get cocky — same change, but now we're gonna do it in time. Ready? One, two, three, four—"

And Theo, eight years old, six weeks in, played G to C in time, badly, gloriously, a start of a musician, light coming off it, and Mira Chen sat in the back room of a store called Fret Not and did the best job in the world.

---

## 4:50 PM

Theo's mom came at the end, the way parents do, hovering in the doorway. "How's he doing?"

And Mira — who could have said *fine* or *good* or any of the things teachers say — looked at Theo packing up his little guitar with the reverence of a kid who'd just fallen in love, and said what was true:

"He changed chords today. Connected them. That's the hard part, the part a lot of people quit before. He didn't quit. He's a guitar player now. It's just going to be a while before he believes me."

Theo's mom looked at her kid like she was seeing him a little differently, and Theo pretended not to hear but stood up an inch straighter, and Mira thought about the color that had come off that first connected C, the bright one with light in it, and kept it to herself like she kept everything to herself, a private beautiful secret in a back room.

She had another student at five — Marcus, thirty-four, intermediate, took it too seriously, needed to be told to have fun. Then she'd close up and go home and maybe, if the apartment was empty and the night was right, play the aggressive stuff, the heavy stuff, the metalcore-adjacent thing that would one day become a band's sound — and maybe the ghost band would form, and maybe the vast dark thing behind them, and maybe she'd wake up somewhere she didn't remember going. That was the night. That was the strange part of her life.

But four o'clock was Theo. Four o'clock was a kid discovering he could make music, and a teacher who could see it happen in color and had learned, at last, to be grateful for the sight instead of afraid of it. Four o'clock was the ordinary part. The good part.

She waved them out, and swept up, and got her water, and waited for Marcus, and hummed a little without noticing, and the back room of Fret Not filled up, just for a second, with a color only she could see.

---

*No entities take over, no blackout, no roof. Just a Tuesday guitar lesson: an eight-year-old connects two chords for the first time and becomes a musician, and his teacher watches it happen. The only trace of what Mira is — she doesn't just hear her students, she SEES their sound in color, and the chromatic sight that got her called "the crazy girl" at twelve is the exact thing that makes her able to catch the bright color of a kid's first real chord and say "do it again" at precisely the right moment. The curse, being a gift, in a back room, where no one will ever know. Some of the best hours are the ones with no monsters in them at all.*
