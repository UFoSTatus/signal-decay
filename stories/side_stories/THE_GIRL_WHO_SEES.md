# THE GIRL WHO SEES
## The Story of Soleil "Sunny" Martinez

---

*For every kid who carried something too heavy, too early, too alone.*

---

# PART 1: AGE 8 (1993) — "THE BEGINNING"

---

The first one Sunny ever noticed was the lady in the kitchen.

She was standing by the stove — a tall woman in a blue housedress with her hair pinned up in a way nobody wore it anymore, curlers wrapped in the back like she'd just forgotten to take them out. She was stirring something. The burner wasn't on. There was no pot. But her arm moved in slow circles, and her lips pressed together in concentration, and she hummed something that had no melody, just a vibration Sunny felt in her back teeth.

Sunny was eight years old. She wore a purple nightgown with a juice stain on the collar and her thick black glasses were slightly crooked because she'd slept in them again. It was a Saturday morning in October of 1993, and she wanted Froot Loops.

"Excuse me," Sunny said, because her mother had raised her polite. She slid past the woman to get to the cereal cabinet.

The woman didn't move. Didn't stop stirring. Didn't look down.

Sunny poured her Froot Loops. Added milk. Got a spoon from the drawer — which meant reaching through the space where the woman's hip was, and that felt like pushing your hand into the cold part of the pool where the sun doesn't reach. But she got her spoon.

She carried her bowl into the living room and turned on *Bobby's World* and ate her cereal cross-legged on the carpet.

Her mother came downstairs twenty minutes later, yawning, Carmen Martinez in her pink terry cloth robe, her own thick body wrapped up warm against the morning.

"Mami," Sunny said, not looking away from the TV, "who's the lady in the kitchen?"

Carmen paused mid-yawn. "What lady, baby?"

"The tall one. By the stove. She's always stirring something."

Carmen went into the kitchen. Came back. Sat on the edge of the couch and looked at her daughter the way she looked at her sometimes — like she was trying to read a book written in a language she'd almost forgotten.

"There's nobody in the kitchen, Sunny."

"She's there RIGHT NOW."

"Baby. There's nobody."

Sunny looked at her mother. Looked back at the TV. Ate another spoonful of Froot Loops, the pink and purple and green ones she always saved for last.

"Okay," she said.

And that was it. That was the beginning — not with a scream or a horror-movie jolt or a dramatic revelation. It began with a child saying *okay* and going back to her cartoons because she didn't have the framework yet to understand that what she'd just described was impossible.

---

She'd been seeing them for as long as she could remember, but she didn't know they were different from anyone else until that morning. Until her mother said *there's nobody there*. Before that, they were just... people. People who were quieter than other people. People who didn't talk much. People who stood in places for a long time without moving.

The man at the bus stop who was there every morning when she walked to school, wearing a brown suit with wide lapels and a hat with a feather in it. He never got on a bus. He just stood there looking down the road like he was waiting for one that never came.

The little girl on the swings at Calloway Park who pumped her legs but never went very high, who wore a dress with a Peter Pan collar even when it was a hundred degrees out, who never got off and never spoke and never looked at anyone.

The woman in the blue housedress in her kitchen.

Before her mother said *there's nobody there*, Sunny just thought they were shy. Some people were shy. Abuela was shy sometimes — she'd go quiet for whole days, sitting in her chair by the window, just watching the street. Sunny figured these quiet people were like that. Just watching. Just being.

But after that Saturday morning, something shifted. Not in what she saw — that stayed the same. Something shifted in her understanding of what she was seeing.

She started paying attention differently.

---

At night was worse, but she didn't know to call it worse yet.

Her bedroom was small — walls painted lavender by her mother before she was born, a bookshelf crammed full, a desk she never used for homework because the bed was better, and glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling that Abuela had helped her stick up there. The stars were important. They meant it was never fully dark. Even when the lights went off and the hallway light went off and the world outside the window went navy blue and quiet — the stars still gave off their greenish glow, fading slow through the night.

She needed them because of who visited.

Not every night. Maybe three or four times a week. Someone would be in her room when she woke up. Or someone would already be there when she went to sleep, and she'd just... share the space with them.

There was an old man who sat in her desk chair sometimes. Just sat there with his hands on his knees, looking at the floor. He wore suspenders and his skin was papery and thin-looking. He never spoke. He was the kind of presence you'd forget about if you weren't looking directly at him — like furniture. Like he'd been sitting in chairs in empty rooms for so long that he'd become one.

There was a teenager — a girl, maybe sixteen or seventeen — who stood by the closet door. She had her arms crossed and she looked angry about something, but not at Sunny. More like angry at the world. At the situation. At being in a child's bedroom when she clearly didn't want to be. She came and went. Sometimes weeks between appearances.

And once, only once, there was something that wasn't a person at all. A shape in the corner where the wall met the ceiling. Darker than the dark. Like someone had cut a hole in the room itself and behind it was nothing — not black, not empty, just *nothing*. It was there for maybe four seconds, and during those four seconds, Sunny's glow-in-the-dark stars stopped glowing. Every single one. Like they'd been swallowed.

Then it was gone and the stars came back and Sunny pulled her blanket over her head and breathed hot breath into the cotton until she fell asleep.

She didn't tell anyone about that one.

---

The thing that made Sunny different — and she wouldn't understand this for years — was not that she could see them. Other Sensitives could see them. There were maybe a hundred people in the country with some form of Thanatological perception.

What made Sunny different was that she could *feel* them before she saw them.

At eight years old, she didn't have words for it. She just knew that some rooms felt heavy and some rooms felt light, and that the heavy feeling came in different flavors. Sometimes a room was heavy like a wool blanket — warm and sad and pressing. Sometimes a room was heavy like a headache — sharp and wrong and wanting you gone. Sometimes a room was heavy like nothing she could describe — like the air had been taken out and replaced with something that wasn't air.

These were frequencies. She was feeling the vibrational signatures of post-death consciousness, each type resonating at a distinct harmonic. But she was eight. She didn't know about frequencies. She just knew that some places made her tummy feel funny and some didn't.

The grocery store on Third Street: warm-heavy, sad. Something in the bread aisle.
The library basement: sharp-heavy, bad. She didn't go down there.
Her abuela's bedroom: light. Clear. Clean. Like walking into open air.
School, mostly: neutral. Except the hallway by the gym at 2:15 every day when the tall woman walked her unchanging path.
Home: mixed. The kitchen was warm-heavy. Her bedroom was mostly light except when visitors came.

She mapped this in her body without knowing she was mapping anything. Her body was her first instrument — before the notebook, before the categories, before the language. Her body told her what was there and what it was. She trusted it absolutely because she had no reason not to. She was eight.

---

The disconnect was the strangest part. The disconnect between what she carried and what she did.

On a Tuesday in November of 1993, she had a conversation with a dead person. An old woman on the bench outside the laundromat, who turned to her and said, "Oh! Oh, you can see me?" and Sunny said "Yes, ma'am" and the woman started crying — crying without tears, crying with her whole transparent face crumpling — and said "Tell my husband I'm sorry about the garden. Tell Raymond I'm sorry I let the garden go. He'll know what it means."

Sunny didn't know anyone named Raymond. Didn't know what to do with this information. Stood there while a dead woman sobbed on a bench outside a laundromat on Kettering Avenue.

Then she walked home and ate a peanut butter sandwich and watched *Animaniacs* and colored in her coloring book and argued with her mother about taking a bath and fell asleep with her glasses on, which she did at least twice a week, and abuela came in to take them off and fold them on the nightstand.

That was the disconnect. That was what would define her childhood — the lurching, impossible gap between *a dead woman asking her to deliver a message she had no way to deliver* and *eating a peanut butter sandwich twenty minutes later like everything was normal*. Because everything WAS normal. To her. This was just how the world worked. You saw dead people and then you ate snacks and watched cartoons. What else were you supposed to do?

She was eight years old. She didn't know the word "compartmentalize." But she was already doing it better than most adults ever learn to.

---

Abuela knew.

Sunny didn't figure this out until later — not until she was eleven and things clicked — but Abuela Rosa knew from the beginning. Maybe before the beginning. Maybe from the moment Sunny was born and her eyes tracked things in the delivery room that no one else could see.

It showed in small ways. The way Abuela never flinched when Sunny talked about the people nobody else could see. The way she'd nod, just slightly, like she was filing the information away. The way she kept certain books on her shelf — old books in Spanish with cracked spines, books about *los muertos* and *el otro lado* and *los que ven*. The way she burned sage in the house without anyone asking her to and said it was for cooking.

It showed in the glow-in-the-dark stars. It was Abuela who'd suggested them. Abuela who'd climbed up on Sunny's desk chair and pressed each one carefully to the ceiling while Sunny directed from the bed: "More over there! A cluster! Like a real constellation!"

"Why do we need the stars, Abuela?"

"So you always have a little light, *mija*. Even in the dark."

She didn't say: *so you can see who's in your room*. She didn't say: *so the dark things that gather in corners have nowhere to hide*. She didn't say: *because I remember what it was like at your age and I swore I'd protect you from the worst of it*.

She said: "A little light is always good."

And Sunny accepted that because she was eight, and abuela's answers always made sense even when they didn't fully explain things.

---

The last memory from that first year — the one that would stay with her forever, the one she'd write about later in purple gel pen in her Lisa Frank notebook with a whole page dedicated to just this moment:

December 1993. Late. Snow outside. The house creaking with cold. Everyone asleep except Sunny, who was awake because there was a man standing at the foot of her bed.

Not the old chair-sitter. Not the angry teenager. Someone new.

He was young — maybe her mom's age. He wore a uniform. Military, she'd realize later. He stood at attention, spine straight, hands at his sides, and he was looking at her with an expression she'd spend years trying to name. Not sad. Not angry. Not confused.

*Grateful.*

He looked at her like she was a drink of water after a long walk. Like she was the first human face he'd seen in decades. Like seeing her — being seen BY her — was the best thing that had happened to him in all his long, lonely years of being dead.

He opened his mouth. She thought he was going to speak.

Instead he just... smiled. Closed his mouth. Smiled. Nodded once, like he was confirming something to himself. Then he faded — not disappeared, *faded* — like a TV turning off in slow motion. The image dimming, going gray, going transparent, going gone.

Sunny lay in her bed with her glow-in-the-dark stars above her and the snow falling outside and the warmth of three blankets piled on top of her, and she felt something she couldn't name. Not fear. Not sadness. Something more like... importance. Like she'd just done something that mattered, even though all she'd done was lie there.

She was eight years old. She pulled the covers up to her chin. She whispered, "You're welcome," to the empty room.

She fell asleep.

---

# PART 2: AGE 9-10 (1994-1995) — "THEY WON'T STOP COMING"

---

The thing about being a nine-year-old with a secret is that nine-year-olds are not good at secrets.

Sunny tried. She really tried. After the Saturday morning when her mother said *there's nobody there*, she understood — dimly, in the way a child understands consequences before understanding reasons — that this was something she should keep quiet about. That talking about the people others couldn't see made her mother's face do something complicated and worried. That it made adults look at each other over her head in that way adults look at each other when they're deciding whether to be concerned.

So she tried to keep it quiet. But she was nine, and she was curious by nature, and the dead kept finding her, and sometimes the information they gave her leaked out before she could catch it.

The school incident happened on a Wednesday in March of 1994.

---

Mrs. Patterson's third grade class. Reading time. Everyone sprawled on the carpet with their books from the class library, that particular twenty-minutes-after-lunch drowsiness settling over the room like a fog. Sunny had a book about dolphins in her lap but she wasn't reading it because the man by the window was distracting her.

He'd been there all year. Every day. Standing by the windows that faced the parking lot, looking out. He wore a shirt with a collar and his hair was parted neatly and his skin was a deep brown and he had kind eyes — she could tell even though he never looked at her. He was a Loop. She knew this by now (though she didn't have that word yet). Same place. Same pose. Same time. Every single day. Like a movie playing on repeat.

But today was different because today Trevor Mathis walked through him.

Trevor, who was going to the pencil sharpener, walked directly through the man by the window. And the man — for the first time all year — *flickered*. Stuttered. Like a VHS tape when you bump the player. His form went jagged and fuzzy and then smoothed back out.

"Whoa," Sunny said. Out loud.

Trevor looked at her. "What?"

She should have said nothing. She should have said "nothing" or "I thought I saw a bug" or any one of a hundred kid-lies that would have ended it.

Instead she said: "You walked through Mr. Hendricks."

"Who?"

She pointed at the window. At the man standing right there, so clearly, so obviously there that pointing at him felt redundant. "Mr. Hendricks. He's standing right there. He's been there all year."

Trevor looked at the window. Looked at the empty space. Looked at Sunny. His face did the thing she was learning to recognize — the *she's weird* recalculation that crossed someone's features when Sunny said something that didn't match the world they lived in.

"There's nobody there, weirdo."

"Yes there IS. He's right — he wears the brown shirt and he — "

"Sunny." Mrs. Patterson. Gentle but firm. "Who are you talking about, sweetheart?"

And the room was looking at her now. Twenty-three third-graders with their books in their laps, all turned toward her, and she could feel every single one of their eyes and she could also feel the man by the window, steady as always, and the disconnect between those two realities — the class seeing nothing, her seeing everything — crushed down on her chest like a hand.

"Nobody," she said. "Sorry. I was making something up."

She went back to her dolphin book. She didn't speak for the rest of the day.

But Mrs. Patterson called her mother that evening, and Carmen sat on the edge of Sunny's bed that night and stroked her hair and said, "Baby, you know you can tell me anything, right?" and Sunny said "I know, Mami" and did not tell her anything.

---

A ghost followed her home in the spring of 1994 and it was the first time she understood that she wasn't safe.

Not a dangerous ghost. A *desperate* one. A woman, middle-aged, with short hair and a face that looked like it had been crying for a very long time — not red-eyed and puffy the way living people look when they cry, but *worn*. Like crying was just her permanent state. Like grief had settled into her features the way wrinkles settle.

Sunny first saw her at school. In the hallway outside the cafeteria, standing against the wall, watching the kids stream past. Watching them the way you watch traffic from a sidewalk — just seeing them move without being part of it.

Sunny made the mistake of looking directly at her.

The woman's head snapped toward Sunny. Her eyes went wide. And she said — Sunny heard it not with her ears but somewhere deeper, somewhere in her chest — "You can SEE me?"

Sunny nodded before she could stop herself.

And that was it. That was all it took. One nod. One acknowledgment. And the woman was with her. In the hallway. In the classroom. At the water fountain. In the bathroom. On the walk home — floating is the wrong word, but she wasn't walking either. She was just *there*, beside Sunny, matching her pace, talking and talking and talking.

"My daughter. I need to tell my daughter. She's twenty-three now, she was twelve when I — when I left. Tell her I didn't choose it. It was the car. The car — the other car came from nowhere and I — I didn't choose to leave her. She thinks I left on purpose. She thinks I wasn't paying attention. But the other car — "

Sunny walked home with her backpack bouncing and her Walkman headphones around her neck and a dead woman's desperate monologue pouring into her consciousness like water filling a cup. She walked past the bodega. Past the laundromat. Past the vacant lot where the older kids smoked. She walked into her house and kicked off her shoes and the woman was STILL THERE, in the living room, still talking.

"— and the insurance, tell her the insurance money is in the account, the one at First National, the number is — oh God, I can't remember the number. I could always remember it. Why can't I — "

"I can't help you," Sunny said. She was nine years old, standing in her living room with her backpack still on, talking to empty air. "I don't know your daughter. I don't know where she is. I can't tell her."

The woman stopped talking. Looked at Sunny with an expression of such raw, bottomless need that Sunny felt it in her stomach like hunger pangs.

"But you can SEE me."

"I know. But I'm just a kid. I don't know how to find your daughter. I'm sorry."

The woman stayed for three days. Three days of her standing in the corner of whatever room Sunny was in. Standing in the bathroom while Sunny brushed her teeth. Standing at the foot of her bed while she tried to sleep. Always with that same expression: *please. Please. I just need someone to hear me.*

On the third night, Sunny — who had not slept well, who had bags under her eyes that her mother noticed, who had gotten a D on a spelling test for the first time in her life — sat up in bed at 2 AM and said, with a firmness she didn't know she was capable of:

"You have to go now."

The woman looked at her.

"I hear you. I know you love your daughter. I know it wasn't your fault. But you have to GO. I can't help you and you can't stay here. This is MY room."

Silence. The glow-in-the-dark stars above. The clock ticking on the nightstand.

"Please go," Sunny whispered. And then, because she didn't know what else to say: "It's okay. You can go."

Something shifted. The woman's face — that grief-worn, desperate face — softened. Not happy. But... released. Like someone had unclenched a fist that had been tight for years.

She faded. The same way the soldier had faded. Slow. Dim. Transparent. Gone.

And Sunny learned two things at once: she could tell them to leave, and some of them needed permission.

---

The grocery store incident happened in June of 1994 and it was the one that got her sent to a therapist.

Foodtown on Kettering Avenue. Saturday afternoon. Carmen pushing the cart, Sunny walking beside her, trailing her fingers along the shelves the way kids do — touching every box of crackers, every can of soup, reading labels because she read *everything*.

The woman appeared at the end of the cereal aisle. Old. Very old. Tiny, hunched, in a cardigan and a floral dress that went to her ankles. She was transparent in the way the Stuck Ones sometimes were — you could see the Rice Krispies display right through her torso. She was looking at Carmen's cart with such intense longing that it took Sunny a moment to realize she wasn't looking at the cart — she was looking at the picture of a little girl on Carmen's keychain. Sunny's school photo from last year.

No. She wasn't looking at the keychain. She was looking at the woman BEHIND the cart.

She was looking at Mrs. Delgado from church.

Mrs. Delgado was in the next aisle over, visible through the gap in the shelves, holding a box of tea and reading the label. The old ghost woman was looking at her with that look Sunny was learning to recognize — the unbearable love of the dead for the living. The love that can't touch. Can't reach. Can only watch.

"That's her granddaughter," Sunny said, before she could stop herself.

Carmen looked down. "What, baby?"

But Sunny wasn't talking to her mother. She was talking to the ghost, who had turned — who was looking at Sunny now with that desperate flash of recognition.

"That's your granddaughter. Mrs. Delgado."

The ghost nodded. Frantic. Nodding and pointing and her mouth moving but no words coming — not all of them could speak, Sunny was learning that too.

And then Mrs. Delgado came around the corner into their aisle and said "Carmen! Hi, how are you?" and while the two women were chatting about church and the heat and someone's surgery, Sunny tugged on Mrs. Delgado's sleeve.

"Your grandma wants you to know she watches you make the tea every morning," Sunny said. "She says you always did it right. The way she taught you."

Mrs. Delgado went white.

Carmen went white.

The store seemed to go very quiet.

"What did you say?" Mrs. Delgado whispered.

Sunny looked at the ghost — who was beaming now, beaming with that horrible bittersweet joy of the dead who've been heard — and said, "She's right there. Behind the cereal. She's smiling."

Mrs. Delgado dropped her box of tea. Her hand went to her mouth. Her eyes filled with tears. "My grandmother has been dead for twelve years," she said to Carmen, and her voice cracked on *twelve*.

They left the grocery store without finishing their shopping. Carmen drove home with both hands tight on the wheel and her jaw set in that way that meant she was scared but trying not to show it. Sunny sat in the back seat and felt terrible, though she wasn't entirely sure what she'd done wrong.

At home, Carmen called her sister. Talked in low rapid Spanish with the bedroom door closed. Then she called someone else — Sunny heard her say "nine years old" and "she keeps saying she sees things" and "I don't know what to do."

Two weeks later, Sunny was sitting in Dr. Reeves' office.

---

Dr. Reeves was nice. That was the awful part — she was genuinely nice. Mid-thirties, Black, with short natural hair and big hoop earrings and a office that had a couch with too many pillows and a shelf of fidget toys Sunny was definitely too old for but played with anyway because what else do you do with your hands when someone is asking you careful questions about what you see.

"Your mom tells me you sometimes see people that aren't there," Dr. Reeves said.

Sunny pushed her glasses up her nose. Squeezed the stress ball. It was shaped like a star.

"They ARE there," Sunny said. "Other people just can't see them."

"Can you tell me what they look like?"

So Sunny told her. She told her about the lady in the kitchen and the man at the bus stop and the girl on the swings and the woman who followed her home. She told her calmly, factually, the way you'd describe what you ate for breakfast. Because to her, it WAS that ordinary.

Dr. Reeves wrote things down. Nodded. Didn't look scared or disgusted or like she thought Sunny was crazy. Just... interested. Clinical. She asked questions like: "Do they ever tell you to do things?" (No.) "Do they scare you?" (Sometimes, but mostly no.) "Do you hear voices when nobody is there?" (Only when one of them is talking to me, and it's not a VOICE really, it's more like... knowing what they're saying.)

She saw Dr. Reeves every Thursday for three months. It was fine. Dr. Reeves never said she was crazy. She also never said Sunny was telling the truth. She existed in this careful middle space — *I believe this is your experience* — that was worse, somehow, than outright disbelief. Because it meant: I think this is happening in your head and I'm being gentle about it.

Sunny learned, at nine years old, what "clinically validated dismissal" feels like. She learned that being believed isn't the same as being understood. She learned to answer Dr. Reeves' questions in a way that wouldn't increase concern: "No, they don't bother me anymore." "No, I don't see them as often." "I think maybe I was making it up before." "I have a big imagination."

She learned to lie.

Dr. Reeves signed off on her after three months. Told Carmen that Sunny had an active imagination and showed no signs of psychosis or trauma and was emotionally well-adjusted and maybe she'd grow out of it.

Sunny came home from her last session and sat on the kitchen floor eating Hot Cheetos with lime and thought: *I'm never telling anyone again. Not until I understand it myself. Not until I can prove it.*

The kitchen lady was still there by the stove, still stirring nothing. Sunny could feel her — a low, sad, steady hum. Like a washing machine in another room.

"I get it now," Sunny said to her, licking red dust off her fingers. "You can't hear me. That's okay. I'm still here."

---

The sleepover was in October of 1994. Maya Rodriguez's birthday. Six girls in Maya's basement with sleeping bags and pizza and rented movies (*Now and Then*, which they'd already seen twice, and *The Little Rascals*) and that specific sleepover energy where everything is hilarious and nobody actually sleeps.

Sunny loved sleepovers. She loved the closeness of other girls, the warmth of being in a pile on the floor, the whispered conversations after lights-out. She loved Maya's house in particular — it usually felt clean, frequency-wise. Light. Clear.

But that night, something was wrong.

She felt it the moment she came down the basement stairs. A *buzz*. Not the warm pulse of a Stuck One or the flat hum of a Loop. Something sharper. Itchy. Like static on a TV screen but translated into a feeling in her skin. It was coming from the corner by the water heater. Something back there, in the dark space where the ceiling dropped low and the concrete went unfinished.

Sunny put her sleeping bag on the opposite side of the room from that corner.

For hours, everything was fine. They ate pizza (Sunny: three slices, which made her feel grounded and good). They watched movies. They did nails. They played M.A.S.H. They whispered about boys. Normal. Good. Nine-year-old girl stuff.

Then Maya's mom turned off the light upstairs and said "Girls, it's midnight, go to sleep." And the lights went off. And the basement went dark. And the *buzz* in the corner got louder.

Not louder like sound. Louder like *presence*. Like whatever was back there had been waiting for the dark. Like the dark was its invitation.

Sunny lay in her sleeping bag with her eyes open and her glasses on and stared at the corner by the water heater. And slowly — slowly enough that she thought she might be imagining it — the dark in that corner got *darker*. Not shadows. Something else. Something that was actively absorbing the little bit of streetlight coming through the basement window.

The temperature dropped. She could see her breath.

Beside her, Maya shifted and muttered in her sleep. Two sleeping bags over, Jessica was snoring softly. Everyone else was out. Only Sunny was awake because only Sunny could feel the frequency of the thing in the corner, and it was getting CLOSER.

Not moving. Not walking. Just... extending. Like the dark itself was reaching across the floor.

Sunny's heart hammered. Her palms went slick. She could feel the wrongness of it — not angry, not sad, not confused. Something else entirely. Something that had no human feeling in it at all. Just cold attention. Just awareness.

She did the only thing she could think to do. She got up. Quietly. Rolled out of her sleeping bag in her flannel pajamas with the moons and stars. Picked up her pillow and her backpack. Walked to the stairs in her socks, step by careful step, not looking back. Not giving it her attention.

She went upstairs. Found Maya's mom asleep on the couch. Shook her gently.

"Mrs. Rodriguez? I don't feel good. Can you call my mom?"

Carmen came at 2:17 AM, in her bathrobe with her coat thrown over it, sleep still in her eyes. She didn't ask questions until they were in the car.

"What happened, Sunny?"

"My stomach hurt."

Carmen looked at her in the rearview mirror. Sunny looked out the window. Neither of them said anything else.

At home, Sunny went straight to her room. The glow-in-the-dark stars were there. The old man in the chair wasn't (good night for it). She climbed into bed fully dressed, glasses still on, and pulled every blanket up to her chin and breathed.

She was nine years old and she had just encountered something that wasn't a dead person. She didn't know what it was. She didn't have a category for it. She just knew: *that was different. That was worse. That was the kind of thing you run from.*

She wouldn't encounter another one like it until she was twelve. By then she'd have a name for them. By then she'd have rules.

But at nine, lying in her bed at 2:30 in the morning, all she had was the absence of language for her fear and the green glow of stick-on stars above her and the sound of her mother's footsteps checking on her one more time before going back to bed.

---

The salvation came through the television.

It was a Wednesday night in November of 1994. Sunny couldn't sleep — the teenager by the closet was having one of her angry nights, all that silent fury radiating off her like heat from asphalt. So Sunny crept downstairs, careful not to wake Abuela, and turned on the TV in the living room with the volume low.

Channel surfing. Infomercials. A rerun of *M*A*S*H*. The news. More infomercials. And then — on some syndicated channel, glowing blue in the dark living room — Robert Stack's voice.

*"Tonight on Unsolved Mysteries: a family in Ohio claims their home is haunted by the spirit of a Civil War soldier..."*

Sunny sat forward on the couch. Pulled a throw pillow into her lap and squeezed it.

The segment showed a family. Normal people. A mom, a dad, two kids. They described seeing a figure in their hallway. Hearing footsteps. Feeling cold spots. The kids said they'd seen a man in uniform watching them from the stairs.

Sunny's hands went cold. The hairs on her arms stood up. Not because she was scared — because she was being *recognized*. Not directly, not personally. But the experience on screen was *hers*. Those kids were describing what she lived every single day.

Robert Stack said the word "haunting." He said "paranormal investigators." He said "residual energy." He said "intelligent spirit."

Words. He was giving her WORDS.

She stayed up until 1 AM watching the rest of the episode. Then she found another rerun of *Sightings* on a different channel — a show she'd never seen before, hosted by a man who talked about the paranormal like it was *news*. Like it was something to be investigated. Documented. Understood.

On *Sightings*, they used words like "cold spot" and "EVP" and "ectoplasm" and "poltergeist." They showed people with equipment — thermometers, cameras, audio recorders — trying to *measure* what Sunny just SAW. Trying to document what she experienced every day.

She sat in the dark living room with the TV casting blue light on her round face and her thick glasses and she felt something crack open in her chest. Not pain. Relief. The relief of a person drowning who suddenly remembers they can swim.

*There are words for what I see. There are people who study this. There are WORDS.*

She didn't know it yet, but she'd just found her education. Not school — school would never teach her what she needed. *Television.* The golden age of paranormal TV was beginning, and it would be her textbook, her dictionary, her companionship for the next five years.

---

She found *Coast to Coast AM with Art Bell* the same month, though she wouldn't become a devoted listener until the following year.

Late night. Past midnight. The house silent. Sunny's clock radio — a birthday gift from Abuela, pink and plasticky and perfect — was tuned to AM because she liked how the stations faded in and out at night, voices from far-away cities drifting through like ghosts of their own kind.

She found Art Bell at 1:23 AM on a Thursday. A caller was describing shadow people in their home. Seeing figures made of darkness in doorways, in corners, in the periphery of vision.

"And does anyone else in the family see them?" Art Bell asked, his voice calm and low and curious. Not mocking. Not clinical. *Curious.*

"Just me," the caller said. "Everyone thinks I'm nuts."

Sunny pressed the radio closer to her ear. Her heart was beating fast. She could feel tears forming behind her glasses and she couldn't have explained why — only that this stranger on the radio, this adult who was probably hundreds of miles away, had just said something Sunny had never heard another person say: *just me. Everyone thinks I'm nuts.*

Art Bell said: "Well, you're not the only one. We get calls like this every week. You are far from alone in this experience."

She cried. Silently, face pressed into her pillow, radio held against her head. She was nine years old and a man on the radio had just told a stranger they weren't alone and Sunny received it like he was talking to her.

She started listening every night she could stay awake. Midnight to 2 AM, sometimes later. Under her covers with the radio, the volume barely a whisper, Art Bell's desert-calm voice and the parade of callers describing things that sounded like her life. Gradually, the isolation — that vast, freezing loneliness of being the only person in her world who could see what she saw — began to thaw. Not disappear. Just... thaw.

---

By 1995, she was ten years old and she was beginning to understand rules.

Not rules anyone told her. Rules she figured out through observation. Through watching. Through being wrong and learning from it. Through keeping track — not in a notebook yet, not officially, but in her head. The way a scientist gathers data before writing a paper. The way Velma gathered clues before revealing the answer.

**Rule: Some of them can see her and some can't.** The Loops — the recordings, the repeaters — can't. They don't know she exists. The others — the aware ones — can. And they react. Some are happy. Some are desperate. Some are angry.

**Rule: She can say no.** After the woman who followed her home, she tested it. Carefully. A man in the park who started toward her with that desperate look — she held up her hand and said "Not now" and he stopped. He actually stopped. She had... authority, somehow. Not always. Not with all of them. But with most.

**Rule: It's worse at night.** Not that there are more of them — there might not be. But at night her sensitivity is higher. She feels them more. Hears them more clearly. They seem more solid. Something about darkness, or about sleep, or about the brain being quiet — it turns up her volume.

**Rule: Full stomach = quieter.** She discovered this by accident. On days when she ate a big lunch — when she was full and warm and grounded in her body — they were dimmer. Quieter. Like the physical fullness took up space that the dead usually occupied. She started eating more. Not from anxiety — from strategy. Grounding in the body works.

**Rule: Music helps.** Her Walkman became essential. Sunny clipped it to her waistband every morning, headphones around her neck like a talisman. When things got too much — too many of them, too loud, too close — she pressed play. Aaliyah's voice. TLC. Selena. The music created a frequency buffer. Like white noise for ghosts. It didn't make them disappear but it made them *recede*. Pushed them to the edges of her perception.

**Rule: Some places are worse than others.** The library basement. The old house on Meridian Street. The third-floor hallway at school. The parking lot behind the hospital. She mapped the bad places in her mind and avoided them when she could. When she couldn't — she moved through fast, eyes forward, music on, frequency closed as tight as she could close it.

**Rule: Telling people never goes well.** The school incident. The grocery store. Dr. Reeves. Every time she told someone, the result was the same: either they thought she was lying, or they believed her and were scared, or they believed her and wanted her to perform like a trick. None of these were good. So she stopped telling.

She was ten years old and she was carrying the weight of two worlds alone and she was getting stronger and she was getting tired and she was still, somehow, a kid who liked Dunkaroos and *Fresh Prince* reruns and painting her nails with her best friend.

# PART 3: AGE 11 (1996) — "THE TALK SHOW"

---

She was home sick. That's how it happened — the thing that changed everything. A sore throat and a low fever that kept her home from school on a Thursday in January of 1996. She was eleven years old, wrapped in the afghan from the couch, and she'd already watched *The Price is Right* and *Maury* (paternity tests — boring) and three episodes of *Fresh Prince* reruns.

Mom was at work. Abuela was in the kitchen making *caldo de pollo* because that was what you made for sick children, and the smell of it — chicken and cilantro and lime — drifted through the house like a warm hand on the forehead.

Sunny was channel surfing with the remote in one hand and a cup of *manzanilla* tea in the other, punching through the channels with the bored restlessness of a kid who's been on the couch too long. Talk shows, soaps, commercials, *Judge Judy*, commercials, cartoons, more commercials —

And then she stopped.

On screen: a studio audience. Bright lights. A woman host — she thought it was Sally Jesse Raphael but she wasn't sure, she was hitting the channel button and everything blurred together — and on the stage, sitting on those talk-show couches that always looked uncomfortable: children. Three of them. A boy maybe nine years old. A girl around twelve. Another boy, older, maybe fourteen. And their parents beside them, looking the way parents look when they don't know what to do.

The chyron at the bottom of the screen said: **"MY CHILD SEES GHOSTS — AM I GOING CRAZY?"**

Sunny's finger froze over the channel button. The tea in her other hand went very still.

The nine-year-old boy was talking. His voice was small and shaky and his mother had her arm around him and he was saying: "They're in my room every night. I tell them to go away but they won't. Nobody believes me."

Sunny set the tea down. She didn't look away from the screen.

The girl — the twelve-year-old, with dark hair and scared eyes — said: "I told my teacher and she called my mom and they put me in therapy. They think something's wrong with me. But I SEE them. They're REAL."

Sunny's chest tightened. She felt her breath go shallow. She felt her eyes burn behind her glasses in a way that meant tears were coming whether she wanted them or not.

A woman in the audience asked: "Have you considered that your children might just have vivid imaginations?" The audience murmured agreement.

The host turned to someone else on stage — a man Sunny hadn't noticed. Older. Gentle face. Sitting apart from the families with the composed posture of someone who'd been on TV before. A psychic. A medium. Some kind of expert. The chyron said his name but Sunny didn't register it — she was too focused on what was about to happen.

The host said: "What would you say to these children?"

The man leaned forward. He looked at the nine-year-old boy. Not the camera, not the audience — the BOY. And he said:

"Sweetheart, listen to me. This isn't a punishment. You didn't do anything wrong. This is a *gift*. You see things other people can't see because you're BUILT different. You're more sensitive. You're more aware. And I know it's scary. I know it feels like a curse. I KNOW. Because I was like you when I was your age. And nobody believed me either."

The boy started crying. So did his mother. So did the twelve-year-old girl. So did Sunny Martinez, alone on her abuela's couch in her pajamas with a fever of 99.8 and chicken soup cooking in the kitchen.

"You are not alone," the man said. "There are hundreds of children just like you. You are not crazy. You are not broken. You are not being punished. You see more than other people. That's all. And it gets better. I promise you — it gets *better*."

---

Sunny cried so hard she couldn't breathe.

Not gentle crying. Not movie-crying where a single tear rolls picturesquely down one cheek. This was the ugly kind. The gasping, snot-running, can't-see-through-your-glasses kind. The kind that comes when something inside you — something you've been holding in a clenched fist for three YEARS — finally opens.

Three years. Three years of thinking she was broken. Three years of praying every night for it to stop. Three years of *why me why me why me*. Three years of carrying the impossible weight of the dead while being nine, ten, eleven years old and understanding nothing about why this was happening to her.

And a man on television just said: *gift, not curse*.

She sobbed into the afghan. She pulled it over her head and cried into the darkness of it, into the smell of fabric softener and old couch and home. She cried until her chest hurt and her eyes burned and her throat — already sore — felt raw and stripped.

Abuela appeared. Sunny hadn't heard her come in from the kitchen, but suddenly she was there, on the couch beside her, her small warm body settling into the cushions with the ease of a woman who has spent decades learning how to be present without demanding explanation.

She didn't ask what was wrong. She didn't look at the TV and say *why are you watching this*. She didn't say *it's okay* or *calm down* or any of the things adults say to crying children that never actually help.

She just put her hand on Sunny's knee. Through the afghan. A firm, warm pressure. *I'm here. I know. I know why you're crying. You don't have to explain.*

They sat like that for a long time. The show went to commercial. An ad for dish soap. An ad for a psychic hotline (Miss Cleo's voice: "Call me NOW"). An ad for Capri Sun.

Sunny pulled the afghan down. Her face was swollen and wet. Her glasses were smeared. She looked at Abuela.

Abuela looked back. Her eyes — dark brown, webbed with wrinkles, infinitely kind — held something that Sunny had been too young to read before. But now, at eleven, with the psychic man's words ringing in her ears, she saw it:

*Recognition.*

Abuela knew. Had always known. Because Abuela had been her.

---

The show came back from commercial. Sunny grabbed the remote. Hit RECORD on the VCR — the family VCR, the one still loaded with last night's tape of *ER* that her mother hadn't watched yet. She'd record over it. Sorry, Mom. This was more important.

The red REC light blinked on. The psychic man was still talking. He was telling the families things — practical things. Things nobody had ever told Sunny:

"You can set boundaries. You can tell them when you're available and when you're not."

(She'd figured that out on her own. But hearing someone else say it — an ADULT, on TV — )

"Not all spirits are bad. Most just want to be seen. Most are just lost."

(She knew this. She KNEW. But nobody had ever confirmed it.)

"Your children are not in danger from most of what they see. The dangerous ones are rare. And your children already know which ones feel wrong — trust their instincts."

(Trust their instincts. Trust their instincts. TRUST THEIR INSTINCTS.)

"This ability often runs in families. One of you may have had it too, when you were young. It often fades in adulthood."

Abuela's hand tightened on Sunny's knee. Just slightly. Just enough.

Sunny looked at her grandmother. Abuela looked at the TV. Her face gave nothing away except that tiny tightening of her hand. But it was enough. It was EVERYTHING.

---

She watched that VHS tape thirty-seven times over the next year.

She counted. She kept a tally mark in the corner of a sheet of notebook paper, and every time she watched the segment — usually late at night, after everyone was asleep, with the volume low and the blue TV glow making the living room a private sanctuary — she added another mark.

Thirty-seven times. The tracking on the tape started getting fuzzy from rewinding the same spot over and over. The psychic man's voice got slightly warped, slightly stretched, but she could still hear every word. She had them memorized. Could recite them in her sleep.

*"This isn't a punishment. This is a gift."*

Every time she watched it, she believed it a little more.

---

The shift happened over weeks, not overnight. But it was seismic. Everything she thought about herself, about her ability, about her life — it ROTATED. Like a picture turned right-side-up after years of hanging crooked.

Before the talk show:
- *Why me?*
- *What did I do wrong?*
- *Please make it stop.*
- *I'm broken.*
- *I'm alone.*
- *This is a punishment.*

After the talk show:
- *Okay. If it's a gift — what can I do with it?*
- *If others have this too — what have THEY figured out?*
- *If it runs in families — does Abuela know things she hasn't told me?*
- *If I can set boundaries — what else can I control?*
- *If the dangerous ones are rare — then most of what I see is just... sad people who need help?*
- *If it gets better — HOW? What do I need to learn?*

She went from victim to *researcher* in a matter of weeks. The questions changed. The posture changed. The entire orientation of her soul shifted from *enduring* to *investigating*.

---

The Lisa Frank notebook came next.

She bought it at Walgreens with her own money — three dollars and forty-nine cents from her allowance. It had dolphins on the front, leaping over a rainbow, against a background of purple and pink that shifted in the holographic light. It was ridiculous and beautiful and perfect.

She bought a pack of gel pens too. Purple. Pink. Blue. Silver. Green.

She sat on her bed that evening with the notebook open on her knees, purple pen in hand, and wrote at the top of the first page in careful, rounded letters:

**FIELD GUIDE**
*by Soleil Martinez, age 11*
*PRIVATE — DO NOT READ*

Not a diary. She was very clear about this in her mind. This was not a diary. Diaries were for feelings and crushes and *dear diary, today was boring*. This was a FIELD GUIDE. A scientific document. A record of observations, hypotheses, and rules.

She started writing. And she didn't stop. Pages and pages in purple and pink gel pen, with drawings in the margins — diagrams of where she'd seen things, stick-figure classifications of different types, timelines of encounters, temperature notes, location maps.

The categories came together. Things she'd been intuiting for three years suddenly organized themselves on paper:

*"Type 1: The ones that repeat. They don't see me. They're not people anymore — they're like recordings. They do the same thing at the same time in the same place. I can't talk to them. They can't hear me. They just play, over and over."*

*"Type 2: The real ones. They SEE me. They know I can see them. Some are nice. Some are desperate. Some are confused. I can talk to them. I can tell them to go away. Sometimes I can tell them it's okay to leave and they DO."*

*"Type 3: The mean ones. They don't want help. They're angry. The air feels BAD around them. Cold and electric and wrong. I stay away."*

*"Type 4: The loud ones. When things move and fall and break but nobody's there — it's usually not a ghost. It's usually a PERSON. A living person whose feelings are too big and they leak out as energy. I saw this happen with Maya."*

*"Type 5: The ??? ones. Not human. Never human. I only met one. I ran. I don't know what they are yet."*

Under each category: notes. Details. Sub-categories that would grow more complex over the years. But this was the beginning — the first draft of a classification system that no one had taught her, that no book had given her, that she'd built from scratch out of five years of observation and fear and courage.

At the bottom of the fifth page, in silver gel pen:

*"I'm not broken. I'm not crazy. I'm a scientist and these are my observations and one day I'm going to understand ALL of it."*

---

Abuela never had the conversation. Not directly. Not the way you'd expect a mentor to sit a student down and say *here's what I know*.

Instead, over the following months, small things happened:

A book appeared on the coffee table. Old, Spanish, leather-bound. *"Los Que Ven: Historias de Sensitivos en México."* Sunny picked it up. Read it in two days. It was accounts — first-person accounts of people in Mexico who could see the dead. Most were women. Most started seeing as children. Most said it faded in their twenties.

Sunny looked at Abuela across the dinner table. Abuela was eating her *sopa* and didn't look up. But the corner of her mouth turned up. Just barely.

A rosary appeared on Sunny's nightstand. Not a regular one — it had a different feel. Smoother. Older. The beads were black and warm and when Sunny held them, her room felt quieter. The frequency of her nighttime visitors dimmed to almost nothing. She kept it under her pillow.

Sage appeared in Sunny's room. Already burned, just the bundle left on her desk, casual, like it had always been there. Sunny figured out quickly that fresh-burned sage cleared a room for about six hours. She started burning it before bed without asking permission.

Abuela watched *Beyond Belief: Fact or Fiction* with her on Friday nights. When Jonathan Frakes said "This story... is TRUE," Sunny would call it before the reveal. "That one's real." "That one's fake." "Real." "Fake." "REAL." She got them right almost every time. Abuela would just nod. Smile. Pat her knee.

She was being mentored. She just wasn't being mentored in words. Abuela was teaching her the way Abuela's own grandmother had taught *her* — through presence, through objects, through quiet acknowledgment. Through the slow, steady message: *I know. You're safe. You'll figure it out. I'm here while you do.*

# PART 4: AGE 12 (1997) — "THE SCARY ONE"

---

The library basement changed everything.

Not the school library — the PUBLIC library. The big one downtown. Three stories of books and computers and study rooms and one basement level that was supposed to be for archives and storage but that had recently been opened for public use because the library was running out of space. They'd put some reference materials down there. Some microfiche machines. A few study carrels tucked between shelves of old periodicals.

Sunny loved the library. It was her place — her church, really, the one place where she could always find answers even if the answers took time. She went twice a week after school, walked the eight blocks with her Walkman on and her backpack heavy with returns.

She never went to the basement.

She'd known since the first time she entered the building, at age seven, that the basement was wrong. Not *had something in it* — WAS wrong. The way a lake is wet. Not a feature of the basement. The fundamental nature of it. The whole sublevel thrummed with a frequency she'd never felt anywhere else: sharp. Hostile. Cold. A sustained note of *get out* that pulsed up through the floor of the main level like a heartbeat that hated you.

She'd avoided it for years. Easy enough — there was nothing down there she needed. Everything good was on the first and second floors.

But in April of 1997, she needed a periodical from 1983 for a school project. And it was in the basement.

---

She stood at the top of the stairs for four full minutes.

Her body was telling her things. Stomach tight. Shoulders drawn up toward her ears. Hands cold — specifically her hands, as if the temperature differential was reaching up the stairwell and touching only her fingers. The frequency pulsed. Sharp. Sharp. *Sharp.*

This was not a Loop. Loops didn't project like this. Loops were self-contained, playing their little movie without awareness of their surroundings.

This was not a Stuck One. Stuck Ones were warm or neutral — even the confused ones, the desperate ones. They were human. They felt human.

This was an Angry One. The worst she'd ever felt.

She went down anyway. Because she was twelve and stubborn and because the project was due Monday and because part of her — the Velma part, the scientist part, the girl who NEEDED to understand — wanted to know. What was down there? What made it so bad? She'd been sensing it for five years. She wanted to see it with her own eyes.

She would regret this decision.

---

The basement was cold. Not library-air-conditioning cold. The kind of cold that has intent behind it. The kind of cold that exists to communicate something. *You are not welcome here.*

Fluorescent lights, the long tubular kind, buzzed overhead. Half of them were out. The ones that worked flickered in an irregular pattern — not electrical-problem flickering. Responsive flickering. Like they were reacting to her presence. Like something was testing them as she walked underneath.

Rows of metal shelving. Bound periodicals in brown and gray, their spines numbered and dull. Microfiche machines in the back corner, their screens dark. Study carrels built into the walls, each with a small lamp. Two were occupied — a college-age man with headphones on, and an older woman reading a newspaper. Neither looked up.

Sunny moved through the space with every nerve firing. She could feel her frequency radiating — her own signal, her own vibrational signature that she was only beginning to understand she had. She felt it pouring off her like body heat, and she felt something in the basement RESPONDING to it. Pushing back. Like two magnets held with the same pole facing.

She found the periodical section. Pulled the 1983 volume she needed. Tucked it under her arm.

Turned to leave.

And he was there.

---

Not a man, exactly. The shape of a man. The suggestion of one. Tall — taller than anyone she'd seen alive or dead. Standing at the end of the aisle, between her and the stairs, and the air around him was so cold she could see her breath crystallizing in front of her face.

His features were wrong. Not blurred — WRONG. Like a face assembled by something that had seen faces but didn't fully understand how they worked. The proportions were slightly off. The mouth was too wide. The eyes were too still.

And from him came a frequency unlike anything she'd ever felt.

Not the sharp buzz she'd sensed from upstairs. That was just the EDGES of him, the peripheral radiation. Being in his presence was different. It was a wall of hatred. Not the hot, human hatred of someone wronged — cold hatred. Architectural hatred. Hatred that had been building in this basement for so long it had calcified into something structural. Load-bearing hatred. The building needed it now. It was part of the foundation.

Sunny could not move.

Her feet were not physically frozen — she would realize this later. She could have walked. But her body refused. Every mammalian instinct she had fired at once: *don't move, don't breathe, don't be seen, if you're very still maybe the predator won't notice.*

But he already saw her. Those too-still eyes were fixed on her with an attention that felt like being pinned to a board.

The lights above her went out. Not all at once — one by one, *tink tink tink*, from the far end of the basement toward her, like dominoes falling.

The college man pulled off his headphones and looked up. The old woman lowered her newspaper. They couldn't see what Sunny saw. They just saw lights going out. Electrical problem. Annoying. The college man started packing his bag.

Sunny stood in the growing dark with a bound volume of newspapers pressed against her chest like a shield and a dead thing between her and the only exit and she was twelve years old and she was more afraid than she had ever been in her life.

The thing at the end of the aisle MOVED. Not walked. Moved. One moment at the end of the row, the next moment six feet closer, without the intervening motion. Like a film with frames cut out.

Something came out of Sunny then.

She didn't choose it. Didn't plan it. Didn't even know it was possible until it happened. Something — some force, some projection of her own frequency — PUSHED outward from her center. A pulse. A wave. A shout without sound. Every ounce of her being screaming *NO. GET BACK.*

The thing stopped.

The lights came back on. All at once. *Tink.* Full brightness. Like nothing had happened.

The thing at the end of the aisle was further away. Not gone — further. Pushed back. By her. By whatever she'd just done.

Its too-wide mouth did something that might have been a smile. Or a snarl. She couldn't tell.

Sunny ran.

She ran past it — or around it — or through the space where it suddenly wasn't — she ran up the stairs taking them two at a time, through the main floor, past the checkout desk, past the children's section with its cheerful posters, through the front doors and down the stone steps and she didn't stop running until she was three blocks away, bent over a parking meter, gasping, the bound periodical still clutched against her chest like it had cost her something profound.

She stood there on the sidewalk and breathed and shook and breathed and shook. Her glasses were fogged. Her heart was trying to escape through her throat. Her hands — both of them — were ice cold and trembling.

But somewhere underneath the terror, something else was happening. A realization. Tiny. Fierce.

*I pushed it back. It came at me and I pushed it BACK.*

She had a force. A frequency she could PROJECT. Not just receive — send. Not just see — affect.

She was twelve years old and she was terrified and she was powerful and she didn't know yet which of those things would matter more.

---

Maya's poltergeist started in September.

Not that anyone called it a poltergeist at first. At first it was just weird things happening around Maya Rodriguez — Sunny's best friend since first grade, skinny and loud and athletic, the opposite of Sunny in almost every physical way but her perfect match in energy and loyalty.

Maya's parents were getting divorced. This had been coming for a year — the fighting getting louder, the silences getting longer, Maya showing up at school with dark circles and short temper and none of her usual bouncy energy. Sunny held space for it the way she held space for everything: with snacks and hugs and sitting close and letting Maya talk or not talk, whatever she needed.

But then the locker started slamming.

Maya's locker. It would burst open in the middle of class, BANG, books and papers spilling into the hallway. Even when she'd closed it properly. Even when she'd locked it. Three times in one week.

Then the papers on Maya's desk. Fluttering, flying, scattering — when there was no wind, no fan, no explanation. During a test. The teacher annoyed. Maya embarrassed, confused, collecting her papers from the floor while other kids snickered.

Then the cup.

Cafeteria. Lunch. Maya talking about her dad moving out that weekend, her voice tight and thin and trying to sound like she didn't care. Her chocolate milk carton sitting on her tray six inches from her hand. Nobody touched it. Nobody bumped the table.

The carton launched itself. Straight sideways, three feet through the air, and exploded against Trevor Mathis's chest.

The whole cafeteria went silent. Maya stared. Trevor stared. Everyone stared.

"What the HELL?" Trevor said.

"I didn't touch it!" Maya said, and she hadn't — Sunny was looking right at her when it happened.

But Sunny was the only one who saw what ACTUALLY happened.

She saw the energy. Coming off Maya like heat shimmer off summer pavement — visible to Sunny in the way spirits were visible, in a layer of reality other people couldn't access. A roiling, chaotic wave of energy pouring off her best friend's body. Pressure that had nowhere to go. Grief and rage and helplessness so big that Maya's twelve-year-old body couldn't contain it, and it was LEAKING OUT.

There was no ghost.

There was no spirit behind the locker slamming, the papers flying, the milk launching. There was only Maya — Maya whose parents were splitting apart, Maya who cried every night, Maya who was too tough to ask for help — and the unbearable surplus of emotional energy that was physically manifesting because it had no other outlet.

A poltergeist. Not a ghost. A living person overflowing.

Sunny sat in the cafeteria and watched her best friend's pain fly across the room and splatter against a boy's chest and she understood something new: the line between the living and the dead wasn't as clear as she'd thought. Living people could generate phenomena too. The energy was the same. It was all frequency. All vibration.

She started spending more time with Maya after that. Not telling her why. Not saying *you're causing poltergeist activity because your emotions are too big.* Just... being there. Sitting with her. Listening. Offering the grounding presence she'd learned to use on spirits: steady, warm, receiving. And slowly — over weeks — the incidents stopped. Maya's energy calmed. Maya processed. Maya grieved. And the locker stopped slamming.

Sunny wrote in her notebook in blue gel pen: *"Type 4 confirmed. Poltergeists = living person overflow. Not ghosts. Help the person, not the 'spirit.'"*

---

It was also at twelve that the body comments started, and it was also at twelve that she started eating more, and these two things were connected but not in the way anyone would guess.

She'd always been thick. Round. Soft. The kind of body that other kids' parents described as "healthy" and other kids described less kindly. Carmen was thick. Abuela was thick. It was the body they came in — the Martinez women built solid and warm.

But at twelve, other people started having opinions about it.

"Damn, Sunny, you ate all the pizza?" — a boy at lunch, and the table laughing, and Sunny's cheeks burning but her face staying neutral because she'd been keeping a poker face about impossible things since she was eight. She could keep a poker face about this.

"You should try out for field hockey," the gym teacher said. "You've got the build for defense." *The build for defense.* Translation: you're big.

"Sunny's not fat, she's just... thick." — Maya, defending her, which meant it needed defending.

It stung. Of course it stung. But here's the thing about Sunny Martinez at twelve: she was carrying two secrets. Two invisible weights that nobody knew about. Her ability — the dead, the frequency, the terror and beauty of seeing what nobody else could see. And her body — the way it existed in a world that wanted it smaller.

And something in her decided, at twelve, that she was not going to hate her body. She couldn't afford to. She needed it too much. Her body was her instrument — the thing that felt frequencies, that grounded her after encounters, that kept her tethered to the living world. Being full made the dead quieter. Being warm made the cold of the Angry Ones bearable. Being PRESENT in her body — all of her body, every inch — was her defense against the incorporeal.

She couldn't hate the very thing that kept her alive.

So she ate. Not from grief. Not from compulsion. From STRATEGY. And from joy — because food was joy, food was Abuela's kitchen, food was love made physical. And she let her body be what it was: thick and strong and present and HERS.

She wrote in her notebook: *"Rule 9: Eating helps. Something about being full makes them quieter. I don't know why yet. But it's grounding. I need my body. ALL of it."*

# PART 5: AGE 13-14 (1998-1999) — "BECOMING"

---

Eighth grade opened like a door into a bigger room.

Sunny was thirteen in September of 1998, and she was different than she'd been a year ago in ways that showed and ways that didn't. The ways that showed: she was taller (five-four now, five-five by Christmas), thicker (size 14, gravitating toward 16), and she'd found her style — big hoop earrings, lip gloss (Wet N Wild, 99 cents at the drugstore, in shades of brown and berry), platform sneakers that added two inches, oversized jerseys (a FUBU one she'd begged for, a Champion hoodie in lavender, a vintage Selena concert tee she found at a thrift store that she wore until the screen print cracked). She wore her hair in two puffs most days, big and round, with butterfly clips holding back the front pieces. She looked like what she was: a thick, cute, put-together girl who liked herself.

The ways that didn't show: she could now classify a spirit's type by frequency alone in under three seconds. She had forty-seven pages of observations in her Lisa Frank notebook. She had rules — real, tested, refined rules — for engaging with the dead. She had encountered her first hostile entity and survived. She had discovered she could push back.

She was becoming something. Not just a girl who sees ghosts — a girl who *works* with the frequency of the dead. Who studies it. Who maps it. Who understands it better than most adults in the field (not that she knew what "the field" was yet). She was becoming an expert in something that didn't officially exist.

And in January of 1999, she found the internet.

---

AOL. The screaming handshake of the modem. The blocky blue logo. The cheerful "Welcome!" and "You've Got Mail!" The family computer in the living room — Carmen's tax refund purchase, a beige Dell with a monitor the size of a microwave.

Sunny had to wait until after 9 PM to use it (free minutes after nine, Mom's rule). She sat at the desk in the living room with the screen glowing and typed, with careful one-finger keystrokes:

**paranormal chat rooms**

And the world broke open.

There were HUNDREDS of them. Chat rooms with names like "GhostHunters Unite" and "Psychic Children Support" and "Real Paranormal Experiences" and "Shadow People Survivors." HUNDREDS of people — adults, mostly, but some teenagers — typing about their experiences in green text on black backgrounds at 2 AM.

Sunny read for three hours without typing a word. Just reading. Reading people describe, in terrible spelling and choppy sentences and CAPS LOCK emphasis, exactly what she'd been living for six years.

*"does anyone else feel them BEFORE they see them? like a buzzing?"*

Her heart stopped. She stared at the screen. Someone else felt the frequency.

*"my daughter sees people who aren't there. started at age 7. she describes them as 'the quiet people.' she's not making it up — she's told us things she couldn't possibly know."*

A parent. Describing their child. Describing Sunny.

*"I've been seeing since I was 5. I'm 34 now. It doesn't go away. But you learn to live with it. You get stronger."*

Thirty-four. An adult. Still seeing. Still living. Still functional. Proof that this didn't end in madness or isolation. Proof that you could carry this for decades and still be a person.

She created a screen name: **SunnyGirl85**

She lurked for two weeks before typing her first message in a chat room called "Young Psychics Support":

*"hi. i'm 13. i've been seeing spirits since i was 8. i can also feel their frequency before i see them — like a buzzing or a hum. does anyone else get that?"*

Three people responded within minutes:
*"Yes!! The frequency thing!!"*
*"Welcome, SunnyGirl. You're not alone here."*
*"I can feel them too. Since age 6. Different types have different frequencies."*

She sat at the computer in the dark living room and cried for the second time in her life over being told she wasn't alone. But this time the tears were quieter. Less desperate. More like... rain after drought. Relief that had been waiting years to fall.

---

She made friends online. Not best friends — you couldn't be best friends with a screen name and a 28.8k modem — but *allies*. People who understood. A woman named Sarah who'd been seeing since childhood and now worked as a hospice counselor ("I help people on both sides of the transition," she wrote once, and Sunny thought about that for days). A teenage boy in Wisconsin named Marcus who had almost exactly the same type of sensitivity she did. A retired professor named Harold who ran the chat room and moderated with gentle authority and once typed, at 11:47 PM on a Tuesday: *"What you children can do is not supernatural. It's natural. Just rare. Like perfect pitch. Like tetrachromacy. You see a spectrum most people can't. That's all."*

She saved these conversations. Printed them on the inkjet printer when nobody was looking. Folded them into her notebook.

August 1999. *The Sixth Sense*.

She went with Maya and two other girls from school. Summer blockbuster. Horror movie. The theater was packed and smelled like artificial butter and carpet cleaner, and Sunny had a large popcorn in her lap (grounding — always grounding) and her thick glasses slightly crooked from someone bumping her on the way in.

The movie opened and it was a ghost story and she settled in to be disappointed the way she usually was by ghost movies. They always got it wrong. The ghosts were always too dramatic, too scary, too active. Real ghosts mostly just stood there looking sad.

But this one was different.

The boy. Cole Sear. Small, scared, hiding under a blanket in his room while dead people walked through his house. His mother not understanding. His therapist trying. The weight of it — the WEIGHT — visible on his tiny shoulders in every frame.

"I see dead people," he whispered to Bruce Willis. And the theater went quiet and someone gasped and Sunny's popcorn went still in her hands because he'd just said it. Out loud. On screen. In a MOVIE. The thing she'd been carrying for six years, the secret, the weight, the impossible truth — someone had put it in a Hollywood movie and an actor said it and the audience went quiet with the solemnity of it.

She didn't cry in the theater. She held it. Held it through the rest of the movie — through the twist, which she saw coming forty minutes before anyone else ("They only see what they want to see" — she knew that already, she'd been invisible to people's perception her whole life). Held it through the credits. Through the walk to the parking lot. Through Maya saying "That was SO CREEPY" and the other girls chattering about whether they'd be able to sleep tonight.

She cried in the car. In the back seat, face turned toward the window, silent tears running down her round cheeks while Carmen drove and Maya sat beside her oblivious.

"You okay, baby?" Carmen asked, glancing in the rearview.

"Fine. Movie was just intense."

---

"I see dead people" became a joke within weeks.

People said it at school. On the playground. In the hallways. On TV. On the radio. In commercials. People said it in funny voices and laughed. People put it on T-shirts. It was a MEME before memes existed — a cultural punchline, a thing you said when something was creepy or weird or when you wanted to be funny at a party.

And every time someone said it — every time a classmate turned to another classmate in the lunchroom and said "I see dead people" in a spooky voice and everyone cracked up — something inside Sunny calcified a little more. Hardened. Became more private.

Because that line wasn't a joke to her. That line was her LIFE. That line was every night with strangers in her room and every day walking past people who didn't know they were dead and every moment of carrying impossible weight while being thirteen and trying to get through algebra.

She didn't tell anyone how much it hurt. She laughed when they laughed. She said "that movie was freaky" with the same casual tone everyone else used. She kept her secret tighter than ever.

But she also felt, underneath the hurt: *seen*. In some weird, backward, painful way — the movie had put her experience into the world. People were THINKING about it, even if they were thinking about it as fiction. Even if they were laughing. The CONCEPT was out there now. A child who sees ghosts. And the movie treated it with... seriousness. With weight. With compassion.

Cole Sear was a boy. He was skinny and white and Bruce Willis was helping him. Sunny was a thick Latina girl with no mentor and a Lisa Frank notebook. But the core of it — the heavy, lonely core of a child carrying something impossible — the movie got that right. And for that, she was grateful.

She bought the DVD the day it came out. Watched it fourteen more times. Paused on the scene where Cole's mother finally believes him and they cry together in the car and Sunny pressed her palm flat against the screen and whispered: "Maybe someday."

---

The family she helped lived on her block. The Washingtons.

Mrs. Washington was a church lady — sweet, always had candy in her purse, always had a word of encouragement for the kids on the street. But in the fall of 1999, she started looking different. Tired. Gray under the eyes. Walking slower. And once, when Sunny passed her on the sidewalk and said hi, she saw Mrs. Washington flinch — flinch and look over her shoulder at her own front door like something was in there she didn't want to go back to.

Sunny felt it from the sidewalk. A Stuck One in that house. Strong. Insistent. Sad as a three-day rain.

She didn't say anything at first. It wasn't her business. She'd learned to mind her own by now, to not insert herself into other people's hauntings unless they asked. But Mrs. Washington kept looking worse. Kept looking scared. And one afternoon in October, Sunny heard her talking to Carmen on the front steps: "I swear to God, Carmen, I hear him. In the basement. Shuffling around. His favorite chair still creaks like someone's sitting in it and he's been DEAD for six months."

Mr. Washington. Dead six months. Sunny hadn't known him well — a quiet man, a church man, big hands and a soft voice. He'd died of a heart attack in April.

She felt him that night from her bedroom window, across the narrow strip of yard that separated their houses. His frequency: warm, steady, sad. A Stuck One. Confused variety — he probably didn't fully understand he was dead yet. Or he understood but didn't know what to do about it. Lingering in his house because it was all he knew.

Sunny spent three days thinking about it. Weighing it. Running scenarios. She was thirteen and she was deciding whether to help a dead man cross over and what that even MEANT in practice.

On a Saturday morning, she walked over to the Washington house. Stood on the porch. Knocked.

Mrs. Washington answered. "Oh, Sunny! Hi, sweetheart."

"Mrs. Washington, can I... um. Can I come in? I think I can help. With the noises."

She didn't explain it well. She was thirteen and fumbling. But Mrs. Washington was at the end of her rope and she let Sunny in and sat in the living room while Sunny went down to the basement alone.

He was there. Mr. Washington. Sitting in his recliner — the one that creaked — in the corner of the basement where he'd clearly spent decades. Beer can on the armrest table. Radio on the shelf. His little kingdom.

"Mr. Washington?" Sunny said softly.

He looked up. His eyes — confused, foggy, the way the lost ones always looked — focused on her.

"You can see me," he said. Not a question.

"Yes, sir."

"Nobody else can. Martha walks right past me. I talk to her and she..." His face crumpled. "She doesn't hear me."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Am I dead?"

The question. The one she hated most. The one she'd learned to answer gently, after that first time at age nine when she'd blurted it and the spirit screamed.

"Yes, sir. You are. You had a heart attack in April."

He was quiet for a long time. The frequency around him shifted — from confused to processing. From static to something smoother. He was accepting it. Some of them took minutes. Some took years. Mr. Washington took about three minutes of silence.

"Martha's okay?" he asked.

"She misses you. But she's okay."

"Tell her..." He paused. "Tell her the life insurance papers are in the filing cabinet. Behind the tax stuff. She's been looking for them."

"I'll tell her."

"And tell her... tell her I heard her. Every night when she talks to me. I heard her."

Sunny's throat tightened. "I'll tell her."

He smiled. The same smile the soldier had given her in her bedroom at age eight — that grateful, relieved, *thank you for seeing me* smile. And then his frequency shifted again. Smoothed out. Harmonized. He was tuning up — that's what she called it, when a spirit was getting ready to go. Their jagged, stuck frequency becoming smooth and resonant, like an instrument being tuned.

"Okay," he said. Like he was agreeing to get in the car for a long drive. "Okay."

He faded. Slow. Warm. Peaceful. The recliner creaked one last time.

Sunny went upstairs. Sat at Mrs. Washington's kitchen table. Drank a glass of water because her hands were shaking (they always shook after). And said:

"Mrs. Washington, I think the noises will stop now. But... I think Mr. Washington wanted you to know that the life insurance papers are in the filing cabinet. Behind the tax stuff."

Mrs. Washington stared at her. Went to the basement. Came back five minutes later with a folder in her hand and tears on her face.

"How did you know that?"

"I just... knew."

She went home. She ate three of Abuela's empanadas. She wrote in her notebook: *"First successful transition assistance. Mr. Washington. 10/16/99. Type 2 (confused). He's gone now. He's at peace. I did that. I HELPED."*

She was thirteen years old and she'd just done something that mattered. Not a school grade. Not a gold star. Something REAL. She'd helped a dead man let go and a living woman find peace. That was purpose. That was what the psychic on the talk show meant by *gift*.

---

By the end of 1999, she was bigger. Not just physically — though she was, size 16 now, and wearing it with intention: big earrings, fitted tops over wide-leg jeans, platforms that made her five-seven, lip liner and gloss and baby hairs laid with gel. She looked like the girls in TLC videos. She looked like *herself*.

But she was bigger in other ways too. Bigger in capability. In understanding. In confidence. The notebook was thick now — sixty-plus pages of observations, rules, categories, case studies. She had an online community. She had proven to herself that her ability could be used for GOOD — not just endured.

She developed formal rules. Written on their own page in the notebook, bordered with silver stars:

1. I don't have to help every single one. I'm allowed to say no.
2. Not at night. NEVER at night anymore. Daytime only.
3. If it feels wrong — LEAVE. Don't wait. Don't investigate. Leave.
4. The angry ones feed on attention. Ignore them.
5. The sad ones just want to be seen. That's usually enough.
6. Never tell someone they're dead unless you're ready for their reaction.
7. Poltergeists aren't ghosts. Help the person, not the "spirit."
8. If it was never human — RUN.
9. Eating helps. Something about being full makes them quieter.
10. I am not broken. I am not crazy. I just see more than other people.

Ten rules. Hard-won. Each one paid for in fear or pain or hard-earned wisdom. Each one the result of being wrong first and learning after. No mentor gave her these. No book. She built them from her own experience, at nine and ten and eleven and twelve and thirteen years old.

The Dark One came in November.

Not the library basement — that one she'd pushed back. This was different. This was worse.

The old house on Meridian Street. Three blocks from school. Nobody had lived there in years. The windows were boarded. The porch sagged. Kids said it was haunted, which made Sunny laugh darkly because MOST buildings were haunted in some small way. Loops and Stuck Ones were everywhere. They weren't special.

But the Meridian house wasn't a normal haunting. Sunny had been feeling it for years — walking past, crossing the street to avoid it, never going near it. It wasn't a frequency she recognized. Not the sharp hostility of the library basement thing (which she now classified as an Angry One, Type 3, powerful but still HUMAN in origin — someone who'd died badly and become hateful in death). This was...

Nothing.

Not nothing as in emptiness. Nothing as in the ABSENCE of frequency. A hole in the world. A spot where the hum of existence — the background vibration of life and death and everything in between — just... stopped. Like a black hole for sound. Like someone had cut a circle out of reality and behind it was a void that had no name.

She was walking home from school. November. Gray sky. Cold enough for her breath to show. Walkman in her coat pocket, headphones around her neck — Lauryn Hill, *Miseducation*, on repeat for the third week straight. She was passing the Meridian house on the OTHER side of the street, as always, as ALWAYS —

And she felt it looking at her.

Not like the library thing, which had been hostile but ultimately comprehensible — anger, she understood anger. This wasn't anger. It wasn't any emotion she had a name for. It was *attention*. Pure attention. Vast and old and completely inhuman.

She stopped walking.

Looked at the house.

In the second-floor window — the one that wasn't boarded — something stood. Not a person. Not the shape of a person. A PRESENCE. Dark against dark. The kind of thing you can only see because the darkness where it stands is somehow DARKER than the darkness around it. As if it were eating the ambient light.

It was looking at her. She couldn't see eyes. She couldn't see features. But she felt the attention like a laser pointer on her skin.

And then she felt something she'd never felt before: it reached out. Not physically. Frequency-wise. It extended something toward her — a tendril of that anti-frequency, that void where vibration should be — and it touched the edge of her perception and —

Cold. Not temperature cold. *Soul* cold. The cold of absolute emptiness. The cold of a thing that was never alive and never dead because it existed OUTSIDE those categories entirely. Something ancient. Something from below the frequency of life.

She ran.

She ran the way she'd run from the library — but faster, because this was worse, this was WORSE — she ran four blocks without stopping, her backpack slamming against her spine, her platforms threatening to twist her ankles, and she didn't stop until she was inside her house with the door locked and Abuela's rosary clutched in her fist.

She sat on the floor of her room. Shaking. Sick. The nausea lasted an hour — her body rejecting the residue of that contact like poison. Like her cells were expelling something foreign.

She wrote in her notebook that night. Her handwriting was shaky:

*"Type 5. The ??? ones. The Dark Ones. I know what they are now. They were never human. Never alive. They're something ELSE. Something OLD. Something from UNDER everything else. I don't know what they want. I don't know why one of them is in that house. I'm never going back there. If it was never human — RUN. Rule 8. Rule 8. Rule 8."*

She underlined Rule 8 three times.

She was thirteen and she had just encountered something beyond her understanding and she had the wisdom — a hard, bright wisdom that most adults never develop — to admit that she was out of her depth. To draw a line. To say: *I am not ready for this. Not yet. Maybe not ever.*

# PART 6: VIGNETTES — "WHAT SHE SEES"

### The Old Man on the Bench

Every day at 3:30 PM, on the bus bench at the corner of Oak and Fifth, an old man sits and watches traffic.

He wears a gray cardigan buttoned wrong — one button off at the bottom, making the whole front hang crooked. His pants are brown and creased. His shoes are the kind nobody makes anymore — cap-toe, leather, polished to a mirror shine even though he's been dead for probably thirty years.

He's a Loop. Sunny knows this because he never looks at her, never reacts, never changes. 3:30 PM. Same bench. Same posture. Same worried expression, like he's waiting for someone who's running late.

She passes him every day on her walk home from school. At first — age eight, nine — she'd say hi. Wave. Try to get his attention. Nothing. He's watching the traffic with that worried look and he doesn't know she exists and he doesn't know HE doesn't exist. Not really. He's just an echo. A stamp in time.

At age twelve, she stops trying to get his attention and starts noticing other things. He's always looking toward the eastbound lane. Watching the cars come from the left. Waiting for a specific car that will never come.

She thinks about him sometimes at night. Who was he waiting for? A wife coming home from work? A child? A friend who'd said "meet me at 3:30"? What happened to make this one afternoon repeat forever — did the person never show up? Did something happen to THEM?

She'll never know. That's the thing about Loops. They're unsolvable. They're pain without context. A forever-question with no answer.

She still says "afternoon" to him when she passes, even though he can't hear. It feels right. Someone should acknowledge him. Even if he's just a recording.

### The Girl from the 1970s

The playground behind the elementary school. Early morning, before other kids arrive. A girl, maybe seven years old, in a brown corduroy jumper and a turtleneck and knee socks. Her hair in two braids with those round plastic ball-ties at the bottom — the kind you see in old photos.

She's on the monkey bars. Has been on the monkey bars every morning since Sunny started at this school. Crossing and re-crossing, hand over hand, with a concentration so fierce it looks like homework.

She's a Loop — but barely. A faint one. Some mornings she's almost invisible. Some mornings she's vivid. She seems to appear more on sunny days, less on gray ones. Sunny has a theory about this: maybe the light was important to whatever moment created her. Maybe she died on a bright day. Maybe the sun is part of the stamp.

Sunny has never been afraid of her. Not once. The playground girl radiates nothing but focus and a tiny, persistent joy — the joy of a seven-year-old whose body works, whose hands grip, whose arms are strong enough to carry her across and across and across.

Sometimes Sunny sits on the bench nearby and watches her before school starts and feels something like affection for this echo of a child who loved monkey bars so much the playground remembered.

### The Angry Man in the Library

He's still there.

After the basement incident — after she ran — she didn't go to that library for three months. But the periodicals were there and the good books were there and she REFUSED to let a dead thing take her library away from her. So she went back. But only to the upper floors. Never the basement. Never again.

He's still there. She can feel him from the first floor — that sharp, cold frequency pulsing up through the floorboards like a second heartbeat under the building. Some days worse than others. Some days she walks in and the frequency is so strong she walks back out immediately.

She's named him. Not out loud, not officially. But in her head: Mr. Basement. (She's thirteen. Her naming conventions are still evolving.)

She doesn't know what he was in life. Doesn't know why he's so angry. Doesn't know if he can be helped — some of the Angry Ones are too far gone, too calcified in their rage, more fury than person by now. Maybe he was always like this. Maybe he died wrong and it curdled him. Maybe centuries of being stuck and unseen rotted whatever kindness he'd once had.

She doesn't need to know. That's Rule 3: if it feels wrong, leave. She doesn't owe him curiosity. She doesn't owe him understanding. She owes herself safety.

But sometimes, on the upper floor, shelving a returned book she'd been reading, she feels him PUSH. Up through the floor. That cold hatred, reaching. Testing. Seeing if she's still there. Seeing if she'll come back down.

She pushes back. Just a little. Just a pulse of *I'm here and I'm not afraid of you and I'm not coming down there.* A boundary. A wall she builds with her own frequency.

He pushes. She pushes. Neither moves. Stalemate.

She'll take it.

### The Woman in the Hospital Parking Lot

The hospital where Carmen works is across town. Sunny goes there sometimes after school — waits in the lobby until Mom's shift ends, does homework in the cafeteria, reads books in the family waiting room.

The parking lot is full of them.

That makes sense, if you think about it. People die in hospitals. A lot of people. And some of them — the sudden ones, the confused ones, the ones who weren't ready — wander out the doors in the daze of their new death and make it as far as the parking lot before they stop. Because the parking lot is where your car is. The parking lot is where you go when you're leaving the hospital. And they were supposed to be leaving.

There are maybe six or seven regulars. Sunny sees them from the lobby windows. Standing by cars that are no longer there. Walking between rows that have been re-lined and re-painted since their time. Looking for something familiar in a landscape that changed without them.

But one of them is different. A woman in a hospital gown — the paper kind, the one that opens in the back. She's barefoot. She's standing at the edge of the parking lot, where the asphalt meets the grass, and she's looking across the road at the apartment building across the street.

She's not looking at the building. She's looking at a specific window.

Sunny sat in the lobby one evening and watched her for twenty minutes. The woman never moved, never wavered. Just stared at that window with a yearning so deep it pulsed off her in waves — a warm, desperate, reaching frequency that felt like arms extended toward something just out of reach.

Her family is in that building. Has to be. A child, probably. Someone she was thinking about when she died — in a hospital bed, in a paper gown, thinking about the person across the street instead of herself. And she got up (she thinks she got up) and she walked out (she thinks she walked out) and she came outside to get closer and she can't get any closer. The edge of the parking lot is as far as she goes. Something holds her there. The boundary of the place where she died.

Sunny has never spoken to her. Doesn't know what she'd say. Can't tell the woman's family — doesn't know who they are, which apartment, which window. Can only see her standing there, barefoot on asphalt, reaching without hands toward a window where maybe a light is on and maybe someone is eating dinner and doesn't know their mother is forty feet away, watching.

Some of them she can't help. That's the weight. That's the part that makes her eat an extra empanada and listen to Lauryn Hill on repeat and hug her own mother harder when she picks her up from the lobby.

### The Soldier at the Intersection

The first one she ever saw visit her room. The one who smiled and faded. He's not just in her room.

She sees him at the intersection of Maple and Broadway too. Not every day — maybe once a month. Standing on the median strip in his uniform, watching the cars pass. At ease, hands behind his back. Not a Loop — he's aware. He looks at her when she passes.

He never speaks. Just nods. That same grateful nod from her bedroom. Like she's doing him a favor just by seeing him. Like being acknowledged is enough.

She's done research. Spent two hours at the library (upper floors) going through WWII records for the neighborhood. A man named James Edward Cartwright lived in the house before Sunny's family moved in. Served in the Pacific. Came home in 1945. Died in 1967 of emphysema. Fifty-two years old.

She thinks it's him. The uniform fits the era. The age seems right. And he was in her house — HIS house — watching a little girl sleep in what might have been his bedroom, and he was just... glad. Glad to not be invisible anymore.

She doesn't mind him. Of all the spirits she's encountered, Corporal Cartwright (she named him — she names the nice ones) is the most welcome. He's quiet. He's respectful. He comes and goes. And that nod — that "thank you for seeing me" nod — makes her feel like she's doing something that matters, even on the days she wishes she couldn't see any of them.

### The Teenager Who Follows Her

This one is newer. Started about six months ago — spring of 1999. A girl. Maybe sixteen, maybe seventeen. Close to Sunny's age, which makes it worse somehow. Close enough in age that they could have been friends, in another life.

She wears a denim jacket and a scrunchie and her face is permanently confused — not frightened, not angry, just *confused*. Like she walked into a room and forgot why she was there, except the room is death and she can't find the door back out.

She appears in Sunny's peripheral vision. At school. At the store. Walking home. Always at a distance — twenty feet, thirty feet. Not approaching. Just... watching. Following at a distance, the way a lost dog follows a stranger, hoping they'll turn around and notice.

Sunny hasn't talked to her yet. She's been observing. Categorizing. (Type 2, Stuck One, confused variety. Relatively recent — the clothing is modern, the hairstyle is current. She died recently. Within the last few years.)

It bothers Sunny that this girl is so close to her own age. Every other spirit she's encountered has been older — adults, elderly people, the occasional child from decades past. But this girl died RECENTLY and she was YOUNG and she's following Sunny with that confused expression and it makes Sunny think about mortality in a way thirteen-year-olds shouldn't have to.

One day soon, Sunny will talk to her. Will stop walking, turn around, and say: "Hey. I can see you. Are you okay?"

She's working up to it. Because this one feels personal. This one feels like it might be hard.

### The One Who Sang

Once — only once — at a bus stop on Elm Street, at dusk, waiting for Carmen to pick her up: an old woman appeared on the bench beside her and SANG.

Not talked. Not moaned or whispered or begged. Sang. In Spanish. An old song — something Sunny half-recognized from Abuela's kitchen radio, something about rivers and forgetting and love that outlasts the body.

She sang for four minutes. Clear and sweet and completely oblivious to Sunny or the cars or the living world. A Loop, maybe — but a BEAUTIFUL one. An echo of joy instead of pain. A moment of singing so pure it stamped itself into the bus stop bench and replayed at dusk when the light was just right.

Sunny sat next to her and listened. Didn't try to interact. Just... listened. And when Carmen pulled up and honked and Sunny got in the car, she looked back and the bench was empty and the song was gone.

She went home and wrote in her notebook: *"Not all impressions are pain. Some are joy. Some are love. Some are just a woman singing because she was happy once and the world remembered."*

### The House

A two-story rowhouse on Kettering Avenue. Brick, painted white sometime in the 1980s (the paint peeling now, showing red underneath). A porch with a metal railing and two folding chairs and a pot of marigolds that Carmen replants every spring. The door is green. The numbers — 4217 — are brass and slightly crooked.

Inside: the smell of whatever Abuela is cooking layered over Pine-Sol and the fabric softener Carmen uses too much of. The carpet is that specific 1990s beige, worn thin in the path between living room and kitchen. There are framed photos on every surface — Sunny as a baby (round, brown, enormous eyes behind even-then-thick glasses), Carmen's quinceañera (lavender dress, big hair, 1982 in all its glory), Abuela's wedding (black and white, serious faces, 1951). Family. Evidence of existing.

The living room has the couch (floral, inherited from someone), the TV (27-inch JVC, the biggest they could afford), the Dell computer on a desk in the corner (their newest possession, their connection to the outside world after 9 PM). There's an afghan on the couch — crocheted by Abuela in shades of brown and gold — that Sunny wraps herself in every time she watches TV.

The kitchen: linoleum floor in a pattern that was once yellow and white. A table for four that always has salt shakers and a napkin holder and whatever mail came that day. The stove where the lady in the blue housedress still stirs nothing (Sunny's been sharing the kitchen with her for six years now; it's like having a very quiet roommate). Abuela's spice rack, hand-labeled in Spanish. The window over the sink that looks out at the Washingtons' house next door.

Upstairs: Carmen's room (always slightly messy, always smelling of perfume — *Exclamation!* by Coty, the purple bottle), Abuela's room (immaculate, religious, a small altar with candles and saints and a rosary draped over the bedpost), and Sunny's room.

### Her Room

Small. Maybe ten by ten. But it contains multitudes.

The bed against the wall — twin size, purple bedspread (she begged for this specific shade of purple for her twelfth birthday and Carmen found it at T.J. Maxx). Three pillows, including one shaped like a crescent moon. The Lisa Frank notebook lives under the moon pillow. Always.

Bookshelves — two of them, both overflowing. Books stacked horizontally on top of vertically-shelved books. Books on the floor in piles. Books on the windowsill. The range: *Matilda* (read eleven times), *The Westing Game* (seven times), *Scary Stories to Tell in the Dark* (she laughed at most of them — too tame — but the illustrations were genuinely unsettling), *A Wrinkle in Time* (loved the idea of folding space), *Roll of Thunder, Hear My Cry* (cried), *The Giver* (terrified her in a different way than ghosts — the idea of chosen emptiness), and the adult shelf: *Life After Life* by Raymond Moody, *The Physics of Consciousness* (she understood maybe 40% but she kept trying), *When Ghosts Speak* (a medium's memoir, found at the used bookstore, read three times).

The desk — covered in gel pens, butterfly clips, a half-finished friendship bracelet, the clock radio (pink, always tuned to AM for Coast to Coast), her old Tamagotchi (dead again — electronics die near her constantly, a phenomenon she's documented), and a small altar.

The altar — Abuela helped her build it when she was eleven. Not religious exactly, though a small Virgin of Guadalupe statue presides. A white candle. A chunk of amethyst (bought at a crystal shop with birthday money). A photo of her great-grandmother, faded sepia. A sprig of dried rosemary. Abuela said the altar would "make the room quieter" and she was RIGHT — the frequency inside these four walls is lower, calmer, more buffered since the altar went up. Sunny doesn't know if it's the objects or the intention or both. She doesn't care. It works.

Glow-in-the-dark stars on the ceiling — still there, still essential, still doing their job. She's thirteen and she still needs them. Will probably need them at thirty. That's okay.

The walls: a poster of Selena (the *Dreaming of You* album cover, Selena in a purple hat, gorgeous). A poster of TLC (*CrazySexyCool* era, all three of them in baggy silk and attitude). A small framed print of the "I Want to Believe" poster from *The X-Files* that she bought at a poster shop at the mall. A photograph of her and Maya at Six Flags, both screaming on a rollercoaster, printed at a one-hour photo place. A calendar from the library's annual book sale with a different literary quote each month.

The closet: FUBU jersey (blue and white, oversized, her pride). Champion hoodies in three colors. Wide-leg jeans. Platform sneakers lined up on the floor. A box on the top shelf that contains: the VHS tape (the talk show, her most sacred possession), a collection of printed AOL chat conversations, a letter from Maya that says "you're my best friend forever," and a mood ring that actually kind of works (it changes color when spirits are near — she's documented this).

### Carmen

Her mother works twelve-hour shifts. Home health aide — she goes to people's houses and takes care of them when they're too sick or too old to take care of themselves. She helps them bathe and eat and take medicine and get from the bed to the chair and back. She comes home smelling of latex gloves and Dial soap and someone else's house.

Carmen is thirty-one years old in 1999. She was sixteen when she had Sunny — a whole other lifetime ago, a version of herself that made decisions she's made peace with. She's thick like Sunny, loud like Sunny will become, full of laughter and opinions and hugs that feel like being wrapped in a warm towel.

She loves her daughter fiercely and does not understand her.

The ghost thing — she doesn't talk about it anymore. Not since the therapist, not since the grocery store incident. She's chosen a position somewhere between belief and denial: *my daughter says she sees things. The therapist said she's fine. She seems fine. She IS fine. I'm not going to make it a thing.* It's not dismissal, exactly. It's survival. Carmen has enough to worry about — bills and work and keeping a house running on one income and her own mother getting older — without adding "my child might be psychic" to the list.

But she does small things. She never turns off Sunny's light without asking. She never says "there's nothing there" anymore. She hugs Sunny extra tight after the nights when Sunny comes downstairs looking pale and tired. She doesn't ask *what happened* — she just makes hot chocolate and sits with her.

Love looks like this sometimes: not understanding, but staying.

### Abuela Rosa

Seventy-three years old. Small — five feet even. Silver hair she keeps in a bun every day. Skin mapped with wrinkles that Sunny traces sometimes with her fingertip when they sit together on the couch. Hands that are always doing something: cooking, folding, praying, patting Sunny's knee.

She had it too. The sight. From age six to twenty-three, when it faded the way the psychic man on TV said it sometimes fades in adulthood. She's never told Sunny this directly. But Sunny knows. She knows from the way Abuela's eyes track to the same spots Sunny's do. She knows from the books left around. She knows from the sage and the rosary and the altar and the quiet, steady presence of a woman who *understands* without ever saying so.

Their language about it is silence. Comfortable silence. Shared silence. The silence of two people in a room who both see the man in the chair but neither needs to say so. Abuela's mentorship is made of: books left where Sunny will find them. Sage burned at the right times. A hand on the knee when Sunny is overwhelmed. The gentle making of food that grounds.

Sunny sometimes wonders what Abuela's experience was like. In the 1930s, in Mexico, as a little girl who saw the dead. There would have been no TV shows. No library books. No internet chat rooms. Only the church and the family and whatever the culture had words for — *bruja, vidente, loca*. She wonders if Abuela was afraid. She wonders if anyone believed her.

She'll ask someday. When she's ready. When they're both ready. For now, the silence is enough.

### Maya

Maya Rodriguez has been Sunny's best friend since first grade and she knows, as of December 1998, about the ghost thing.

The reveal wasn't dramatic. It wasn't planned. It happened because Maya stayed over one night and at 3 AM, the teenager by the closet was having a particularly loud night (frequency-wise — Sunny could feel the anger pulsing off her like heat from an engine). And Sunny, half-asleep and not thinking clearly, said: "Can you PLEASE chill tonight, I have a friend over."

"Who are you talking to?" Maya, awake in the sleeping bag on the floor.

Silence. Sunny's heart pounding.

"Who were you just talking to, Sunny?"

And because it was 3 AM and because she was tired and because Maya was her best friend and because she was THIRTEEN and carrying this alone was getting heavier every year — she told her. Not everything. Not the notebook or the categories or the online community or the frequency physics. Just: "I see ghosts. I've always seen ghosts. There's one in my room right now. She's mad but she won't hurt us. I've never told anyone because nobody believes me."

Maya was quiet for a long time. Then: "The teenager? By your closet? Is she wearing, like, a plaid skirt?"

Sunny's blood went cold. "You can see her?"

"No. I can... feel that something is there. I thought it was just your room being creepy. Like, your room has always felt... *full*."

Maya couldn't see them. But she could feel the frequency. Just barely. Just enough to sense *something* without seeing anything. Barely Sensitive — a 0.5 on a scale where Sunny was an 8. But enough to make her believe without question.

"I believe you," Maya said. "I've always known something was different about you. I just didn't know it was THIS."

They stayed up until dawn. Sunny talked and talked and talked — years of silence pouring out, every story, every encounter, every fear. Maya listened with her eyes huge and her hands wrapped around a mug of tea Sunny made at 4 AM. She asked questions. Good ones. Smart ones. She didn't run. She didn't say "you're crazy." She said: "That grocery store thing in second grade when you freaked out Mrs. Delgado — THAT'S what that was?"

"Yeah."

"DUDE."

They've been closer since then. Not talking about it constantly — but knowing. Maya knowing. Having ONE person in her physical life who knows and believes and doesn't think she's broken. It changed the weight. It didn't remove it. But it made it carryable.

### School

George Washington Middle School. Eighth grade. A brick building from the 1950s with narrow halls and lockers that stick and a gym that smells like rubber and frustration.

Sunny gets B's. Mostly. An occasional A in English (when she writes essays that would get an A++ if the teacher knew what experiences she was drawing from). An occasional C in math (not because she can't — because she can't make herself care about polynomials when she spent last night helping a dead woman understand that her daughter survived the accident she didn't). She doesn't try to be average. She IS average — in the way that a person with a second full-time job appears to be average at their first one. School is her first job. The dead are her second. There's only so much attention to go around.

She's known as the sweet one. The hugging one. The one who always has a snack to share. The one who'll sit with you if you're having a bad day. She's not popular (popular takes maintenance, and she doesn't have the energy). She's not unpopular (she's too warm for that, too present, too good at making people feel seen — years of practice with the dead translating directly into emotional intelligence with the living). She's... liked. Quietly. Widely. Reliably.

Her teachers describe her as "lovely" and "well-adjusted" and "sweet but could apply herself more." They have no idea. They will never have any idea.

She is thirteen years old. She is everything at once.

### The Signal (December 1999)

She's watching the ball drop on New Year's Eve. Dick Clark on screen, the crowd in Times Square, 1999 rolling into 2000 and everyone worried about Y2K and Sunny on the couch with Carmen and Abuela and a plate of *tamales* and the future arriving like it always does: without asking permission.

And at 12:00:00 AM on January 1st, 2000 — for 3.7 seconds — every spirit she has ever felt goes silent.

ALL of them. The lady in the kitchen. The man at the bus stop. The playground girl. Mr. Basement. The soldier. The teenager in her closet. The woman at the hospital. Every frequency she monitors, consciously and unconsciously, every background hum she's learned to live with the way you live with traffic noise — it all goes QUIET.

Not just quiet. Aligned. Every single spirit turns the same direction. Southwest. Like a compass needle. Like something pulled them.

3.7 seconds of absolute silence in a world that has NEVER been silent for her. Not since she was eight years old.

Then it comes back. All of it. LOUDER. Stronger. Closer. The frequencies amplified, the presences more vivid, the dead more HERE than they've ever been.

Sunny drops her *tamale*. Stands up. Puts her hand on the wall because the world is TILTING — not physically, but frequency-wise. Something fundamental shifted. Something in the architecture of what she perceives just... changed key.

"Sunny? What's wrong?" Carmen, concerned.

"Nothing. Dizzy. New Year's excitement." She sits back down. Smiles. Eats her *tamale*.

But her hand is shaking. And Abuela is looking at her from across the room with an expression Sunny has never seen on her face before: recognition, yes — but also fear.

Something happened. Something BIG. Something she doesn't have categories for yet.

She doesn't know that six hundred miles away, five strangers just played their instruments in the same room for the first time. She doesn't know that the sound they made — The Signal — just rearranged the frequency architecture of reality itself. She doesn't know that she just felt, in her bones, the moment everything changed.

She's thirteen years old. She's been carrying the dead for five years. She has ten rules in a holographic notebook and a VHS tape she's watched thirty-seven times and a best friend who believes her and a grandmother who *understands* her and a mother who loves her without understanding.

She is becoming something. She doesn't know what yet.

But she's getting stronger. And the world — the whole world, living and dead, seen and unseen — just got louder. Something woke up. Something is shifting. And she, Soleil "Sunny" Martinez, is going to be right in the middle of it.

She pushes her glasses up her nose. She finishes her *tamale*. She whispers to the room — to whatever's listening, dead or alive or something in between:

"Okay. I'm ready."
