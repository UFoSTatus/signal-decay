## CHILDHOOD (Ages 5-12)

### Family Vacations & Trips

**1. The Philippines — Age 8 (Summer 2009)**

Every three or four years, the Solanas flew to Manila to visit Lorna's side — a sprawling, noisy clan spread across Quezon City and Cavite. Oren had Lahore; Ami had Manila. Different heat. Same weight of belonging.

The flight: sixteen hours through Narita. Ami slept on her father's shoulder and woke to the descent — Manila at dawn, the city spreading below like a circuit board of orange lights and tin roofs. The humidity hit when the plane door opened. A wall of warm wet air that smelled like exhaust and frangipani and rain-on-concrete.

Lola's (grandmother's) house in Quezon City: the tiled floors always cool, the TV always on (TFC — The Filipino Channel — playing game shows in Tagalog that Ami half-understood). Twelve cousins ranging from ages 3 to 17, all competing for attention, all touching her hair ("Amerikana!"), all dragging her to the sari-sari store for Chippy and C2 iced tea.

The sounds: jeepney horns (that specific two-tone honk), roosters at 4 AM, karaoke from the neighbor's garage (every night, EVERY night — someone's tito belting Sinatra at maximum volume with maximum confidence and minimum skill). These sounds should have been noise. For Ami, they were music. The first time she heard Manila, she thought: *this is a song that never stops.*

Rafe took her to a guitar shop in Makati. A small place, all handmade instruments, the smell of wood shavings and lacquer. The luthier — an old man with hands like carved wood himself — let her hold a classical guitar sized for a child. She played one chord (D major — the first one Rafe had taught her) and the old man said, in Tagalog to Rafe: "She has the hands." Rafe bought the guitar. It cost $200 (expensive for them). She still has it.

**2. Yosemite — Age 6 (Fall 2007)**

A camping trip. Just the three of them — Rafe, Lorna, Ami. A tent that was slightly too small and a sleeping bag that smelled like the last person who rented it from REI.

What Ami remembers: the SILENCE. Bay Area silence isn't really silence — it's traffic-distant and neighbor-adjacent. Yosemite silence was actual silence. Standing in a meadow at dusk, the grass up to her waist, and hearing literally nothing except her own heartbeat.

And then: the sounds that WEREN'T nothing. Water over rocks in the distance. A bird she couldn't identify. Wind through pine needles (a specific shushing sound — not like wind through leaves, more like someone saying "shhhhh" very gently, on a loop). She stood there so long her parents came looking for her and found her motionless in the meadow, head tilted, eyes closed, LISTENING.

"What are you doing, anak?" Rafe asked.

"There's someone here," she said. She was six. She meant the sound felt like a presence. But her parents exchanged a look. Because Ami said things like this a lot.

**3. Big Sur — Age 10 (Spring 2011)**

A weekend trip, just Ami and her father. Lorna was installing a show in the city. Rafe drove them down Highway 1 — the Pacific on the left, the cliffs on the right, the road winding like a musical phrase that refuses to resolve.

They stopped at Pfeiffer Beach. The purple sand. The arch rock. The waves hitting the shore with a force Ami could feel in her molars. She brought her guitar (the small one from Manila) and sat on the rocks and tried to play something that matched the ocean.

She couldn't. The ocean was in a time signature she didn't have. She got frustrated, put the guitar down, and just sat. Listened. Let the sound be bigger than her.

Rafe sat next to her. Didn't play. Didn't suggest. Just existed there while his daughter wrestled with something she couldn't name. After thirty minutes, she picked the guitar back up and played a single note — let it ring — and then another — let it ring — not a melody, just frequencies placed in space like Lorna placed paintings on a gallery wall. Space between. Breath between.

"That's new," Rafe said.

"I'm not playing a song. I'm playing the gaps."

He didn't fully understand. But he recognized it as something true.

*(This moment — "playing the gaps" — is what eventually becomes Ami's signature style. Negative space as music. Silence as loud as sound. She traces it back to this beach, this afternoon, this father who let her figure it out alone.)*

**4. Monterey Bay Aquarium — Age 7 (2008)**

School field trip but Rafe chaperoned. Thirty second-graders and their screaming. But Ami stood in front of the kelp forest exhibit — the massive tank, the kelp swaying, the fish moving through it — and went completely still.

What she heard: the way the kelp moved carried a rhythm. Not random — patterned. Responding to the water circulation system's hum (a mechanical pulse the other kids didn't notice). The fish moved WITH it, their paths timed to the sway. Everything in the tank was in sync. A silent orchestra.

She pressed her hand against the glass and felt the vibration of the filtration system. Low. Constant. A drone note. And she hummed along — not the melody, the FOUNDATION — and Rafe watched her and thought: *she hears things nobody else hears.*

(He was right. But not in the way he thought.)

### Birthday Traditions

Ami's birthday: June 21st — summer solstice. The longest day.

Lorna made it a thing: "You were born on the longest day, so your birthday IS the day. All of it." From sunup to sundown, Ami got to choose everything. What to eat. Where to go. What music played in the house. The rule was: Ami's frequency all day.

Morning: Rafe made pandesal (Filipino bread rolls) from scratch and they ate them hot with butter and coffee (Ami drank coffee with her parents from age nine — Filipino thing, heavily creamed and sugared).

Afternoon: Usually the ocean. Pacifica or Half Moon Bay. Sitting in the sand. Ami playing guitar. Lorna sketching. Rafe napping.

Evening: Rafe cooked pancit (Ami's choice, always — the noodles slippery and sweet-savory, bean sprouts crunching). A cake from Goldilocks (Filipino bakery on Mission Street — ube-macapuno, purple and coconut, always). Candles. Singing.

Gift tradition: Lorna always made something. A painting. A collage. One year, a shadow box with objects from Ami's year — a concert ticket, a guitar pick, a feather, a photo of the three of them. These lined Ami's bedroom wall. A timeline of herself, curated by her mother's eye.

After age 13 (post-piano incident, post-isolation deepening), the birthdays got quieter. Just the three of them. Smaller. But the tradition held: pandesal, ocean, pancit, Goldilocks cake. Even in the bad years. Even when Ami didn't want to celebrate. Her parents showed up anyway, with bread and love and a candle, and said: "You're here. That's enough."

### Summer Routines

Summer in Daly City (ages 8-12) meant:

**Morning**: Fog. The specific Daly City fog that rolls in off the Pacific and makes June mornings feel like November. Ami's house was on a hill and some mornings you couldn't see past the front yard. She loved it — the fog muted everything, made the world quiet, made her bedroom feel like it was floating.

**Midday**: Guitar in the garage. Rafe converted half their garage into a music space — soundproofing (foam panels, secondhand), a small amp, two guitars (his Telecaster, her classical). Summer meant hours in the garage. Not lessons — Rafe didn't teach her formally. He just played and she played and they figured things out together.

**Afternoon**: Walking to the library on Mission Street. Ami read voraciously — not just novels, but art books (Lorna's influence), music theory books (some for adults, which she half-understood), and manga. She'd sit in the library's quiet room and exist in that specific library stillness — the air conditioning hum, the page-turning rustle, the distant beep of books being checked out.

**Evening**: Dinner together. Always. The Solanas were small (three people) but PRESENT. Meals were long — an hour, sometimes more. Talking. Rafe playing something on his phone ("listen to this bass line, anak"). Lorna showing Ami gallery submissions on her laptop ("what do you see in this one?"). Ami absorbing their creative attention like a plant in sunlight.

**Night**: The dead came at night — as sound, never sight. Since age 7, Ami's nights had a visitor. Usually "Keys" — the jazz pianist who'd attached himself early. He didn't do anything frightening. She'd hear piano notes drifting through her room as she fell asleep. Phantom arpeggios. Gentle. Like a lullaby from someone who couldn't touch the physical world anymore but could still PLAY in the space between dimensions.

She didn't tell her parents until she was twelve. (They didn't believe her. Then they did. Then they were afraid. Then — because they were Rafe and Lorna, creative and open — they tried to understand.)

### Holiday Memory — Christmas Eve (Age 9, 2010)

The Filipino Christmas: not December 25th — that's fine, that's presents and American things. The REAL celebration is Noche Buena — December 24th, midnight. Lorna's family tradition, imported wholesale.

At 10 PM, the Solana-Reyes extended family gathered at Tita Carmen's house in South San Francisco. Thirty people in a house meant for twelve. The noise level OBSCENE — titas laughing, lolos telling stories, cousins chasing each other, the TV playing a Manny Pacquiao replay. The food: lechon (whole roasted pig, its skin crackling), pancit, lumpia, bibingka (rice cake cooked in banana leaves), a ham studded with pineapple rings, and tupperware of kare-kare that someone brought from a restaurant because nobody trusted Tito Jun's recipe.

Midnight mass at the parish on El Camino: the incense, the singing, the old women with their rosaries. Ami didn't understand the liturgy but she understood the SOUND — the congregation singing "Silent Night" in Filipino-accented English, her Lola's voice wavering but committed, the church acoustics turning everything into reverb.

Back home at 1 AM: the gift exchange. Not secret Santa — just everyone giving everyone small things. Ami got a set of guitar picks from Rafe (custom-made, tortoiseshell, her initials stamped in gold), a sketchbook from Lorna, and ube ice cream from Lola.

She fell asleep in the car on the way home, full of food and warmth, and heard — faintly, in the space between waking and sleep — "Keys" playing "Silent Night" in jazz voicings, just for her, his phantom notes layering under the car radio like a secret only she could hear.

### A Family Car Ride (Age 11)

Driving home from a gig. Rafe had played a session — wedding band, covers, four hours on his feet. Ami went to watch (she always watched when she could). It was late — 10:30 PM, past her bedtime, but Lorna was at an opening and couldn't pick her up so Ami was Rafe's date.

In the car: Rafe's Telecaster in the back seat, lying in its case like it was sleeping. Ami in the passenger seat (she was tall for eleven — just barely legal). The highway dark, the Bay Bridge lights ahead, the radio on 88.5 KQED (jazz, always jazz in Rafe's car).

A Wes Montgomery track came on. Octave melody. Clean and round and warm.

Rafe: "Hear that? He's playing the melody an octave below and above at the same time. One thumb. No pick."

Ami: "How?"

Rafe: "Practice. And something else. Something you can't teach."

Ami: "What?"

Rafe: *(thinking)* "Loving the sound more than the technique. Playing because the note wants to exist, not because you want to prove you can hit it."

Ami sat with this. Watching the bridge lights reflect on the water below. Thinking about notes that want to exist. Thinking about the sounds she heard that nobody else did — the phantom piano, the whispered harmonies, the presence behind her when she played. Those notes wanted to exist too. They pushed through from somewhere else because they NEEDED to be heard.

She didn't tell Rafe this. Just said: "I think I understand."

He reached over and squeezed her shoulder. "I know you do, anak."

### Her Bedroom at Age 10 (Daly City, 2011)

Upstairs. The room with the sloped ceiling (it was a converted attic — Lorna's choice, because "artists need garrets"). Walls painted a muted gold by Lorna (who painted everything). One window facing west — the fog, always the fog, and on clear days, a sliver of ocean.

Her guitar: the classical from Manila, hanging on the wall on a proper hanger (Rafe installed it). Below it, a poster of PJ Harvey (Lorna's choice — "women who make noise, anak") and a hand-drawn lyric sheet in Ami's own handwriting — a song she wrote at nine, mostly nonsense words, but the chord progression was real.

The bookshelves: manga (Sailor Moon, Naruto), novels (Madeleine L'Engle, Tamora Pierce), and a shelf of her mother's art books that Lorna rotated — "your visual education." At ten, the book was Frida Kahlo. Ami stared at the self-portraits for hours. Not understanding all of it. Understanding the feeling: *I will put my insides on the outside and you will look.*

A rug: hand-woven, brought back from Manila, colors faded from sun. Ami sat on this rug to play guitar. Cross-legged. The rug's texture under her knees a signal: *this is where music happens.*

Under the bed: nothing scary. Except — sometimes — a cold spot. A place where the air was different. Where "Keys" lingered. She'd feel it at night: a patch of cold near the foot of the bed, the faint smell of cigarette smoke (Keys smoked when he was alive — the residue followed him into death). At ten, this didn't scare her anymore. It was just her room. Her room with a ghost in it. Normal.

*(She told her school counselor once — age 9 — that someone played piano in her room at night. The counselor called Lorna. Lorna asked Ami careful questions. Decided it was "an imaginary friend — artistic child, vivid imagination." This was wrong. But it was also the last time Ami told any adult the truth until she was seventeen.)*

## TEENAGE YEARS (Ages 13-18)

### Summer Jobs & Activities

**Age 13 (Summer 2014)**: Nothing formal. The summer after the piano incident with Ashley. Ami retreated — spent June and July in the garage, playing guitar alone, learning every PJ Harvey song by ear. Lorna signed her up for an art camp but she dropped out after day three ("too many people, too many feelings").

**Age 14 (Summer 2015)**: Volunteered at Lorna's gallery — hanging shows, writing placards, sitting in the empty gallery during slow hours. Learned to be alone in large spaces. Learned that silence isn't empty — it has texture.

**Age 15 (Summer 2016)**: Got a job at a record store on Haight Street (lied about her age — said sixteen). $9/hour. Alphabetizing vinyl. Listening to whatever the owner put on the store system. Discovering Cocteau Twins, Deerhunter, My Bloody Valentine — the shoegaze and dreampop that would become her sonic palette. The owner, a guy named Pete with a gray ponytail, let her borrow records. "You've got ears, kid."

**Age 16 (Summer 2017)**: Rafe got her a gig playing background guitar at a restaurant in the Mission — acoustic covers, two hours on Friday nights, tips in a jar. Her first paid music work. She made $60-$100 a night. She played "Fade Into You" by Mazzy Star every set and meant every note like a prayer.

### A Vacation Where Something Changed

**Santa Cruz — Age 15 (July 2016)**

Lorna rented a house for a week. Just the family. A small cottage near the boardwalk, the kind with salt-damaged wood and windows that stuck. The purpose: "We need a reset. All of us." (It had been a hard year — Ami's isolation at school deepening, Rafe's session work drying up, Lorna's gallery losing funding.)

Day three: Ami walked to the beach at sunset. Alone. She brought her guitar — the acoustic Rafe had given her for her fourteenth birthday, a Martin 000-15M, the nicest thing she'd ever owned. She sat on the sand near the water and played.

She wasn't playing for anyone. The beach was nearly empty — a couple far down, a guy with a dog. She played with her eyes closed. Something in E minor. Slow. The waves underneath.

And then the entities came. Not one — several. Drawn by her playing, by the wide-open space, by the liminal zone between land and water where the veil was thin. She felt them arrive: cold spots in the summer air, pressure behind her eyes, the harmonic undertones that appeared when she played alone.

She kept her eyes closed — there was never anything to see, for her; there was only ever sound — but she HEARD them. Three distinct frequencies joining her. A bass note (The Roadie, steady and low). A piano arpeggio (Keys, always Keys). And something new — a voice. A female voice. Singing a melody over Ami's chords. High. Clear. Sad. A voice she'd never heard before.

She played for forty minutes. The longest she'd ever kept the connection. And when she stopped — when the entities faded back — she realized: a man had been standing fifty feet away, watching. He had tears on his face.

He approached. Middle-aged. Ordinary. "I'm sorry," he said. "I didn't mean to — I just — who was that singing?"

Ami's blood went cold. "What?"

"The woman. Singing. I couldn't see anyone but I heard — was it a recording?"

"...Yes. A recording. I play along to tracks sometimes."

He nodded. Wiped his face. "It sounded like my mother. She died last year. I know that's crazy. I'm sorry." He walked away.

Ami sat on the beach until dark. Processing. Someone else had HEARD them. Not just her. The entities, when she played, became audible to others. Her music was a channel. A doorway. And she didn't know if that was a gift or something much more dangerous.

She never told her parents about this. She wrote it in a journal that lives in a box under her bed, written in a code of guitar tabs and symbols.

### Friend Group Dynamics in Summer

Ami didn't have a "group." She had Dex. Dexter Armitage — the one kid at her school who also ate lunch alone, who also stared at walls, who also heard things that weren't there (though in his case it was severe ADHD, not dead musicians). They bonded over being the weird ones. Not romantically — never romantically. Just two misfits who understood mutual invisibility.

Summers with Dex: taking BART into the city and walking the Mission murals. Sitting in Dolores Park and people-watching. Going to Amoeba Music and spending two hours pulling records they couldn't afford. Dex talking (always talking — his brain never stopped) and Ami listening (always listening — her ears never closed).

Their tradition: once each summer, they'd ride the N-Judah to Ocean Beach at sunset. They'd sit on the seawall, not talking, and watch the sky change colors. Dex would time how long the green flash lasted (he was convinced it was real; Ami never saw it). They'd share a burrito from the place on Irving. Walk home in the dark.

It wasn't exciting. It wasn't cinematic. It was just — someone who showed up. Someone who didn't flinch when Ami said weird things. Someone who, when she said "I can hear something in this song that shouldn't be there," just said "describe it" instead of "you're crazy."

Dex moved to Portland junior year. They texted for a while. Then didn't. But she still thinks of him at sunsets on Ocean Beach.

### A Road Trip or Concert

**My Bloody Valentine Reunion Show — Age 17 (2018)**

Rafe drove her. Five hours to LA (they played the Palladium). He didn't even like My Bloody Valentine — "it's noise, anak, but it's YOUR noise, so let's go." They left at 6 AM. Stopped at In-N-Out in Kettleman City. Ami ate a Double-Double in the passenger seat and felt the specific freedom of being seventeen and heading toward a sound that understood her.

The show. The wall of noise. The moment during "You Made Me Realise" when the feedback extended for twenty-three straight minutes — twenty-three minutes of pure, undifferentiated SOUND — and everyone in the venue was overwhelmed, covering their ears, some leaving —

But Ami stood in the middle of it with her eyes closed and tears running and heard EVERYTHING inside the noise. Every frequency. Every layer. The overtones. The harmonics. The spaces between. And underneath it all, very faintly, an entity she'd never felt before — something that lived IN distortion, that existed only when sound reached a certain threshold of chaos. It didn't have a form. It was just... there. In the frequencies nobody else could parse.

She stood for twenty-three minutes in a wall of sound and felt less alone than she had in years.

In the car after, her ears ringing, Rafe said: "You had a moment in there, huh."

"Yeah."

"Was it a good one?"

"...I think I know what my music needs to sound like now."

He nodded. Didn't ask more. Drove them home through the 5 AM Central Valley dark, and Ami slept against the window and dreamed of frequencies.

### First Time Staying Out All Night

**Age 16 — A House Show in Oakland (2017)**

Dex knew someone who knew someone. A DIY show in a basement in West Oakland. Four bands. No permits. A generator and a PA system that someone "borrowed." The kind of show that exists for one night and is never repeated.

Rafe would have said no. So Ami told Lorna she was sleeping at Dex's. (Lorna suspected. Lorna chose not to verify. A gift.)

The show was loud, sweaty, and alive. Bands she'd never heard — queer punks, a noise duo, an ambient project that used only tape loops. She stood at the back and absorbed it all. The crowd was older (mostly 20s) and nobody cared that she was sixteen. She was just another body in the dark.

After the show (1 AM), people migrated to the backyard. Someone had a fire pit. Someone had cheap wine. Someone had a guitar — a battered Strat with two dead strings. It got passed around. When it reached Ami, she played — just noodling, nothing structured — and the backyard went quiet. Not because she was doing anything flashy. Because the notes she played had WEIGHT. Resonance that a four-string guitar shouldn't have.

A woman (early twenties, shaved head, tattoo of a moth on her neck) said: "What the fuck. That sounded like six guitars."

Ami stopped playing. "Sorry."

"Don't be SORRY. That was beautiful. Who are you?"

She walked home at 4 AM. Through Oakland, across the Bay Bridge pedestrian path, into Daly City. Three hours of walking. She wasn't tired. She was full of something — a feeling that somewhere in the world there were people who heard music the way she did, and she just had to find them.

## EARLY ADULTHOOD (Ages 18-24, Pre-Band)

### Where She Lived

At eighteen, Ami didn't leave the Bay Area. She couldn't afford to. Applied to SF State, got in (music program, partial scholarship from the Latino/Filipino community fund). Lived at home for the first two years — the Daly City house, the garret room, the fog.

At twenty, she moved into a house in the Outer Sunset with three roommates she found on Craigslist. A Victorian with bad plumbing and excellent acoustics (high ceilings, hardwood floors, the sound bounced beautifully). Her room was tiny — mattress on the floor, guitar on the wall, amp in the corner, nothing else. She liked the emptiness. Less interference with the sounds.

The Sunset was her place: foggy, quiet, close to the ocean. She could walk to Ocean Beach in twelve minutes. She did it daily. The sound of the Pacific became her tuning fork — something to return to when the entities got too loud or her own head got too noisy.

### A Trip Alone

**Joshua Tree, solo — Age 21 (2022)**

Three days in the desert. Alone. She rented a car (her first time renting a car — the Budget counter guy looked skeptical when she said "just me"). Drove seven hours south. Checked into a $40/night motel in Twentynine Palms. The room smelled like dust and old sunscreen.

The purpose: silence. The desert has a specific silence that the Bay Area doesn't — no ocean, no traffic, no fog-horn. Just space and wind and the creaking of Joshua trees.

She brought her guitar and a notebook. Played under the stars at night — the acoustic rang differently in dry air (brighter, sharper). She recorded voice memos on her phone: ideas, fragments, melodies that came from the silence.

On the second night, sitting in the desert at 2 AM, she played a chord and heard NOTHING respond. No entities. No phantom harmonies. No cold spots. Complete silence except her own guitar.

She panicked. Stopped playing. Listened. Nothing. Nothing. Nothing.

She played again. A full song. Poured herself into it. And still — nothing came through. She was alone. Actually alone. For the first time in fourteen years.

She sat in the desert and cried. Not because the entities were gone (they weren't — she felt them when she got home, immediately, swarming back). But because she realized: she didn't know who she was WITHOUT them. She didn't know what her music sounded like WITHOUT the dead harmonizing. She'd never heard only herself.

She drove home a day early. Called Rafe from Barstow. "Dad. What does my guitar sound like to YOU? Without anything else?" He was confused. "It sounds like you, anak." She cried on the phone and he didn't know why and he just kept saying "you sound like yourself" and she needed that more than he could know.

### Seasonal Rhythms

**Winter**: The Bay Area doesn't really have winter but the closest thing — December, January — the rain. The specific sound of rain on the Sunset District's flat roofs. Ami was most creative in rain. Something about water — it muted the entities slightly, made her own voice louder in the mix.

**Spring**: Restless. Itchy. The fog lifting earlier each day, the sun appearing, and Ami feeling exposed. Spring was too bright. Too visible. She played more shows in spring to counteract it — the dark of venues as shelter.

**Summer**: The fog returned (Karl — everyone in SF calls the fog Karl). Ami came alive in summer fog. Playing on the beach in June while tourists shivered and complained. Feeling at home in the gray.

**Fall**: Melancholy. Beautiful melancholy. The kind that makes good music. Ami wrote her best material in October. The entities were most active in autumn — something about the thinning of the veil, the shortening days, the world going quiet.

### The Summer at Her Lowest

**Summer 2020 — Age 19**

The pandemic summer. Locked in the Daly City house with her parents. The live music world dead overnight. Her scholarship reduced. Her part-time job at the record store gone (the store closed permanently in April).

But worse than the logistics: the entities were LOUD that summer. Trapped inside with her, no venues to channel them into, no audiences to absorb the output. They crowded her room. Keys playing endlessly — the same arpeggios, over and over, like a broken record. The Preacher humming in harmonics that gave her headaches. Something new — a presence without a name — that stood in the corner and watched.

She stopped sleeping properly. Played guitar at 3 AM with headphones on (respecting Rafe and Lorna through the walls). Wrote forty-three songs in two months. Most of them were bad. Some of them were terrifying — melodies that didn't come from her, lyrics in languages she didn't speak. She deleted those.

By August, she was barely eating. Lorna found her sitting in the garage at 6 AM, guitar across her lap, staring at nothing, eyes red. "How long have you been here?" "I don't know." "Did you sleep?" "I don't know."

Lorna held her. Didn't ask what was wrong (how do you explain "the dead musicians in my room won't shut up" to your mother?). Just held her. And Ami let herself be held, feeling small and human and overwhelmed.

Rafe started sleeping in the garage with her. Brought his guitar. If the entities wanted to make noise, they'd have to compete with HIM. He played — loudly, physically, grounding — and somehow, his playing pushed them back. Not because he had any ability. Because his music was so PRESENT, so bodily, so alive that the dead couldn't harmonize with it. He was too real for them.

It worked. Slowly. By September, Ami was sleeping again. Not well. But sleeping.

### A Random Tuesday (Age 22)

**Tuesday, November 15, 2022**

8:30 AM — Waking to the sound of her roommate Maya's alarm (three rooms away but the Victorian walls are thin — everything is thin here, the walls between rooms, the walls between worlds). Gray light. Always gray in the Sunset.

8:45 AM — Making coffee in the shared kitchen. The French press that belongs to nobody and everybody. The last of the good beans (someone needs to buy more — not it). Standing at the counter in boxers and an oversized Cocteau Twins t-shirt, scrolling her phone. A text from Lorna: a photo of a new painting, no words, just the image. Ami sends back three fire emojis.

9:30 AM — Walking to campus. SF State. The fog so thick today the buildings appear one at a time, like a video game rendering. Headphones in — listening to a Mdou Moctar record. The Tuareg guitar lines impossible and fluid. She tries to follow the fingering in her mind.

11:00 AM — Music Theory III. The professor is talking about modes. Ami already knows this — Rafe taught her modes at thirteen. She doodles in her notebook. A sketch of a stage setup. Speaker placement. Where she'd put the monitors if she had her own show.

1:00 PM — Lunch. A banh mi from the cart on Holloway Ave. Eating it on the grass outside the library. A squirrel approaches with excessive confidence. She gives it a piece of bread. It takes it and doesn't leave. "That's all you're getting," she tells it. It stays. She names it Martin (after the guitar brand). They eat lunch together.

2:30 PM — Practice room. SF State has four of them and they're always booked but Ami knows the sign-up girl and gets room 3 (the one with the best acoustics — tiled floor, no carpet). She plays for ninety minutes. Working on a song she's been building for weeks. The chorus isn't right. She plays it forty ways. None of them work.

On attempt forty-one, she plays the chorus with a rest in the middle — a full beat of silence between the third and fourth measure. And in that silence, she hears Keys finish the phrase for her. A ghost note. A phantom completion. And it's PERFECT.

She records the idea on her phone. Pretends the ghost note was her own invention. Feels slightly guilty. Writes "Keys helped" in her notebook margin in tiny letters.

4:30 PM — Walking to the beach. Ocean Beach. End of Judah Street. The Pacific enormous and gray and patient. She stands at the water's edge and lets the sound wash over her — the white noise of waves, the layered frequencies. She closes her eyes and listens to the INSIDE of the ocean sound. The individual droplets. The undertones. The low-frequency rumble that most people can't hear but she feels in her teeth.

6:00 PM — Home. The house smells like someone is making curry (her roommate Eli, probably). She takes her boots off at the door. Goes to her room. Sits on the bed. Stares at the guitar on the wall.

7:00 PM — Dinner with roommates. Four of them at the small kitchen table (legs uneven — a folded napkin under one). Eli's curry. Maya's rice. Ami contributed a bag of chips and nobody complains. They talk about nothing — a show Eli saw, Maya's terrible boss, the weird noise the building made last night (that was Ami's entities, but she says "old pipes" and everyone agrees).

9:00 PM — Her room. Playing guitar quietly. No amp — acoustic only. Something soft. Something for herself. The entities are gentle tonight — Keys hovering at the edge of her awareness, not intruding, just present. Like a cat in the room. She plays until her fingers hurt and then stops.

11:00 PM — Bed. The fog pressing against her window like something trying to get in. The distant foghorn from the Golden Gate. Once. Twice. A metronome for the whole city.

She sleeps.

## FAMILY DYNAMICS

### A Family Dinner Scene (Age 20, Thanksgiving Weekend 2021)

The Daly City house. Not Thanksgiving itself (the Solanas aren't big on American holidays — they do Noche Buena) but the Saturday after. Rafe made sinigang (tamarind soup, sour and warming) because the fog was especially thick and Lorna said "I need something that fights back against this cold."

The three of them at the small kitchen table. The table that's been there since Ami was born — oval, wooden, a burn mark on one corner from when Rafe set a hot pan down in 2008 and Lorna didn't speak to him for an hour.

LORNA: I sold two pieces this week.

RAFE: The big ones? The series?

LORNA: The small ones. But still. Someone WANTED them.

MIRA: Which ones?

LORNA: The fog studies. The blue ones.

MIRA: I loved those.

LORNA: I know. That's why I didn't want to sell them. But we need a new water heater.

*(The Solanas live like this — art against practicality. A constant negotiation between beauty and plumbing.)*

RAFE: I got three new students this month. All beginners. I'll be teaching "Wonderwall" by Christmas.

MIRA: Dad. No.

RAFE: "Anyway, here's Wonderwall."

MIRA: I'll disown you.

RAFE: *(grinning)* You can't. I taught you E minor.

LORNA: He has a point, anak.

Ami eats her sinigang and watches her parents — the way Rafe automatically refills Lorna's glass, the way Lorna puts her hand on Rafe's arm when she laughs. Twenty-three years married and they still LEAN toward each other. Still seek contact.

And she thinks (not for the first time): *will I ever have this? Can I have this? With the things I carry? With the dead always in the room?*

She finishes her soup and asks for seconds and doesn't voice the thought. Some heaviness you carry alone.

### How Their Family Handles Conflict

Loudly, but with love. The Solanas argue like musicians — it gets LOUD and then someone makes a joke and the tension breaks. Nobody holds grudges. Nobody goes silent. The Solana way is: say the thing, feel the feeling, resolve it before bed.

Lorna and Rafe's fights sound like this: rapid-fire, half in English, half in Tagalog, gesturing, occasionally something gets slammed (a cabinet, not a fist — never a fist). And then, almost always within the hour: "I'm sorry." "I'm sorry too." "You want tea?" "Yes."

Ami's conflicts with her parents were different — because hers involved things she couldn't explain. The fights about "you're too isolated" and "why won't you see the therapist" and "something is WRONG and you won't tell us" — those couldn't resolve because the truth was unutterable.

The worst: age sixteen. Lorna found Ami's journal — the one with the entity descriptions, the frequency maps, the notes about what she heard in the walls. Lorna read it. Confronted Ami. "Are these REAL? The things in here — are you hearing things? Voices?"

Ami screamed: "YES. And if you send me to a doctor they'll give me pills and the pills will make me DEAF and I'd rather die than be deaf."

Lorna went white. Called Rafe. A family conference at the kitchen table that lasted three hours. Crying from all three. At the end, Rafe said: "We don't understand. But we believe you. And we're not taking you anywhere. Just... let us IN. Please."

They compromised: Ami would tell them if anything scared her. They would not force medication or therapy unless she wanted it. And every Sunday, Rafe would play guitar with her in the garage and she could tell him what she heard — or not. No pressure.

It wasn't perfect. But it was enough.

### A Moment She Saw Her Parent as a Person

**Age 21.** She came home from a gig (2 AM, a bar in the Tenderloin, $60 and a free beer). The Daly City house was dark. She let herself in quietly. Headed upstairs.

Stopped. A sound from the garage. Muffled. Electric guitar, very low volume. At 2 AM.

She opened the garage door. And there was Rafe — in his pajamas, barefoot, playing his Telecaster through the amp at whisper-volume. Playing something she'd never heard before. Not a cover. Not a lesson exercise. Something HIS. Original. Sad and beautiful and complex.

He didn't see her. She stood in the doorway and watched her father play music for nobody. Music that wasn't for students or wedding bands or his daughter's education. Music that was just for HIM. Because he was a musician before he was a father. Before he was a teacher. Before he was anything else. He was someone who NEEDED to play the way other people needed to breathe.

And she saw: he'd given up so much of that. Traded his own music for hers. Session work for guitar lessons. His career for stability. Not resentfully — lovingly. But the thing he'd surrendered was still alive in him, coming out at 2 AM in an empty garage.

She stepped back. Closed the door softly. Went upstairs. Didn't mention it. But the next morning at breakfast, she said: "Dad, you should record something. Your own stuff. I'll engineer it."

He looked startled. Then moved. "Maybe someday."

"Not someday. Soon."

He smiled into his coffee. And she saw him — not as "Dad" but as Rafael Solana, a man who loved sound, who had sacrificed his own music to make sure his daughter's was heard. And she thought: *I won't let that be for nothing.*

### A Perfect Day (Hour by Hour)

**A Saturday in June. Age 23.**

7:00 AM — Waking. The fog so thick the window looks painted white. Lying still. Hearing the foghorn. Once. Twice. Feeling deeply, specifically safe.

8:00 AM — Walking to the corner store (in sweats, flip-flops — she's not going far). Buying: a pastry, orange juice, and a newspaper she won't read but likes the ritual of buying. The Filipino woman at the register knows her. "Morning, anak." (She's not her anak. But the word still works.)

9:00 AM — The ocean. Walking to the beach with her coffee. The sand cold under her bare feet. Nobody else out — too early, too foggy. Just her and the Pacific. She stands at the edge and lets one wave break over her ankles. Gasps. Laughs at herself.

10:00 AM — Playing guitar on the seawall. Not for an audience. Barely for herself. Just — placing notes into the fog. Letting them disappear. This is meditation for Ami: playing without intention. Letting her hands go where they want. Today they want minor chords and fingerpicking. Arpeggios that climb and fall. Simple. Human.

12:00 PM — Home. Making rice in the rice cooker (the only appliance she uses correctly, every time). Eating rice with egg and soy sauce and hot sauce — the broke musician meal, the one that's actually perfect.

1:30 PM — Walking to the record store on Irving Street (not where she worked — a different one). Spending an hour flipping through bins. Finding: a Broadcast LP she's been looking for months. $22. Worth it. Playing it the moment she gets home, lying on her bed, letting the analog warmth fill her room.

4:00 PM — Calling Dex (they've reconnected — he's still in Portland but they talk again). Forty-five minutes about nothing. A show he saw. A weird thing she heard on the beach. His new job at a bike shop. Her new song that won't finish itself. Normal human contact. Easy.

5:30 PM — The golden hour (even in fog, it happens — the light turning amber behind the gray, the world briefly warm). Sitting at her window. Watching the fog thin. Playing a single chord and letting it ring until it dies naturally. The sustain lasting eleven seconds.

7:00 PM — Cooking dinner for herself and Maya. Garlic rice and adobo (Lorna's recipe, texted in fragments over the years: "brown the chicken FIRST anak, the caramelization IS the flavor"). They eat on the floor of Maya's room because the kitchen table has laundry on it.

9:00 PM — Playing for Rafe. The Sunday night phone call, moved to Saturday this week. She plays him the new song — still unfinished — through the phone speaker. He listens. Says: "The bridge needs one more measure of rest. Let it breathe." She plays it with the rest. He's right.

10:30 PM — Bath (the Victorian has a claw-foot tub — the house's one luxury). Hot water. No music. Just silence and steam. Feeling her muscles release. Feeling the day's weight lift.

11:30 PM — Bed. The fog back outside (it always comes back). Keys is there — a faint cold spot near the foot of the bed, a barely-audible arpeggio hovering in the air. She whispers: "Goodnight." The arpeggio resolves. Silence. Sleep.

### Comfort Routine When Life Is Hard

1. The ocean: walking to the water's edge and standing there until the cold numbs her feet. The numbness as relief — feeling nothing physical so the emotional pain is all she has to carry.
2. Rafe's guitar tone: calling him and asking "play me something." He always does. No questions. Just music through a phone speaker. His Telecaster clean-tone warm as a blanket.
3. Cooking rice: the act of washing it (rinse, drain, rinse, drain — four times) is meditative. The sound of the rice cooker clicking from "cook" to "warm." The steam releasing. Something completed. Something done right.
4. Driving Highway 1: north, toward Stinson Beach. Windows down despite the cold. Music loud — specifically: Grouper, *Dragging a Dead Deer Up a Hill*. The most beautiful album she knows. The one that sounds like the inside of grief.
5. Lying on the floor: not the bed. The FLOOR. Hard surface. Grounding. Feeling the building's vibration through her spine. The house's hum. The living resonance of a structure that's stood for a hundred years. It reminds her that solid things exist.

### A Recurring Dream

She's in a concert hall. Empty. Thousands of seats, all unoccupied. She's on stage alone, holding her guitar. And in every seat sits an entity — a dead musician, a presence, a thing-that-once-played. Thousands of them. Watching her.

She starts to play. And one by one, they stand. And walk toward the stage. And as they reach her, they dissolve INTO her guitar — absorbed, consumed, becoming part of the sound. Until the hall is empty and her guitar holds every dead musician who ever existed and the sound it makes is so enormous it cracks the walls.

She wakes from this dream sweating. Heart racing. But not afraid. Determined. Like it's a prophecy, not a nightmare.

She's had it since she was fifteen. It comes more often the older she gets.

### The Smell/Sound That Puts Her Back in Childhood

**Smell**: Adobo. Specifically the moment the vinegar and soy sauce reduce and the kitchen fills with that sweet-salt-sour cloud. Lorna's kitchen. Every Sunday. The smell of being fed by someone who loves you.

**Sound**: The garage door opening. The specific grind-and-rattle of the Daly City garage door rolling up — which meant: someone is about to make music. It could be Rafe going in to practice, or Ami going in to play, or both of them going in together. That sound was the prelude. The count-in before the song.
