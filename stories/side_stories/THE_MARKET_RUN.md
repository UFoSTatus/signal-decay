## SATURDAY, 8:40 AM

The farmer's market set up every Saturday in the parking lot of the old Lutheran church, the one with the roof that always looked like it was thinking about leaving, and Zara Kovač and her sister Lena got there at 8:40 because the good tomatoes were gone by nine and Zara had strong feelings about tomatoes.

It was September and the morning had that specific edge to it — not cold, but the *memory* of cold, the first Saturday of the year where you thought about a jacket and then didn't bring one and were a little wrong about it. The light came in low and gold across the parking lot and made everything look better than it was: the dented folding tables, the hand-markered signs, the coffee guy's janky espresso cart wheezing steam into the chill. It smelled like wet cardboard and cut basil and somebody's breakfast sausage and, under all of it, the particular green smell of just-picked things still cold from the ground.

Lena was seventeen and had opinions about being awake at 8:40 on a Saturday. She communicated these opinions through the medium of her entire body — the hood up, the hands jammed in the pouch pocket, the walk that was somehow both slouching and dramatic. "I don't understand," she said, "how you're a *person* right now. It's the middle of the night."

"It's almost nine."

"The middle of the night," Lena said, with finality.

Zara handed her the coffee she'd bought without asking — oat milk, one sugar, because Zara knew — and Lena took it without thanking her, which was its own kind of thank you, the kind between sisters who don't need the words. She wrapped both hands around the cup. Some of the drama left her body. "Okay," she admitted, to the coffee, not to Zara. "Okay, this was a good idea."

---

## THE TOMATO GUY

The tomato guy's name was Bill and he was maybe seventy and he'd been coming to this market since before Lena was born, and he *loved* Zara, in the uncomplicated way that old vendors love the young person who actually cares about the produce. He'd started setting a box aside for her — the ugly heirlooms, the split ones, the ones too weird-looking to sell to normal people, the ones that tasted like something.

"There she is," Bill said, when he saw her. "There's my girl. I got you the Cherokee Purples, they came in ugly this week, ugly as sin, you're gonna love 'em." He held one up. It was, in fact, ugly — a lumpen, shoulder-cracked, brownish-purple thing that looked less like a tomato and more like a small bruised heart. "Look at that," Bill said, with total love. "Isn't that horrible? Taste that later and tell me that's not the best thing you ever ate."

Zara took it in both hands and did the thing she did with produce, the thing Lena had watched her do her whole life and never thought was strange because it was just *Zara* — she held it close, almost to her face, and went still for a second, and something in her expression softened all the way down to the bottom. Bill thought she was smelling it. Lena thought she was being weird. Neither of them was exactly wrong.

"It's perfect," Zara said quietly, and meant it more than Bill could know.

Because here is the only not-quite-ordinary thing in this ordinary morning, and it passed in a second and no one caught it: when Zara held a growing thing — really held it, a thing pulled from real dirt and ripened in real sun — she could feel, under the smell and the weight, the faint *rightness* of it. The frequency of a thing that had grown the way growing was supposed to go. Bill's ugly tomato hummed, very faintly, at a note she recognized from a life she couldn't talk about — the note of *life doing what it was tuned to do.* Most food in the world, to Zara, was a photocopy of a photocopy, flat and quiet. Bill's ugly tomatoes rang like a struck string.

She didn't say any of that. She said, "Give me the whole box, Bill. And whatever else came in ugly."

"That's my girl," said Bill.

## THE REST OF IT

They wandered. That was the actual point of the market, the wandering — the produce was the excuse. Lena thawed the way seventeen-year-olds thaw, in increments, over caffeine, until she was actually talking, telling Zara a long involved story about a group project and a girl named Priya who was "genuinely, clinically insane, Zar, I'm not exaggerating," and Zara listened the way she listened to everything, completely, because listening to Lena was the one thing in her life that cost her nothing and gave her everything.

They bought too much. That was also tradition. A bag of apples that were "for baking" and would be eaten raw within two days. A bunch of dahlias the color of a sunset having a good day, which Lena picked out and pretended she was buying for the kitchen but was really buying for her own room. A wedge of the sharp cheese from the cheese lady. A loaf of the sourdough that was still warm, that they tore the end off of and split walking to the car, and it was so good that Lena stopped mid-sentence and said "*oh my god*" with her mouth full, and Zara laughed — the real laugh, the one almost no one got to see, the one that had no performance in it at all.

At the flower stand, while Lena debated dahlias, Zara stood a half-step back and watched her sister in the low gold light — seventeen, alive, unaware, gorgeous in a hoodie and a bad mood improving — and felt the thing she always felt and never said. Not the frequency thing. The other thing. The plain enormous human thing. *I would take apart the sky for you. I have arranged my entire existence around keeping you safe and you will never know it and that's how it should be. Buy the flowers. Buy all the flowers. I've got you. I've always got you.*

"This one," Lena decided, holding up the sunset dahlias.

"Get two," Zara said.

"They're expensive."

"Get two," Zara said, and paid before Lena could argue, and Lena rolled her eyes and carried both bunches like she was annoyed about it, and held them very carefully the whole way to the car.

---

## THE DRIVE HOME

They loaded the trunk — the ugly tomatoes, the baking apples, the cheese, the warm bread's heel, two bunches of dahlias riding shotgun in the cupholders because there was nowhere else safe. The morning had lost its edge; it was just a nice day now, the gold gone ordinary, the parking lot filling up with later, less serious shoppers who would not get the good tomatoes.

In the car Lena put her feet on the dash, which she wasn't supposed to do, and Zara let her, because some rules aren't worth it. Lena queued up something on her phone through the aux cord, a song Zara didn't know, and turned it up, and they drove home through the ordinary Saturday town with the windows cracked and the September air coming in and two bunches of dahlias glowing in the cupholders.

And Lena — full of coffee and bread and the specific contentment of a good market run — did the thing she did sometimes without knowing she did it. She hummed. Under the song, off to the side of it, a low little tuneless hum, the kind of sound a person makes when they're happy and don't notice they're making it.

Zara's hands stayed easy on the wheel. Her face didn't change. But she heard it — the way she always heard it — the low note under her sister's breath, the one Lena had hummed in her sleep every night of her life without ever being taught, the one that was the exact complement to Zara's own. In the closed car, with the windows cracked and the good tomatoes in the trunk, the two notes — Lena's happy little unconscious hum and the sixty-two-cycle floor that was always, quietly, underneath everything Zara was — met in the air and made, for just a second, a complete thing. A chord with no gaps. A silence-less signal.

Zara didn't say anything. She just drove her sister home through the gold September morning, two bunches of dahlias between them, a chord she'd waited her whole life to hear humming quietly in a Honda that needed an oil change.

It was the best morning she'd had in weeks. It was completely ordinary. Those were, she had learned, very often the same thing.

*No powers. No Legacy. No sky splitting open. Two sisters, a farmer's market, ugly tomatoes, warm bread, two bunches of dahlias, a hum in a car. The only trace of the cosmic is the one Zara never mentions and Lena never knows — that when her happy little tuneless hum meets her sister's, they make, for a second, a complete chord. The band isn't only made of the strange nights. It's made of mornings like this. Especially mornings like this.*
