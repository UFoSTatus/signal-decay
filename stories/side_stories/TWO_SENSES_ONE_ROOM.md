# TWO SENSES, ONE ROOM

*Side-Story · Ami Solana × Mira Chen · ~2026, around the band's formation*
*Register: kindred-recognition — the mirror meeting*
*Created: 2026-09-23*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. Two young women who have each spent their whole lives perceiving a layer of the world nobody else can — one by sight, one by sound — end up in the same small room. Neither has ever met anyone else who knows. This is the ninety seconds where that changes.*

---

## THE ROOM ABOVE THE FREQUENCY

It was a guitar-swap meetup, which is the most ordinary reason two guitarists ever end up in a room together. Someone on a Bay Area players' forum had a '78 Telecaster neck for trade; someone else had a rehearsal room booked above The Frequency for the afternoon and said *just come by, I'll be here till five.* So the room filled, slowly, with the specific population of a weekday guitar swap — men mostly, a couple of women, everyone handling everyone else's instruments with the exaggerated care of people touching something they can't afford to break.

Mira Chen came for the neck. Amihan Solana came because Rafe had texted her the listing — *anak, that's the neck you wanted, go* — and because she had a free afternoon and a car.

They didn't notice each other at first. Why would they. Two guitarists in a room full of guitarists.

Then someone plugged a demo amp into the wall, and the room started to hum.

---

## WHAT MIRA SAW

Mira saw it before she heard it, the way she always did.

The amp came on and threw a color — that flat fluorescent buzz that every cheap amp makes, the 60-cycle hum, and to Mira it was a specific dull mustard-yellow that filled the lower third of the room like water. She'd seen it ten thousand times. Every amp did it. She'd learned to read past it the way you learn to read past a smudge on your glasses.

But there was a woman standing near the amp — dark hair, a canvas jacket, holding a classical guitar she clearly hadn't come to trade, just to hold — and when the mustard hum rose, the woman *flinched.* Not a startle. A wince. The specific wince of someone hearing a wrong note. Her head tilted a few degrees, the way a dog's does. And her hand, the one not holding the guitar, made a small motion at her side — fingers spreading, then closing — like she was pressing down on something in the air.

Mira knew that motion. She made it herself, with color. *Turning down a channel nobody else can see.*

She watched the woman, and the mustard-yellow hum in the room did something it had never done in ten thousand amps: near the woman, it went *thin.* Like she was absorbing it. Like the color was leaning toward her.

---

## WHAT AMI HEARD

Ami heard the amp come on and it was, as always, unbearable for exactly one second.

The 60-cycle hum — everyone in the room registered it as background, the sound you stop hearing the moment it starts. Ami had never once stopped hearing it. It arrived in her the way a smell arrives, whole and insistent: a flat, buzzing B-natural-ish drone, slightly sharp, sitting under everything like a floor she hadn't agreed to stand on. She did the thing she did — the small pressure with her hand, an old unconscious habit, as if she could physically push a fader down — and breathed through it until it became bearable. Furniture. Just furniture.

But there was something *else* in the room. Under the hum. A second thing, and it wasn't coming from the amp.

It was coming from a person. A woman across the room, burgundy in her hair when she moved her head, silver rings on her left hand. And the woman wasn't making a sound — she was just standing there holding a strap, looking at the air near the ceiling — but Ami could *hear her.* A tone. Faint, high, clear, sitting a perfect octave-and-a-third above the amp's ugly drone, and utterly steady. A person-shaped note. Ami had heard rooms hum. She had heard the ocean and kelp tanks and the karaoke ghost of Manila. She had never once heard a *person* ring like a struck glass.

She stood very still, the way she'd stood in the Yosemite meadow at six, and she thought the thing she'd thought then, the thing she'd learned never to say out loud:

*There's someone here.*

---

## THE NINETY SECONDS

They looked at each other at the same time.

This is the part that matters, the part that's the whole story. Two people who had each spent a lifetime performing normal — who had each, separately, learned by age twelve that you do not tell anyone that the room has colors, that the amp has a wrong note, that a person can *ring* — looked across a crowded rehearsal room and caught the other one *doing it.* The tilt. The hand. The listening-to-nothing. The looking-at-nothing.

Mira crossed the room. She didn't decide to; she was just moving, the neck she'd come for forgotten on a chair.

"Hey," Mira said. And then, because thirty years of caution deserted her all at once, because she had never in her life met someone she suspected might *know*, she said the reckless thing, the tell, the thing that would sound insane to anyone who wasn't: "That amp. The hum. What — what note is that, to you?"

Ami's face did something complicated. Hope and fear are close cousins and they arrived together. Because that was not a normal question. That was a question with a floor under it. She answered carefully, the way you put your weight on ice: "B. A little sharp. Sits right —" she touched her sternum "— here."

Mira's breath went out of her.

"It's yellow," Mira said. "To me. It's — the hum is mustard-yellow. It fills the room up to about here." She held her hand at hip height. "And you go —" she pointed, helpless, at Ami "— when you stand near it, it goes *thin.* The color leans toward you. Like you're pulling it in."

"I *am* pulling it in," Ami said, and her voice cracked on the *am,* because she had never once said it and had it *land.* "I push it down. So I can stand it. I've done it my whole life. I thought —" she laughed, and there were tears in it, the specific tears of a locked door opening "— I thought everybody could and just didn't. I thought I was the only one who *bothered.*"

"I thought I was crazy," Mira said. "For about ten years I thought I was actually, clinically —" She stopped. Started again. "You *hear* it. I *see* it. It's the same thing. It's the *same thing,* and you hear it."

"You see it," Ami repeated, like a person learning a word in a new language. "You can *see* it."

"The whole layer. All of it. Color."

"I can hear the whole layer. All of it. Sound."

And they stood there, in a room full of guitarists arguing about a Telecaster neck, two women who had each been the only one their entire lives, looking at each other with the vertigo of the suddenly-not-alone — the same locked room, entered through two different doors, and here at last was someone standing in it who'd come in the other way.

---

## AFTER

They left the swap together. Neither of them got the neck.

They went down the side stair and around to the front of The Frequency and sat on the curb in the Bay Area afternoon and did the thing that neither of them had ever been able to do with another living person: they *compared notes.* Literally. Ami hummed the B-sharp of a passing bus's brakes and Mira told her the color of it (a brown-orange, like rust). Mira pointed at the streetlight just warming up at dusk and told Ami it was about to sing, and three seconds later Ami heard it start — a thin rising whine as the sodium bulb cycled on — and she gasped, because Mira had *called it,* had seen the color arrive before the sound did.

"You're ahead of me," Ami said. "You get it as light. Light's faster than sound. You see it coming."

"You get it *deeper,*" Mira said. "I only get the surface. The color. You get the — the actual frequency. The note. You could *play* it."

"I do play it," Ami said quietly. "It's all I've ever played. The gaps. The stuff underneath. I never knew what I was doing. I just — followed the sound nobody else heard."

Mira looked at her for a long moment. The dusk coming down. The streetlight singing its thin sodium note that only one of them could hear and only the other could see.

"I know some people," Mira said slowly. "A band. They're — we're all a little like this. Different flavors of it. You should —" She stopped, suddenly shy, suddenly aware of how big the thing she was about to offer was. "You're not the only one. That's what I'm trying to say. You've never been the only one. There's a whole — there's *more* of us."

Ami Solana sat on the curb outside The Frequency and cried, a little, in the ordinary way a person cries when a weight they've carried so long they forgot it was a weight is suddenly, gently, lifted off.

"Okay," she said. "Yeah. Okay. Introduce me."

*The mirror meeting. Mira Chen sees the hidden layer as color; Amihan Solana hears it as frequency — and at a nothing-special guitar swap above The Frequency, each catches the other doing the thing they'd both hidden their whole lives. No powers detonate, no Legacy alarm trips; two people who were each "the only one" simply find each other, compare notes on a curb at dusk, and one of them says: you've never been the only one. It sets up everything — Ami toward the band, toward the question of whether she's a sixth frequency or the most fluent human sensitive yet. But tonight it's just two women and a singing streetlight, and the enormous quiet relief of being, at last, believed.*
