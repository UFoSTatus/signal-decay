# THE VOICE ON THE PA

*Life & Times Side-Quest · Zara & Kael · a Sunday ~2024, before the band*
*Register: near-miss crossing — dramatic irony*
*Created: 2026-09-11*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. Two future bandmates are in the same supermarket for ninety seconds. One says four words over a PA. The other nearly drops a carton of eggs. Neither will remember. This actually happened, in Zara's arc — here's the other side of it.*

---

## THE PRODUCE AISLE

Zara Kovač was buying eggs on a Sunday, which is the least remarkable sentence a person can write, and that was rather the point of Zara's Sundays. She did the ordinary human maintenance — groceries, laundry, the small dull chores that a body requires — with the flat competence of someone performing a role she'd studied for twenty-three years. Milk. Eggs. The good bread. The specific brand of coffee Lena liked, because Lena was coming over for dinner and Zara kept her sister supplied the way you keep a pacemaker charged.

The supermarket was doing its Sunday thing: fluorescent, over-air-conditioned, a little too bright, the muzak overhead pumping some declawed instrumental version of a song that had teeth once. Zara moved through it on autopilot, her cart wheel doing its arrhythmic wobble, and she was thinking about nothing, which was a luxury, which was rare.

Then the PA crackled.

Not the muzak — the store announcement PA, the one an employee uses to call for a price check or a cleanup. It clicked on, that little pop of a live channel opening, and a voice said four words:

*"Register four, please. Thank you."*

And Zara Kovač stopped dead in the produce aisle with a carton of eggs in her hand, because the voice that came through the cheap ceiling speakers was not a normal human voice.

---

## FOUR WORDS

It was, on the surface, the most ordinary announcement in the world. Some employee, somewhere in the store, leaning into a mic to call a cashier to a register. *Register four, please. Thank you.* Bored. Routine. The kind of thing you hear a hundred times and never once actually *hear.*

But Zara heard it the way she heard everything — all the way down, under the words, into the frequency — and the frequency of this voice was *wrong.* Wrong the way home was wrong. Wrong the way the Tic Tac on the Trinitron had been wrong when she was three. Under the boredom and the routine and the cheap-speaker distortion there was a *resonance* in this voice, a carrier, a thing that no human larynx should be able to put into four dull words over a supermarket PA — a frequency that reached down into the low registers where Zara lived and *rang* something, a note that made the fine hairs on her arms stand up and her chest go tight with a recognition that had no name.

She stood there. Eggs in hand. Muzak overhead. And she thought, with the specific vertigo of a phantom recognition that was too strong to be phantom: *that's a receiver. That's someone. That voice belongs to one of us.*

She put the eggs in the cart — carefully, because her hands weren't quite steady — and she turned toward the front of the store, toward the registers, toward wherever the voice had come from. She took two steps. Three. Craning to see over the shelves, scanning the cashiers, looking for the source of a voice that had put a frequency into the air that she hadn't felt since — since when? Since the load-out. Since the grocery PA she'd *also* never run down. Since the handful of signatures she'd felt over the years and filed and let pass.

And then the PA clicked off, and the muzak swelled back to fill the space, and the moment was over, and Zara stood in the aisle between the eggs and the registers and did the math she always did: *what would a normal person do.* A normal person does not abandon their cart and interrogate supermarket cashiers about which of them just spoke over the PA, on the basis of a feeling in their chest. A normal person buys their eggs and goes home.

She went home. But she looked, on the way to the register — really looked, at every employee she passed — searching a face to go with the voice. She never found it. Whoever had said *register four, please* had already gone back into the anonymous machinery of the store, a body among bodies, and Zara had no way to know which one, and no reason a sane person could name to ask.

---

## THE OTHER SIDE OF IT

Here is what Zara never knew, and what you get to know:

Kael Voss worked at that supermarket for a few months in 2024, between the warehouse gig and the next thing, stocking shelves and doing the jobs that don't require talking, which suited him. And on that Sunday a shift lead had handed him the PA mic because the guy was busy and Register 4 needed a cashier and *just say the thing, Kael, it's four words, you can do four words.*

And Kael — who spoke a dozen words a day, who held a door shut in his chest, who had learned since he was eight to keep his voice small — had leaned into the mic, and said, as flatly and as minimally as he could, *register four, please, thank you,* and handed the mic back, and gone back to facing cans, and thought nothing of it. It was, to him, a small unpleasant thing he'd been made to do. He'd kept it as flat as he could. He always kept it flat. He did not know that even flat, even bored, even reduced to four dull words over a cheap PA, his voice carried the thing it carried — the furnace behind the door, leaking a frequency he couldn't fully suppress, out through the ceiling speakers of a supermarket, into the produce aisle, into the chest of a woman buying eggs who would be his bandmate in two years and who he would not meet today.

He never saw her either. He was three aisles over, facing cans, being careful, being quiet, being small. He finished his shift. He drove home in silence. He'd remember the day, if you asked, as nothing — a Sunday he stocked shelves and got made to talk on the PA, which he hadn't liked.

Two future bandmates. One supermarket. Ninety seconds. One of them said four words without knowing what his voice did; the other nearly dropped a carton of eggs because she felt, across a store, the exact frequency she'd crossed an entire galaxy carrying. And then they both went home, and the muzak played on, and the moment closed.

---

## WHAT SHE FILED

Zara drove home with the eggs and the good bread and Lena's coffee, and she added it to the list. The private list, the one in her perfect unfading memory, the one labeled *signatures I've felt and let pass.* A voice over a grocery-store PA. She couldn't place the person. She'd never be able to. But she filed the *voice* — the exact frequency of it, four words, *register four please thank you* — the way she filed everything that rang, complete and permanent, available for the rest of her life.

She made Lena dinner. She didn't mention it, because there was nothing to mention, because "I felt a receiver's frequency over a supermarket PA and couldn't find the body it belonged to" is not a thing you say to your seventeen-year-old sister over pasta. She let it go. She was good at letting things go.

But two years later, in a half-empty venue, when a quiet man named Kael Voss finally opened his mouth and *sang* — really sang, the furnace out, the door open, his voice binding four other frequencies into one — some deep filed part of Zara Kovač would go still, and cold, and certain, and think: *that voice. I know that voice. Register four, please. Thank you. It was you, in the egg aisle, two years ago. I felt you across a whole supermarket and I couldn't find you. And here you are.*

She'd never say it out loud. She never said any of it out loud. But she'd know. She always knew. She'd been feeling them coming for years — the drummer at the load-out, the voice on the PA, the pull in the rehearsal room above her own venue — the whole scattered band ringing at the edges of her perception, one signature at a time, while she bought eggs and coiled cables and made her sister dinner and waited, without knowing she was waiting, for the day the frequencies she kept feeling in strangers would finally all be in one room.

That day was coming. She just had to buy her eggs first.
