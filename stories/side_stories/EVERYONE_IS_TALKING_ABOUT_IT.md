# EVERYONE'S TALKING ABOUT IT

*Life & Times Side-Quest · Ensemble · December 16, 2017*
*Real event in the background: the New York Times breaks the AATIP story — the secret Pentagon UFO program — and releases the Nimitz videos*
*Register: dramatic irony, five-way intercut*
*Created: 2026-09-10*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. Five kids, five cities, one headline, one day. None of them know the others exist. In nine years they'll be a band. Today they're just five people who each, for one moment, feel something they can't explain — and then go back to their lives.*

---

## THE HEADLINE

On the morning of December 16, 2017, the New York Times published a story that should have changed the world and didn't. A secret Pentagon program. Recovered materials. Navy pilots on camera describing a "Tic Tac" that outflew them off the California coast in 2004. For about seventy-two hours the whole internet lit up, and then a bill and a scandal and a celebrity divorce rolled over it like weather, and by New Year's it was a thing people half-remembered, a *did you hear about that UFO thing,* a shrug in a headline's clothing.

But on that one Saturday it was everywhere. On every phone. In every feed. And five people who did not know each other — who would not meet for nine more years, in a half-empty venue, to become the only five people on Earth who could do what they could do — each ran into it, in the ordinary course of an ordinary Saturday, and each felt the floor of themselves shift a quarter-inch, and each explained it away, because that is what you do when you are eighteen or seventeen or sixteen or fifteen or thirteen and you have no idea what you are.

Here are the five moments. They happened within about six hours of each other, across four states, and no one will ever know they were connected. Except you.

---

## 10:40 AM — OREN, 18

Oren Malik is doing the thing he does instead of sleeping, which is lying on his back at the foot of his bed with his headphones in, listening to the building. It's Saturday, so the construction across the street is quiet, and without the jackhammer-hihat and the crane-kick he's been unconsciously arranging into a groove all week, the silence has a texture he doesn't like. Four years since his father died. Four years of the pattern-brain running with no off switch. He's learned to point it at rhythms because rhythms, at least, resolve.

His phone buzzes. It's Hass — his DJ friend, the one who'll eventually, unknowingly, be the thread that pulls him into the band — and it's a link and three words: *bro. UFOs are REAL.*

Oren opens it. Reads the whole thing, twice, the way he reads everything, sequential and total. And when he gets to the part where the pilots describe the object — *it dropped 80,000 feet, it had no exhaust, it moved and then it was just somewhere else* — something in his chest does a thing it has not done since the afternoon at 2:15 when his father's soul collapsed into him and the time-drift began.

It *recognizes a rhythm.* Not consciously. He just suddenly feels, reading the pilots' words, the specific vertigo of *time not behaving* — the drop, the skip, the being-somewhere-else — and it's the feeling he has when he loses twenty minutes, the feeling he's spent four years pretending he doesn't have. For one second, lying on the floor, he thinks: *I know that. I know what that feels like from the inside.*

Then he thinks: that's insane, it's a jet story, you didn't sleep, and he texts Hass back *lol wild* and puts on a Meshuggah record because the polyrhythm will drown out the thought. It works. Mostly. He does not sleep that night either, but he tells himself that's normal, because for him it is.

## 12:15 PM — JUDE, 17

Jude Okafor is at the kitchen table in his mother Grace's house, and the news is on the little counter TV because Grace watches the news the way she prays — constantly, half-listening, a hedge against a world she doesn't trust. The anchor is doing the UFO story now, the recovered-materials thing, and Grace makes the sound she makes at all news, a soft *mm* of disapproval at the state of everything.

Jude isn't really listening. He's got two years until the thing that will send him away — the incident, the palm-strike that travels through a whole house, the conviction — and right now he's just seventeen and full of an anger he can't name and can't spend, watching his hands on the table because his hands are where the pressure lives.

Then the anchor says the word *frequency.* Some scientist, some clip: the objects seemed to interfere with instruments, with radar, with *frequency.* And Jude's hands, flat on the table, feel it — a hum, faint, up through the wood grain, the specific sensation he gets sometimes when a truck goes by or a bass note hits just right, the sense that he can feel the *architecture* of a thing, its resonance, the way it would break. He presses his palms flatter. The hum is there. It's always there, a little. But the word *frequency* on the TV made it — louder? Or made him notice it?

"Turn that off, please," Grace says, not because of the UFOs, just because it's lunch. Jude turns it off. The hum in the table fades to the usual background almost-nothing. He flexes his hands. Thinks nothing, on purpose, the way he's trained himself to think nothing, because thinking about the hum leads to bad places. He eats his mother's jollof and says it's good, and it is.

## 1:50 PM — ZARA, 16

Zara Kovač is in the break room at The Frequency — she's sixteen, technically too young to work the door, but the owner lets her run merch and coil cables for cash because she's the most reliable kid he's ever met and she never, ever complains. There's a laptop open on the ratty couch and someone's got the NYT story pulled up, and two of the older sound guys are arguing about it, the good-natured argument of men who don't actually care: *it's weather balloons, man / it's a psyop / it's aliens, obviously.*

Zara says nothing. Zara is refilling the mini-fridge with the venue's terrible sparkling water and performing, as she performs every waking second, the role of *unremarkable teenage employee.* But she has read the story. Of course she has. She read it at 6 AM, in the dark, in bed, on her phone, the moment the alerts started, the way she reads everything about the sky.

And she knows exactly what the pilots saw off California in 2004, because she was three years old and she watched it on a Trinitron and she hailed it and it didn't come back. She knows the "Tic Tac" is a Shepherd craft. She knows the shape is a lie. She has known for thirteen years, which is longer than either of these arguing men has known anything.

One of them turns to her. "Yo, Zara. Aliens — real or fake?"

And Zara — sixteen, cornered, holding a case of sparkling water — does the calculation she has done ten thousand times, the one that governs her whole life: *what would a normal person say.* She makes her face do a little laugh. She says, "I hope they've got better music than us," and the men laugh, and the moment passes, and she goes back to the fridge, and no one sees the thing that crossed her face for one-eighth of one second, which was the oldest exhaustion in the world: *I could tell you. I could tell you everything. And it would ruin your Saturday and change nothing and they still would not come.* She closes the fridge. The gasket sucks shut. She goes back to work.

## 3:30 PM — KAEL, 15

Kael Voss is in Derek's truck, which is parked, which is rare, because Derek is almost always driving. But it's Saturday and Derek is home between hauls and he's taken his silent son to get tires, and now they're in the lot of the tire place with the radio on low, not talking, because they don't talk, because Kael doesn't and Derek has stopped knowing how to start.

The radio does the UFO story — a national feed, the AATIP thing, a clip of a pilot's voice. And Kael, fifteen, who has not spoken more than a few words a day since he was eight, who holds a door shut inside himself every night at 3 AM, listens to the pilot's voice on the radio and does something he almost never does on purpose: he *listens to the voice itself,* not the words. The frequency of it. The fear under the training. The way the man is describing something that unmade his understanding of the world and trying to sound like a professional while he does it.

And Kael thinks — in the wordless place where he lives — *he sounds like I feel.* Like there's a thing inside you too big for the shape you're allowed to make. Like you saw something real and now you have to spend the rest of your life saying it small so people don't look at you funny.

Derek, watching his son's face in the rearview out of habit, sees Kael's expression change — sees, for one second, something *open* — and Derek's heart does the thing it always does, the leap of *maybe he'll say something,* and Derek says, too eager, "Crazy, right? The UFO stuff?"

And the door in Kael closes. The opening goes. He gives his father the smallest nod and looks out the window. Derek deflates, hating himself for pushing, and starts the truck. Neither of them knows that for one second, over a stranger's voice on the radio, Kael Voss came closer to speaking than he had in seven years. The tires are fine. They drive home in silence.

## 5:05 PM — MIRA, 13

Mira Chen is thirteen and she is not allowed to say the true thing anymore, so when the UFO story comes across the family iPad at the dinner table and her brother Jason says "see, I *told* you the government's hiding aliens," and her father David makes his careful neutral face, Mira keeps her eyes on her rice.

Because Mira learned, at twelve, in the back seat of a car outside a psychiatrist's office, watching her father cry: *never tell them anything again.* She sees the entities every day. She has since she was born — they were the first faces she saw. She has a whole second world layered over this one, chromatic and crowded and constant, and she has spent a year now teaching herself to look at it without looking, to live in both and mention neither.

So when Jason laughs about aliens and David says something diplomatic and the iPad shows the grainy jet and the word *unexplained,* Mira feels a small dangerous warmth in her chest — the warmth of *what if it's all real, what if I'm not crazy, what if the thing I see is the same kind of thing they can't explain* — and she smothers it immediately, the way she's learned to smother everything, because hope is more dangerous than despair when you're thirteen and everyone thinks you might be sick.

She asks to be excused. She goes to her room. And there, alone, safe, she does the one thing she allows herself: she looks — really looks — at the corner of her ceiling, where one of them is, the tall still radiant one that's been with her since the crib, and she whispers, so quietly it's barely a shape in the air: "Are you like the thing on the news?"

The entity doesn't answer. They never answer — she'll understand why much later, that they were honoring a promise she made before she was born. But it *brightens,* just slightly, the chromatic dark of it deepening, and Mira takes that, and holds it, and it's the closest thing to *yes* she'll get for years. She goes to sleep almost happy. She dreams in colors that don't have names.

## THAT NIGHT

By Monday the story was already sinking. By New Year's it was a shrug. The world had been told, plainly, on the front page of its most serious newspaper, that the government had been studying craft it couldn't explain — and the world said *huh,* and *wild,* and *anyway,* and went back to its life, because a truth delivered as a mystery asks nothing of you, and people are tired, and there's always another headline.

Five kids went to sleep that night in four states. An eighteen-year-old who'd felt a rhythm he recognized. A seventeen-year-old whose hands had hummed at a word. A sixteen-year-old who could have explained the whole thing and chose a joke instead. A fifteen-year-old who almost spoke. A thirteen-year-old who whispered to her ceiling and got a color for an answer.

None of them knew the others were out there. None of them connected their small strange Saturday to anything. They were, each of them, alone with a thing they had no name for, on a day the whole country spent a few hours almost caring about the exact thing they secretly *were.*

Nine years. In nine years a DJ named Hass will mention a drummer who won't drum, and a sound tech will feel a rhythmic gap in a room, and it will begin. But not yet. Tonight they just sleep, five instruments scattered across a country, each humming faintly at a frequency none of them can hear, while the headline that was about them fades quietly off the front page.

The Signal was calling, that Saturday, on the evening news, in every feed, to everyone.

Only five people felt it. And not one of them knew.
