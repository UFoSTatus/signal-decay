# THE COFFEE SHOP RUN

## POST-NIGHTS

Oren Malik had worked four twelves in a row, which is the kind of stretch that turns a person into a ghost of themselves, and now he had three days off and no idea how to be a human being during daylight. This was the perpetual problem of nights. Your body forgets what noon is for. You come home when the world is waking up and you sleep through the part where it lives, and on your first day off you surface around 1 PM into an afternoon everyone else is halfway done with, blinking, useless, free.

So he did the thing he'd learned to do to re-enter the species: he went and sat in a coffee shop.

Not to be social — Oren was not social, not since his father died and the weight came down and the pattern-brain started running with no off switch. He went to a coffee shop for the specific reason that a coffee shop was a controlled amount of *other people.* A hum of humanity at a safe distance. Enough life to remind him he was in the world; not so much that he had to be *in* it.

The place was called Verdant, which was a stupid name, and it was two blocks from his apartment, and the barista with the forearm tattoos knew his order and didn't make him say it, which was the highest form of intimacy Oren currently allowed. He got the same thing every time — a large drip, black, and if he was feeling fancy, which he was today because he'd survived four nights, a cardamom bun, the sticky kind, the kind that made his fingers tacky and required a napkin campaign.

"Rough week?" the barista said, ringing him up. He had the drip already pouring. He'd started it when Oren walked in. That was the thing about Oren — even strangers could set their watch by him.

"Four nights," Oren said.

The barista winced in solidarity. "On the house," he said, and nudged the cardamom bun across the counter, and Oren felt, briefly, the small ridiculous warmth of a free pastry from a person who didn't know his name, and thought: *this is what people mean. This is the stuff. Okay.*

## 2:15 PM

He took his usual seat, the one in the corner with his back to the wall and a view of the door, which he told himself was a nurse thing (situational awareness, exits) and which was maybe just an Oren thing. He had a book — a used paperback, a spy novel from the seventies with a cracked spine and someone else's coffee ring already on page forty, which he found comforting, the idea that this exact book had sat in some other person's exact afternoon decades ago.

And for a while it was perfect. It was, genuinely, one of the good hours. The September light came through the front windows at a slant and lit up the steam off his cup. The espresso machine did its thing in bursts — the *chunk* of the portafilter, the shriek of the steam wand, the tick of it cooling. Conversations murmured at the edge of hearing, none of them his problem. He read his silly spy novel. He ate his sticky bun with a campaign of napkins. He drank his coffee while it was still hot, which never happened at work, where coffee was a thing you found cold three hours after you poured it, if you found it at all.

Here is the one not-quite-ordinary thing, and it was so gentle it wasn't even a problem, just a texture of being Oren:

He couldn't *not* hear the rhythm of the place.

The espresso machine was the kit. The portafilter knock was the kick — *chunk* ... *chunk-chunk* ... a syncopated thing, irregular, human, whenever the barista banged the grounds out. The steam wand was a hi-hat, a long shhhh with a choke at the end when he cut it. The little bell on the door was a rimshot, every time someone came in — *ting.* The espresso grinder was a snare roll. And under all of it, the bassline: the walk-in cooler's compressor, a low steady hum you'd never notice unless you were built to notice, holding down a fundamental while everything else played on top.

Oren didn't choose to hear it this way. He never had, not since he was fourteen. The world came to him pre-arranged into grooves. Most of the time it was a low-grade torment — the construction outside his window, the machinery, everything insisting on being *music* whether he wanted it or not. But sometimes, in a good hour, with a free cardamom bun and a silly book and September light, it wasn't torment. It was just... the room, being a song. The coffee shop had a groove. It was in about 96 BPM, shuffled, and it swung, and Oren sat in the corner of it and let it play and did not, for once, try to make it stop.

His foot was tapping. He noticed after a while that his foot was tapping — not to any music, because there was no music playing, just to the *room,* to the portafilter kick and the door-bell rimshot and the compressor bassline. He looked at his own foot with the mild betrayal he always felt toward his own body's relationship with rhythm. And then — this was new, this was the thing — instead of making it stop, the way he always made it stop, he just... let it tap. In the corner. Where no one could see. To the song the coffee shop didn't know it was playing.

It felt, distantly, like the first three seconds of something he wasn't ready to do. He didn't examine that. He turned a page.

## THE STRANGER'S KID

There was a woman two tables over with a toddler, one of those toddlers operating at the volume of a much larger person, and the kid was banging a spoon on the table — arrhythmic, chaotic, joyous, the way toddlers do — and the mom was doing the low-grade apology-face at the room that all parents of loud toddlers do.

And Oren, without deciding to, without even really noticing, started tapping his finger on his own table. Quietly. In a simple, steady, four-count — a pocket. A little groove for the kid to fall into.

The toddler noticed. Toddlers always notice. The chaotic banging faltered, and then — the way kids do, drawn to a pulse like iron filings to a magnet — the kid started banging *on* Oren's beat. Not perfectly. But *with* it. Found the pocket. Locked in, in the loose approximate way a two-year-old locks into anything, and got *delighted* about it, this whole-body toddler delight, banging the spoon in time and looking around like *are you all seeing this.*

The mom looked over, ready to apologize, and saw a tired-looking guy in the corner with a paperback quietly giving her kid a beat to play to, and her face did something soft and grateful, and she mouthed *thank you,* and Oren — who did not do social, who had not initiated human contact on purpose in longer than he'd admit — gave her a small nod and the ghost of a smile and went back to his book, his finger still keeping the four-count, the kid still banging along, the coffee shop's own groove humming underneath all of it: the compressor bass, the portafilter kick, the door-bell rimshot, a tired nurse's finger, a delighted toddler's spoon, all of it, briefly, in time.

For about ninety seconds the whole room was one groove and Oren Malik was, without touching a drum, without meaning to, without even knowing he was doing it, holding the pocket for a stranger's kid.

Then the kid got bored, the way kids do, and threw the spoon on the floor, and the groove dissolved, and the mom scooped her chaos back up, and Oren turned a page.

But he was still tapping. He noticed, later, walking home in the four-o'clock light with coffee in him and a silly book finished, that he'd been tapping the whole time. On his leg. A steady four. And that for once he hadn't made it stop.

He didn't know what that meant. He wasn't ready to. It would be a while yet — a DJ friend, a mention, a half-empty venue, a rhythmic gap in a room that five people were unconsciously trying to fill. Not today. Today was just a good hour: a free cardamom bun, a silly spy novel, September light, a stranger's kid, and a groove the coffee shop didn't know it was playing.

Today he was just a guy who got a coffee. It was one of the best days he'd had in months. He'd tell you nothing happened.

Something did, a little. His foot kept time all the way home, and he let it.
