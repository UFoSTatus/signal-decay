# THE LOAD-OUT

*Life & Times Side-Quest · Oren & Zara · a night ~2025, before the band*
*Register: near-miss crossing — dramatic irony*
*Created: 2026-09-11*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. Two future bandmates are in the same room for eleven minutes, a year before they'll meet "for the first time," and neither of them knows. One of them almost feels it.*

---

## 11:40 PM

Oren Malik did not want to be at The Frequency. He wanted to be asleep, or as close to asleep as he got, which was lying on the floor of his apartment with headphones in, letting the building's HVAC play its slow four-on-the-floor while he didn't sleep. But Hass had texted — *need help moving Kyle's sub amp, thing weighs like 90lbs, 30 min tops then I drive you home* — and Hass was the closest thing Oren had to a friend who didn't require anything of him, so here he was, 11:40 on a weeknight, at the back dock of a mid-tier venue, waiting for a DJ to finish so he could carry something heavy.

The show was winding down. Some local act Oren didn't know, the kind of band that plays to forty people on a Tuesday and means it more than the forty people do. He stood by the loading door in the specific posture of a man waiting to lift something, hands in his pockets, watching the room break down.

And the room, to Oren, was a rhythm. It always was. The clink of glasses being bussed (irregular, a shaker), the roll of a road case over the floor seams (a tom fill, *da-dun da-dun*), the thunk of mic stands folding, someone's laugh, the bass still humming in the PA even with nothing playing — that low idle hum a sound system makes when it's on but empty, a sustained fundamental under everything. Oren stood in the middle of the venue's after-show groove and did the thing he couldn't not do: he heard it as music, and part of him, the part he kept locked, wanted to lay a kick under it.

He didn't. He never did. But tonight the *itch* was strong, and he didn't know why, and he shifted his weight and told himself it was because he was tired.

He didn't know that the reason the itch was strong was standing forty feet away, coiling a cable.

---

## THE SOUND TECH

Zara Kovač was closing down the board. This was the part of the night she liked best — the room emptying, the work simplifying to cables and cases, the ordinary meditative labor of putting a venue to sleep. She was good at it, fast and quiet, and the owner left her alone because she never needed managing. She coiled a mic cable over-under without looking, the loop falling perfect every time, muscle memory in a body she'd worn for twenty-four years and still sometimes forgot was hers.

And then she felt it.

Not loud. A flicker. A frequency in the room that shouldn't have been there — a rhythmic *pull*, a 7/8 kind of thing, right at the edge of perception, coming from the direction of the loading door. She'd been feeling little gaps like this in rooms lately, more than usual, phantom recognitions she'd trained herself to ignore since she was a toddler. Earth was full of frequencies that *almost* matched something. You learned to let them pass. She'd let ten thousand of them pass.

But this one made her stop coiling.

She looked up. Toward the loading door. There was a guy standing there — youngish, tired-looking, hands in his pockets, the posture of someone waiting to carry gear. Nothing remarkable. A friend of a friend, roped into a load-out. She'd seen a thousand of him. He wasn't doing anything. He was just standing there, shifting his weight slightly, and —

*and his weight was shifting in 7/8.*

Not consciously. He wasn't tapping a foot or nodding along; there was no music playing. He was just a tired man standing by a door, and his body, without his knowledge, was keeping a time signature that doesn't occur in human popular music, a compound meter that shifted every bar, the exact rhythmic shape of the void Zara had been feeling in the room.

Zara stood very still, a coiled cable in her hand, and looked at a stranger keeping impossible time by a loading door, and something in the oldest part of her — the part that had hailed a Tic Tac off a Trinitron at three years old — went quiet and alert. *That's not nothing,* she thought. *That's a signature. That's someone.*

---

## THE ELEVEN MINUTES

Here is everything that happened, which is almost nothing:

Hass came off the decks, sweaty and delighted, and clapped Oren on the shoulder and said "my man, thank you, come on, it's the big black one by the stage," and Oren pushed off the wall and went to lift a 90-pound subwoofer amp with a stranger, and his path across the venue took him within about fifteen feet of the sound board.

He passed Zara. Neither of them said anything, because why would they — a load-out guy and a sound tech, two people doing jobs in a closing venue, the most ordinary non-encounter in the world. Oren didn't even see her, really; she was just part of the room, a person at a board. He was thinking about his back and the ninety pounds and getting home.

Zara saw him. Zara watched him the whole fifteen feet, the way she watched anything that rang, and as he passed the board the rhythmic pull peaked — the 7/8 thing loud in her chest for one second, undeniable, *right there* — and then he was past, and it faded, and he was crouching by the stage getting his hands under an amp with Hass, an ordinary tired guy doing a favor, and the moment was over.

She almost said something. This is the part that matters, the part that's the whole story. Zara Kovač — who had spent twenty-four years performing normal, who never initiated, who let ten thousand phantom recognitions pass — stood at her board with a cable in her hand and *almost* walked over and said... what? *Hey. Do you play drums?* To a stranger carrying an amp? On the basis of a feeling in her chest she could never explain? On the basis of the way his weight shifted?

She didn't. Of course she didn't. You don't do that. You don't walk up to a random load-out guy and tell him his body is keeping alien time. That's how you get looked at. That's how twenty-four years of careful invisibility comes apart. So she went back to coiling cable, and she let him pass, and she let the recognition go, the way she'd let everything go, and Oren and Hass hauled the sub amp out the loading door into the night and were gone.

Eleven minutes, door to door. Two future bandmates in one emptying room. One of them oblivious. One of them feeling the pull and choosing, as she always chose, silence.

---

## WHAT THEY BOTH TOOK HOME

Oren rode home in Hass's car with his back aching and the radio low, and he felt, vaguely, better than he had in a while, lighter, though he couldn't have told you why. He'd remember it, if you asked him later, as *nothing* — a night he helped Hass move an amp. He would not connect it, a year on, to the drummer he'd become. He'd have no reason to. He never saw her.

But the itch had been strong in that room. And for a few days after, the locked door in him — the one behind which the drummer waited — felt, not open, but *knocked on.* Like something had passed close by and rapped its knuckles once and moved on. He didn't examine it. He put on a record and let it fade.

Zara drove home to the apartment near Lena's, and she thought about the guy at the load-out, and she filed him — the way she filed everything, perfect recall, unfading — under a category she'd been quietly building for years: *signatures I've felt and let pass.* There were a handful in there. A voice over a grocery-store PA once. A pull in a rehearsal room above her own venue that she'd never run down. And now a tired man keeping 7/8 by a loading door.

She didn't know they'd meet again. She didn't know that in about a year a DJ named Hass would mention to someone that his friend *should* be a drummer but won't play, and that she'd be in the room, and that she'd feel the same 7/8 pull and this time — this time — not let it pass. She didn't know the load-out guy was one of the Five, that she was one of the Five, that the room had held two-fifths of a band that would one day matter more than either of them could imagine.

She just knew she'd felt something, and let it go, and driven home. She was good at letting things go. She'd had twenty-four years of practice.

But she filed him. She filed everyone who rang. And when she finally met Oren Malik "for the first time," a year later, and felt the 7/8 pull for the second time in her life — some quiet part of her, the part that remembered everything, would think: *oh. It's you. From the load-out. I knew I'd felt you before.*

She'd never say it. But she'd know. She always knew.

Oren carries an amp and feels, faintly, a locked door get knocked on. Zara feels the 7/8 pull of his unconscious rhythm across the room, almost speaks, and — as she always does — lets it pass and files it away in her perfect memory. The band was always going to happen. The frequencies were already finding each other. The people just hadn't caught up yet.*
