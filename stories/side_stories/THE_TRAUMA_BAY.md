# THE TRAUMA BAY

*Life & Times Side-Quest · Jude & Oren · a bad night ~2025, before the band*
*Register: near-miss crossing — dramatic irony (Jude's POV, for a change)*
*Created: 2026-09-11*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. Every other crossing has been felt from Zara's side. This one's from Jude's — because Jude Okafor doesn't feel frequencies, he feels structure, and one bad night on a job site puts him in an ER trauma bay where a night-shift nurse keeps time on his pulse like a metronome, and Jude, flat on a gurney, feels the whole building around him and one steady thing inside it that he can't explain.*

---

## THE POUR THAT WENT WRONG

Jude Okafor was three years out and keeping his head down, which for Jude meant work — the physical, honest, nobody-asks-questions kind of work that a man with a record can still get: concrete. He was good at it. Better than good, in a way he never advertised, because Jude could *feel* a pour the way other men could see it. Feel where the mix was setting wrong, feel a void forming deep in a form before it ever showed on the surface, feel the difference between concrete that would hold and concrete that would crack. His crews thought he had instincts. Jude knew it wasn't instincts. He knew it was the thing he didn't talk about, the thing that had been with him since before prison, the thing that made him lay his palm flat on a fresh wall and *know* it.

The night it went wrong wasn't his fault, which almost never matters. A form blew on a tall pour — the bracing gave, a face of wet concrete and formwork came down, and Jude, who'd felt the void an instant before it went and was already moving, got most of the way clear and not all the way. A steel waler caught his leg. Deep gash, a lot of blood, the specific white-hot wrongness of a bone that isn't broken but wishes it were. His crew got him out. Somebody drove too fast to the nearest ER. And that's how Jude Okafor, who avoided institutions of every kind on principle, ended up flat on his back on a gurney under fluorescent lights at two in the morning, exactly where he never wanted to be.

---

## THE BUILDING AROUND HIM

Here is what nobody in the ER knew about the man bleeding on their gurney: he was reading their building.

He couldn't not. Flat on his back, leg on fire, waiting — Jude did the thing he always did in any structure, the thing that had kept him calm in a cell for three years: he felt the building around him. The load paths. The old bones of the place — this was an old hospital, added onto a dozen times, and Jude could feel the seams where the additions met the original, feel the tired steel and the good steel, feel the hum of the structure holding itself up. It steadied him. It always steadied him. A building was a body and Jude could feel its skeleton, and knowing the skeleton meant knowing you were held, and Jude, who trusted almost nothing, trusted structure.

So he lay there reading the hospital's bones and waiting to be seen, and that's when the night-shift nurse came in.

---

## THE NURSE WHO KEPT TIME

The nurse was youngish, moved fast and calm, the specific unhurried-hurry of someone who'd done a thousand bad nights. He got Jude's leg elevated, got pressure on it, talked low and steady the whole time — *okay, you're okay, that's a mean one but it's clean, we're gonna take good care of you* — and took Jude's pulse with two fingers on his wrist, and here is the thing Jude noticed, the thing he'd remember, the small strange thing:

The nurse kept perfect time.

Not with a watch. He wasn't looking at a clock. He had two fingers on Jude's wrist and his lips barely moving and Jude, who felt rhythm in structures the way he felt everything, felt the nurse *counting* — and the count was *exact.* Metronome-exact. Inhumanly steady, a pulse-count with no drift in it at all, the kind of perfect internal time that Jude had never once felt off another person. Jude's own heart was going too fast, ragged with adrenaline, an arrhythmic mess — and the nurse's count under it was a rock, a perfect grid, and for a second Jude had the disorienting sense that the nurse's steadiness was pulling his own racing heart *toward* it, the way a strong beat pulls a sloppy one into line.

He didn't have words for it. He wasn't a frequency guy; he was a structure guy. But if you'd made him say it, he'd have said: *that nurse is built like good steel. There's no give in his time.*

---

## WHAT THE NURSE WAS

Oren Malik was the nurse, working the night shift he always worked, doing the job that gave his broken sleep somewhere to go. And the reason his pulse-count was metronome-exact — the reason it had no drift — was the same reason he couldn't play the drums he was made for: Oren *was* time. He kept perfect internal tempo the way other people breathe, an involuntary metronome that ran under everything he did, that made him the best pulse-taker on the floor and the worst insomniac in the county, because you cannot turn off a clock that is your own body. He counted Jude's pulse without a watch because he never needed a watch. He'd stopped noticing he did it.

He had no idea the bleeding man on the gurney could feel his steadiness. He had no idea the man was reading the hospital's structure like sheet music, or that the perfect time Oren couldn't help keeping was landing in that man as *good steel, no give.* To Oren, this was a routine leg lac at 2 AM — clean the wound, keep the guy calm, wait for the doc, chart it, next. He was kind and he was fast and he was steady, and he never once thought about the patient again after his shift, because there'd been eleven other patients and it was just a Tuesday in the department.

Two future bandmates. One trauma bay. Forty minutes, maybe. The drummer keeping perfect time on the ex-con's wrist; the ex-con feeling that perfect time as structural soundness and taking a strange comfort from it he couldn't name. Then the doctor came, and the nurse moved on to the next bay, and Jude got stitched and splinted and sent home on crutches, and it was over.

---

## WHAT JUDE TOOK HOME

Jude Okafor healed up and went back to work, and he did not think about the hospital, because Jude trained himself not to think about places he didn't want to be. But the *nurse* stuck, a little, in the odd way small things stick. Not the face — Jude couldn't have described the face. The *steadiness.* For a while after, on bad nights, when his own heart was going ragged with the things that made an ex-con's heart go ragged at 3 AM, Jude would find himself reaching for the memory of that count. That perfect, un-drifting, good-steel time somebody had kept on his wrist once. It steadied him the way a sound structure steadied him. He didn't know why. He filed it, the way you file a thing that helped, and mostly forgot it.

He didn't know it was a drummer. He didn't know the man's metronome heart was the same instrument that would, in a couple of years, lock under Jude's rhythm guitar and hold the whole band to the grid. He didn't know he'd already met, and been calmed by, the timekeeper of his own future. He just knew a nurse had once been steady when Jude needed steady, and that it had helped.

And a couple of years later, when Jude Okafor stood in a rehearsal room and heard Oren Malik play drums for the first time — that perfect, un-drifting, no-give *time,* rock-solid under everything, pulling the whole ragged room into line — some deep body-memory in Jude would stir, and he wouldn't be able to place it, not exactly, but he'd feel a comfort he'd felt exactly once before, flat on his back under bad lights, and he'd think, without knowing why he thought it:

*Good steel. No give. I know this. I've been held by this before.*

He'd never run it down. Jude wasn't a run-it-down kind of man. But he'd trust Oren fast — faster than Jude trusted anyone — and he'd never quite know that the reason was a trauma bay at 2 AM and a count on his wrist, the night the drummer he didn't know yet held his racing heart to a grid and made a bleeding stranger feel, for forty minutes, like a building that would hold.
