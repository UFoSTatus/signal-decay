# TWO KINDS OF OLD

*Side-Story · Ami Solana × Zara Kovač · ~2026, a few weeks into the sixth chair*
*Register: two-hander / the quiet recognition between two listeners*
*Created: 2026-09-23*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. Ami has been coming to practices for a few weeks now — the girl in the sixth chair who hears everything. This is the night the oldest person in the room decides to sit with her after, and the two of them find out they're listening for different things in the same silence.*

---

## AFTER PRACTICE

The others cleared out the way bands do — Oren first (always somewhere to be), then Jude and Kael together arguing amiably about a bridge, then Mira, who squeezed Ami's shoulder on the way past and said "same time Thursday?" and was gone before Ami finished nodding.

Which left the sound tech. Zara Kovač, coiling cable at the back of the upstairs room the way she did every night, unhurried, the loops falling perfect without her looking. Ami had noticed that about her — that Zara never watched her own hands. As if the hands knew the work so deeply that watching would be an insult.

Ami should have left. She stayed. She'd been staying a little longer each practice, and she was starting to understand that the reason was Zara, and she was starting to understand that Zara knew it.

"You hear the room," Zara said, not turning around. "Even now. Empty. You're listening to it."

Ami didn't bother denying it. That was the thing about this band — you didn't have to perform normal here, and the relief of that was still so new it made her reckless. "It's louder empty," she said. "When you're all playing, it's — full. Resolved. There's nowhere for the room-tone to sit because you're sitting in all of it. But empty —" she tilted her head "— the northeast corner comes back. That hum. It's always there. This building sits on a B-flat, a little sharp."

Now Zara turned. And she looked at Ami with those eyes that were too steady, too old for a twenty-five-year-old's face, and she said: "It's not the building."

---

## WHAT ZARA KNEW ABOUT THE CORNER

"What do you mean it's not the building," Ami said.

Zara set down the cable. She crossed to the northeast corner — the one Ami's ear always came back to — and put her palm flat against the brick, the way Jude read a wall, except Zara wasn't reading load. She was reading something older.

"I've worked this room three years," Zara said. "First day I walked in, my whole body went *oh.* Like coming home. I thought it was the acoustics — the brick, the concrete, the height. Good bones. That's what I told myself." She pressed her palm flatter. "It's not the bones. This building sits on a well. A small one. A place where the — " she paused, choosing a word that a normal person could hear "— where the frequency comes *up* out of the ground a little. Most rooms sit on nothing. This one sits on a note. You're the first person besides me who's ever walked in and heard it."

Ami's skin prickled. "How do you know that's what it is? A — a well?"

And Zara smiled, which she rarely did, and it was a strange smile, kind and very far away. "Because I'm old," she said. "Older than I look. Older than the others know. I've been listening for that note a long time." A beat. "Longer than one life."

Ami didn't understand the words. Her ear understood the tone under them — the way Zara's voice, when she said *longer than one life,* dropped into that register Ami had clocked through the wall on her first night, the register that didn't sound entirely human, the note pretending to be a note. And for once Ami didn't push. Some sounds you don't run down. You just let them ring and file them.

"You're like Mira," Ami said carefully. "You said — the first night — you asked if I *make* the sound or only hear it. Like it mattered which."

"It matters," Zara said, "which."

---

## THE THING ZARA CHECKED, AND WHY

Zara came and sat down across from her — actually sat, folded onto the floor of the empty room, which Ami had never seen her do. Up close, in the quiet, the too-old eyes were gentle.

"The others make the sound," Zara said. "Kael, Jude, Oren, Mira, me — we broadcast. We can't help it. We've been broadcasting since before we could talk, and things that listen for broadcasts have been able to find us our whole lives. You understand? Being able to make the note is the same as being *findable.* It's not a gift. It's a porch light. It tells the things in the dark exactly where you sleep."

Ami went very still. "What things in the dark."

"Old question. Wrong question for tonight." Zara waved it off, gently, the way you'd steer a child from a stove. "The right thing — the thing I checked, that first night, and the reason I've been watching you since — is that *you don't make the note.* You only hear it. Perfectly. Better than any of us. But you don't emit. You're dark. Silent. A receiver with the transmitter switched off." She looked at Ami with something Ami slowly realized was *relief,* even tenderness. "Do you know what that means? For someone built like you, in a world with things that hunt the light?"

"...No."

"It means the porch light is off," Zara said softly. "It means you hear everything and nothing hears *you.* You could stand in the middle of the loudest room in the world and the things that find the rest of us would walk right past you. You're the safest one here, Ami. And you have no idea, and I hope you never have to."

---

## WHAT AMI HEARD UNDERNEATH

Ami sat with that. Her whole life she'd thought her ear was the thing that made her *strange* — the wrongness, the too-much, the thing you learned by twelve to hide. And here was this ancient-eyed woman on the floor of an empty room telling her that the same ear that made her strange also made her *invisible* to something Zara clearly feared and wouldn't name.

She didn't fully understand it. But she understood the *shape* of it, the way she understood everything — by ear, by the tone under the words. And the tone under Zara's words was protective. Fierce, even, under all that stillness. Zara had checked, that first night, whether Ami was findable — and had been *glad* when the answer was no.

"You were worried about me," Ami said. "When you asked. You wanted the answer to be that I only hear it."

"Yes."

"Why? You didn't even know me."

Zara was quiet for a moment, and the room-tone hummed its flat B-flat in the corner, and the well under the floor sang its note that only the two of them could hear.

"Because I've watched people I love get found," Zara said finally. "More than you'd believe. And when I met you, and you heard us through the wall, and you named us all by our frequencies — for one second my heart stopped, because I thought: *another one. Another porch light. Another one they'll come for.*" She looked up. "And then I listened, really listened, and I heard that you were dark. And I could breathe again. You're the one of us they can't take. That's worth being glad about. That's worth an old woman sitting on a dirty floor to tell you, even if you won't understand it for years."

---

## TWO KINDS OF OLD

They sat a while longer. Didn't say much. Two listeners in an empty room that sat on a note, one who broadcast and one who only received, one impossibly old and one just young enough to not yet know what she'd been spared.

Before Ami left, she stopped at the door — the same door she'd stood outside on her first night, listening through the wall to five signals ringing.

"You said you're old," Ami said. "Older than the others know."

Zara, coiling the last cable, didn't look up. "I did."

"How old?"

And Zara smiled again, that far-away smile, and gave her the only answer she ever gave anyone, the true one disguised as a joke: "Older than the note in that corner. I helped tune it."

Ami laughed, because it sounded like a joke.

It wasn't a joke. But she wouldn't know that for a long time, and by then she'd have learned what Zara already knew: that in a room full of people the dark was hunting, the girl who only listened was the one thing in it that the dark would never hear coming — and that the oldest soul in the band had loved her, quietly, from the first night, for exactly that reason.
