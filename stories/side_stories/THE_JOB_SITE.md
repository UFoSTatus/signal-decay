# THE JOB SITE

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. A guy pours concrete, eats lunch, goes home. Nobody has powers today. It's just a day. Those matter too.*

---

## 6:50 AM

Jude Okafor got to the site ten minutes early because he always got everywhere ten minutes early, a habit he'd built inside, where being late was a thing that happened to other people and had consequences you felt in your whole body. Ten minutes early meant you got the good coffee from the gas station on the way, not the burnt bottom-of-the-pot at the trailer. Ten minutes early meant you picked your spot. Ten minutes early meant nobody could say anything to you, and Jude had spent a lot of his life arranging things so nobody could say anything to him.

It was a foundation pour today, which Jude liked, because a foundation pour was honest work with a clear beginning and end. You framed it, you set the rebar, the truck came, you moved fast because concrete doesn't wait for anybody, and at the end of the day there was a *thing* there that hadn't been there in the morning. A floor. A start of a house. Somebody would live on top of what Jude did today, for fifty years, and never know his name, and that was fine. That was better than fine. Jude had made peace with being the thing underneath that holds the weight. He'd had practice.

The foreman, a leathery Portuguese guy named Tavares who ran the crew like a tired uncle, nodded at him. "Okafor. You're on the vibrator today." Meaning the concrete vibrator — the tool you sink into wet concrete to shake the air bubbles out, to settle it dense and solid so it cures without voids. "Big John's out. His back."

"Big John's back's been out since the Clinton administration," said someone, and there was the loose morning laughter of men who don't especially want to be awake.

Jude took the vibrator. It was, privately, his favorite job, and he'd never say why, because the why was a little strange even to him.

---

## THE VIBRATOR

Here is the one not-ordinary thing in this ordinary day, and it's so quiet it barely counts:

When Jude ran the concrete vibrator — sank the head into the wet gray slurry and felt the hum travel up the shaft into his hands, felt it move *through* the pour, shaking the aggregate down, driving the air out — he could feel where the voids were.

Not with instruments. With his hands. The way some men can feel a stud behind drywall by knocking, Jude could feel the *resonance* of the pour change as it settled — could feel, through the vibrating steel, the difference between concrete that had gone dense and true and concrete that still had a pocket of air hiding in it, a weakness, a place that would crack in ten years if you left it. Tavares had noticed, over the months, that Jude's pours never had voids. Never. The inspector's little hammer test always came back clean. Tavares didn't know how and didn't ask; you don't ask a good worker how, you just put him on the vibrator when Big John's back goes out.

Jude knew how. Jude had known since he was fifteen, when a hard chug on a guitar could travel ten feet through a floor, and known it worse at nineteen, when a single palm-strike went through an entire house and put him somewhere he spent years learning wasn't anyone's fault but his own. He could feel the deep architecture of solid things. It was the worst thing about him. It was also, it turned out, extremely useful for pouring concrete, which was a joke the universe had made at his expense and which he had decided, somewhere along the way, to find funny instead of bitter.

So he ran the vibrator, and felt the pour go dense and true under his hands, and found the one air pocket over by the northeast corner that nobody else would've caught, and worked it out, and the floor came up perfect. Honest work. A thing that would hold weight. He was good at it for reasons he'd never explain, and being good at something without anyone knowing why was, for Jude, the safest kind of good there was.

## LUNCH

At noon they sat on overturned buckets and the tailgate of Tavares's truck and ate. Jude had brought jollof, because his mother Grace had made a huge pot Sunday and had been distributing it to him in foil-covered bowls all week like a woman feeding a much larger family than she had. It was better cold, somehow, on day three, the flavors gone deep. He ate it with a plastic fork and didn't share and nobody asked, because everyone on a crew guards their lunch.

His phone buzzed. It was Faith. His little sister — the only person alive who called him *Ori*, which meant "head" in Yoruba, the seat of destiny, and which she'd started calling him when she was six and had never explained. She'd texted a photo: her, grinning, holding up an acceptance letter. She'd gotten into the program. The nursing thing. The one she'd been terrified about.

Jude looked at the photo on a bucket at a construction site with jollof on a plastic fork, and something moved through his chest that had nothing to do with frequency and everything to do with being a person. Pride. Big, plain, uncomplicated pride, the kind he almost never got to feel about anything, least of all himself.

He typed, deleted, typed. He was not a words guy. Finally he sent: *knew you would. proud of you Faith.* Then, after a second, because it was true and she should hear it: *dad wouldve been proud too.*

The three dots appeared. Disappeared. Appeared. Then: *stop im at work im gonna cry lol.* Then: *love you Ori.*

Jude put the phone in his pocket and finished his jollof and did not cry, because he was at work too, and there were men around, and he'd built a whole life out of not doing certain things where men could see. But he sat in the noon sun on an overturned bucket for a minute longer than he needed to, feeling the good feeling, letting it be there. His sister was going to be a nurse. He'd poured a floor that morning that would hold a family for fifty years. It was, all things considered, a good day, and Jude Okafor had learned the hard way not to waste the good days by looking past them.

---

## 4:30 PM

The pour cured. The light went long and orange the way it does at the end of a work day, and the crew broke down the forms and hosed off the tools and the floor sat there gray and perfect, a start of a house, a thing that hadn't existed at dawn.

Tavares clapped Jude on the shoulder on the way to the trucks. "Clean pour, Okafor. You got a feel for it." He said it the way foremen say things, not really looking, already thinking about tomorrow. But Jude heard it. *You got a feel for it.* If Tavares only knew. If anyone only knew what Jude's *feel* was, where it came from, what it had done at nineteen, what it could do if he ever stopped holding it in. But Tavares didn't know, and never would, and that was exactly how Jude wanted it. To be, to everyone here, just a reliable guy with a good feel for concrete. Not the cage. Not the incident. Not the thing pressing against the walls he'd built. Just a guy who poured a clean floor.

He drove home with the windows down. He'd stop and see his mother, drop off the empty jollof bowls, tell her about Faith in person because Grace deserved to hear it from a face and not a phone. Maybe stay for dinner. Maybe not. He'd feel it out.

His hands, on the steering wheel, still had the faint hum in them from a day on the vibrator — the deep resonance of solid things, the sense of the world's architecture that never fully switched off. He flexed them at a red light. It was there, low, like it was always there. But today it hadn't been a curse. Today it had made a floor come up perfect and a foreman say *you got a feel for it* and it had let a man who could shatter a house with a touch spend his day building one instead.

There were worse days. There'd been worse days. Today Jude Okafor poured concrete, got a text from his sister, ate his mother's cooking cold on a bucket, and drove home in the orange light with his windows down.

Nothing happened. It was one of the good ones.
