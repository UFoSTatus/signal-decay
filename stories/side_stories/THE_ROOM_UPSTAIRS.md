# THE ROOM UPSTAIRS

*Life & Times Side-Quest · Zara & Mira · a stretch of weeknights ~2025, before the band*
*Register: near-miss crossing — dramatic irony (the long one, over weeks)*
*Created: 2026-09-11*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. This is the crossing that isn't ninety seconds — it's weeks. Two future bandmates worked one floor apart, over and over, and never once stood in the same room. One of them felt the other through the ceiling every single night and never ran it down.*

---

## THE BUILDING

The Frequency was a two-floor building, which almost nobody who drank there ever thought about. Downstairs was the venue — the room, the bar, the stage, the board where Zara Kovač worked. Upstairs, reachable by a fire-stair around the side that most patrons never saw, was a warren of cheap rehearsal rooms the owner rented by the hour to bands who needed a place to be loud that wasn't a garage. The two floors kept separate hours, separate doors, separate people. You could work the board downstairs for two years and never once climb the side stair. Zara had.

But she knew the room upstairs was there. Not by seeing it. By feeling it.

Because for a stretch of weeknights that spring, somebody rented the upstairs corner room — the one directly above the board — and practiced guitar. Alone. Late. And Zara Kovač, one floor down, running sound for whatever act was on, kept getting *pulled.*

---

## THE PULL THROUGH THE CEILING

It started small. A Tuesday, slow night, some singer-songwriter on stage doing his earnest thing, and Zara at the board felt — under the room, under the PA, under the singer — a *color.* That was the only word for it, which annoyed her, because Zara felt frequencies as frequencies, cleanly, the way she felt everything; she didn't do *color.* But this was leaking down through the ceiling from the room upstairs, some guitar being practiced up there, and it wasn't coming to her as sound (the venue was too loud, she couldn't have heard it) — it was coming to her as a *pull,* a resonance in the low registers she lived in, and it had a quality she could only call color. Warm. Amber-going-red. A frequency that didn't just sound like something but *looked* like something, if a frequency could look.

She wrote it off. Long night. Tired.

But it happened again the next weeknight. And the one after. Whoever was up in the corner room kept coming back — Tuesdays, Wednesdays, the dead nights — and every time they did, Zara felt it through the floor of the room above her: a guitar practiced alone, throwing off a resonance that reached her as color, amber and moving, a signature that *rang* in the exact way the handful of filed signatures in her memory rang. The voice on the PA. The drummer at the load-out. And now this — a guitarist upstairs she never saw, painting the ceiling a color only she could feel.

---

## WHAT WAS ACTUALLY UP THERE

Mira Chen was up there, and Mira Chen was doing the thing Mira did that she'd never told anyone about, because how do you tell anyone: she was practicing scales and watching them turn colors in the air.

Mira was a synesthete — sound was color to her, always had been, since before she had words for it. A C was a particular deep red. A minor third bruised purple. When she played, the room filled with light that only she could see, ribbons of it coming off the strings, and she'd learned as a kid to stop mentioning it because the look people gave you when you said "that chord is orange" was a look you only needed to get a few times. So she practiced alone, late, in the cheap corner room above a venue, because it was cheap and it was private and up there nobody could see her face when the colors got good.

She had no idea there was a woman one floor below her who could feel the colors she was making. She had no idea the room she'd picked for its cheapness and privacy sat directly on top of a person built to receive exactly what Mira couldn't help transmitting. She just played her scales, watched her private aurora, packed up her guitar around eleven, went down the side stair — the *side* stair, never through the venue — and drove home, having brushed, without a single point of contact, the bassist she'd play beside for the rest of her life.

Weeknight after weeknight. One floor apart. The synesthete throwing color she thought only she could see; the alien-souled sound tech below her, feeling that color come down through the ceiling and *ring.*

---

## THE NIGHT SHE ALMOST WENT UP

There was one night Zara almost climbed the stair.

It was late, the venue was clearing, the amber pull through the ceiling was strong — the guitarist upstairs was into something, some run of notes that came down to Zara as a color so specific and so *right* that she stood at her half-torn-down board with a cable in her hand and thought, clearly, in words: *I should go up there. I should just go up and see who that is.*

And she got as far as the bottom of the side stair. She actually went outside, around to the side of the building, and stood at the foot of the fire-stair with her hand on the cold rail, looking up at the one lit window on the second floor, the corner room, where somebody was playing a guitar that painted the dark amber.

And then she did the math she always did. *What would a normal person do.* A normal person does not climb a stranger's fire-stair at eleven at night to knock on a rehearsal-room door and say — what? *Hey. Your guitar is a color. I can feel it through the floor. Are you like me?* A normal person does not do that. A normal person, if they even noticed the guitar at all, thinks *someone's practicing* and goes home.

So Zara took her hand off the rail. She looked up at the lit window one more time. And she went home, and she filed it — *the guitarist upstairs, amber, plays alone on weeknights* — in the private list of signatures she'd felt and let pass, and she let it pass, the way she let all of them pass, the way she'd been trained by twenty-four years to let everything pass.

The light in the corner window went dark a little after eleven. Mira packed up and took the side stair down, and crossed the parking lot maybe thirty feet from Zara's car, two women going home from the same building on the same night, and neither of them looked up.

---

## THE LOOP CLOSES (LATER)

The upstairs rentals ended when Mira's money did, and the amber stopped coming through the ceiling, and Zara noticed the *absence* of it for a week or two — the way you notice a sound that stops — and then filed that too, and moved on. She never learned who it had been. She had no way to. A guitarist, upstairs, gone now. One more signature felt and lost.

But a year and change later, in a half-empty room, Zara Kovač would stand near a lead guitarist named Mira Chen for the first time — the "first time" — while Mira ran a warm-up scale, unthinking, and the notes would come off the strings and Zara would feel the *color* of them, amber going red, and every hair on her body would stand up, and the oldest part of her would go still and certain and think:

*It's you. You're the room upstairs. You're the amber that came down through my ceiling for a month. I stood at the bottom of your stair once and I almost came up. I felt you for weeks and I never saw your face and here it is.*

She'd never say it. She never said any of it. But she'd know — she always knew — and there'd be a private, enormous relief in it, the relief of a question closing after a year: *oh. So that's who that was.* She'd spent her whole life feeling the band coming, one signature at a time, through ceilings and PAs and loading docks, filing each one, letting each one pass. And now here they were, all of them, walking into one room, and Zara Kovač — who forgot nothing — recognized every single one.

---

*The long crossing. Not ninety seconds — weeks. Mira practiced guitar alone in the cheap corner room directly above Zara's sound board, throwing off colors she thought only she could see, while Zara, one floor down, felt those colors come through the ceiling and ring. They kept separate doors, separate stairs, separate hours; they never once shared a room. One night Zara stood at the foot of Mira's fire-stair, looked up at the lit window, almost climbed it — and did the normal-person math and went home. When the band finally forms, Zara recognizes Mira on sight as "the room upstairs." The frequencies had been finding each other for a year. The people just kept using different doors.*
