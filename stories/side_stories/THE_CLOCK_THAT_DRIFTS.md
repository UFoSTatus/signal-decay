# THE CLOCK THAT DRIFTS

*Side-Story · Ami Solana × Oren Malik · ~2026, into the sixth chair*
*Register: two-hander / the exhausted timekeeper and the listener*
*Created: 2026-09-23*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. Oren Malik keeps perfect time — a metronome heart that makes him the best pulse-taker in the ER and the worst insomniac in the county, because you cannot switch off a clock that is your own body. Ami Solana hears him as the pulse under the band, the tick so even it's almost inhuman. This is the night she hears the one place his perfect time comes apart — and what's hiding in the gap.*

---

## THE ONE WHO NEVER STOPS TICKING

Oren was the easy one to like and the hard one to know. He talked the most, joked the most, filled silences the way Troy filled them for Kael — the social engine of the band, always moving, sticks going even at rest, a man who seemed made entirely of surface because the surface never stopped.

Ami heard him differently. Through the wall, that first night, he'd been *the clock* — a pulse under everything, metronomic, so even it didn't sound human. And a few weeks in, she'd started to hear the cost of it: that the pulse never, ever stopped. Not when he set the sticks down. Not when he laughed. Not when the practice ended and he was just standing there drinking a flat energy drink and talking too fast. Underneath all of it, the tick. Perfect. Relentless. A metronome with no off switch.

"You don't sleep," Ami said one night, when the others had gone and Oren was still there, wired, spinning a stick, because home was just a quieter place to not sleep.

He grinned, the automatic grin. "Nurse thing. Night shift. You get used to—"

"No," Ami said, kindly. "It's not the night shift. It's the tick. You keep perfect time all the way down and it doesn't turn off. I can hear it. It's going right now. You couldn't stop it if you tried, could you? That's why you don't sleep. You can't put the clock down because you *are* the clock."

Oren stopped spinning the stick. The grin stayed but the eyes behind it went somewhere else — the specific relief-and-terror of being *seen* that Ami kept accidentally giving people in this band.

"...Nobody's ever said it like that," he said. "Yeah. It never stops. Even when I — yeah."

---

## THE GAP IN THE PULSE

"Can I tell you the strange part?" Ami said. "The part I don't understand?"

"There's a stranger part?"

"Your tick is perfect," she said. "*Too* perfect. No human keeps time that clean — everybody drifts a little, speeds up when they're excited, drags when they're tired. You don't. You're a machine. Except—" she frowned, listening to the memory of it "—except sometimes, for like a *fraction* of a beat, you're not. There's a gap. The pulse hitches — not late, not early — it just... skips somewhere I can't follow. Like the beat goes out of the room for a half-second and comes back. I've only caught it a few times. Always when something intense is happening." She looked at him. "Where does it go, Oren? For that half-second. Where does the beat go?"

And Oren Malik — who joked to fill silence, who never let a serious moment sit — went quiet. Genuinely quiet. For once.

"You can hear that," he said. It wasn't a question. It was awe, a little afraid.

"Only barely. Nobody else could. What is it?"

He set the stick down. Rubbed his face with both hands, the gesture of a man deciding to say a true thing he'd never said. "Sometimes," he said, "time... skips. For me. Not the room — me. I'll be doing chest compressions on a coding patient and I'll — I'll already know how it ends. Not guess. *Know.* Like I've seen the next thirty seconds. Or I'll reach for something before it falls. Or I'll answer a question nobody's asked yet." He laughed, no humor in it. "In the ER they think I've got great instincts. Best reflexes on the floor. It's not reflexes. It's — for half a second, sometimes, I'm a little bit ahead. Or behind. Time doesn't sit still around me the way it does for everybody else." He looked at her, the surface all gone now. "I don't tell anyone. It sounds insane. It sounds like the thing you don't say."

"Not here," Ami said quietly. "You can say it here."

---

## THE MOST DORMANT ONE

"Here's what I hear," Ami said. "And I've been thinking about it, because yours is different from the others. Kael's got a door. Jude's got a lid. Zara's got — something old. Mira sees. You—" she searched for it "—you're the least *awake* of any of them. Your thing barely leaks. A half-second skip, once in a while, when it's intense. The others carry theirs every second. Yours is asleep. Mostly. It only wakes up for a heartbeat and then it's gone and you cover it with a joke."

Oren was watching her like she was reading his chart. "Is that — good? Bad?"

"I don't know," Ami said honestly. "I only hear it, I don't know what it *means.* But I'll tell you what I notice. The tick that never stops — the thing that wrecks your sleep — I don't think that's the problem. I think that's the *guard.* I think you keep perfect time so hard, all the way down, every second, because as long as you're holding the beat perfectly steady, the gap can't open. The metronome is how you keep time from skipping. You're not an insomniac because you're broken. You're an insomniac because you're standing guard over a door in the floor, and the second you relax the beat—" she snapped, softly "—it skips. So you never relax the beat. So you never sleep."

The room was silent except for the hum in the corner and, if you were Ami, the faint relentless tick of a man who could not stop counting.

"That's..." Oren started, and stopped. His eyes were wet, which Ami politely did not look at directly. "That's the first thing anybody's ever said that makes it make sense. I thought I was just — broken. Wired wrong. And you're telling me I'm..."

"Holding a door shut with a rhythm," Ami said. "Yeah. And it's costing you every night's sleep you've ever had. I'm not saying open it. God, I'm not saying that — Zara or Mira, someday, the people who understand this stuff. I'm just saying: you're not broken. You're guarding something. There's a difference. And you've been guarding it alone your whole life and nobody even knew you were on duty."

---

## OFF THE CLOCK, FOR A SECOND

Oren didn't say much after that. Which, for Oren, was the loudest thing he could have done.

Before Ami left, he said — quiet, the surface still gone — "The tick. You said you can hear it. Is it —" he hunted for the words "—is it always the same? Like, is it *me?* Is it steady because I'm steady, or steady because I'm scared?"

Ami listened to him for a moment, really listened, the way she listened to a room. "Right now?" she said. "It's a little slower than usual. Just a hair. You've dropped maybe two BPM since you sat down and got honest." She smiled. "That's the closest I've heard you get to resting. You want my honest professional opinion as the ear of this band? The clock slows down when you're not alone with it. You've been keeping time by yourself for thirty years. Maybe the thing that finally lets you sleep isn't stopping the beat. Maybe it's just... not being the only one holding it."

She left him there — a tired man on a stool, sticks quiet for once, his perfect relentless clock running two beats slower than it had in years — and she walked down the side stair into the night thinking about doors and lids and locks and clocks, and about how every single one of the five carried a held-shut thing, and how she, who held nothing, could hear all of them.

That was the job. The sixth chair. Not to make the sound. To hear what the sound was protecting — and to tell each of them, one at a time, in a language only she could speak: *you're not broken. You're guarding something. And you're not on duty alone anymore.*
