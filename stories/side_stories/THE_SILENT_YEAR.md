# THE SILENT YEAR

*Life & Times Side-Quest · Kael · Spring 2020 (age 18)*
*Real event in the background: the COVID-19 lockdown — the world goes quiet*
*Register: quiet, interior; a faint undertone*
*Created: 2026-09-10*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. It is about the year the whole world went as silent as Kael Voss already was — and what stirred in him when, for once, the quiet wasn't only his.*

---

## THE WORLD GOES QUIET

Kael Voss graduated high school in the spring of 2020, which is to say he didn't. There was no walk across a stage, no name called, no cap thrown. There was a cardboard box mailed to the house with a rolled diploma inside it and a tassel that smelled like a factory, and there was a "virtual ceremony" his mother Marissa watched on the laptop at the kitchen table with her hand pressed flat to her chest — the way she'd pressed it to Kael's chest when he was small, to feel his voice through her deafness, back before he stopped giving it.

The world had gone quiet in March. Kael Voss noticed it the way a fish would notice the whole ocean going still.

Because Kael had been the quietest thing in every room he'd ever been in. Eighteen years old, and he spoke maybe a dozen words a day, each one measured, each one spent like the last cash in a pocket. He'd broken his long childhood silence at fifteen — barely, minimally — but he still lived inside it, still held a door shut somewhere behind his sternum, still woke at 3 AM to a silence that had *weight*, that pressed against his skin like a hand.

And now, in the spring of 2020, the whole world joined him.

The highways emptied. Derek's trucking routes went strange and then still — deemed essential, then rerouted, then quiet in a way even a lifelong long-hauler had never heard: interstates at noon with no one on them, rest stops with the lights on and no cars, a whole continent holding its breath. The town outside the window went silent. No planes. No traffic hum. The specific roar of *civilization running* — the sound Kael had spent his life pressed against like a swimmer against a current — just... lowered. Week by week. Until the loudest thing on his street was birdsong, absurd and enormous, birds that must have been singing all along under the engine of the world, suddenly audible, suddenly *huge.*

Kael, who had been alone in his silence for eighteen years, discovered what it was like when the silence was shared.

It was, he found — sitting at his window in April with a mail-order diploma he hadn't opened — almost unbearably beautiful. And it woke something up.

## THE FURNACE, WARM

Here is the thing about Kael's silence that no one — not Marissa, not Derek, not the string of doctors and counselors — ever understood: it was not emptiness. It was *pressure holding back pressure.* There was a furnace in him. A voice — the real one, the one that had made his deaf mother feel melodies through her palm when he was two, the one that had, twice in his life, come out with everything behind it and put a grown man on his knees. He kept it shut. He had to. The one time it fully escaped, something had happened that he would not discuss, and he had learned that his voice was not a gift but a *hazard,* a thing to be contained, a door to be held.

But holding a door is easy when the room beyond it is full of noise. You can pretend the door doesn't exist when the whole world is shouting on your side of it. Kael had spent eighteen years using the roar of civilization as a kind of white noise to bury the furnace under. As long as the world was loud, he could be quiet, and no one would notice that his quiet was a *dam.*

The lockdown took the roar away.

And in the new enormous stillness — the empty highways, the birds, the town holding its breath — the furnace, for the first time in years, could be *heard.* Not by anyone else. By Kael. Late at night, at 3 AM, the hour that had always pressed on him, he'd lie awake and feel it, and now, without the world's noise to mask it, it wasn't the threatening presence it had been. It was — this was the word that came to him, unbidden, in April, and frightened him with its tenderness — it was *company.* Warm. Familiar. Patient. Something inside him that had waited a very long time, and that seemed to say, in the shared silence of a stopped world: *finally. now that it's quiet enough, can you hear me? I've been here the whole time. I'll keep waiting. But — can you hear me now?*

Kael could hear it. That was the terror and the gift of 2020. For the first time, in a world as silent as he was, he could hear the thing inside him, and it did not sound like a hazard.

It sounded like it wanted to sing.

## THE ONE NOTE

He almost did, once. That spring. It's the only real *event* in this quiet story, and it lasted about four seconds, and no one witnessed it, which is the only reason it was safe.

It was late — 3 AM, of course, the hour — and the house was asleep and the street was that impossible new silent and the birds hadn't started yet and Kael was at his open bedroom window in the cool dark, and the furnace was warm in his chest, and the whole world was so *quiet* that for once there was room. Room for a sound. Room he'd never had before, because there'd always been an engine running somewhere that his voice might hurt.

And Kael Voss — who spoke a dozen words a day, who held the door, who had put a man on his knees with a single word and never forgiven himself — opened his mouth in the dark at his window, and let out, very softly, on one breath, a single sustained note.

Not words. Just a tone. The quietest thing. Barely more than a hum, pitched low, aimed at nothing, out the window into the empty street.

And the note went out into the silent world, and — this is the part he'd never be sure of, the part that had no proof, the part that was maybe just an exhausted eighteen-year-old's imagination at 3 AM — the note seemed to *keep going.* To not decay the way a sound should. To travel out over the empty street and the silent town and the birdless dark and *continue,* the way a struck bell keeps ringing, the way something travels when there's nothing in the way to stop it. For four seconds Kael Voss made the smallest sound of his life, and it felt, out there in the stopped world, like it went for miles.

Then a dog barked, somewhere, and the spell broke, and Kael closed his mouth, and his heart was pounding, and he shut the window, and he shut the door in his chest, and he did not do it again.

But he had heard it. He had heard what his voice did when the world was quiet enough to let it travel. And some part of him — the part that had been asleep for eighteen years, the part that would, in six more years, become the voice that binds four other souls into one — some part of him filed it away, in the warm patient dark, and thought: *not yet. But someday. When it's quiet enough. When there's room. When there's a reason.*

---

## WHAT THE QUIET WAS FOR

The world came back, of course. The engines started again. The highways filled, the planes returned, the roar of civilization resumed and buried the furnace back under its white noise, and Kael went back to being the quietest thing in every room, and got a job moving boxes in a warehouse, and drove in silence like his father, and held the door.

But he'd had the year. The one year the world was as silent as he was. And it had taught him something he couldn't have learned any other way, something he wouldn't understand the size of until a night nine — no, six — years later, in a half-empty venue, when four other people's frequencies would reach for his and he'd finally, finally open the door on purpose and let the furnace out where it was *supposed* to go:

That his silence had never been the problem. His silence was just a room with the lights off. And in 2020, when the whole world finally turned *its* lights off too, he'd learned that the room wasn't empty. It never had been. There was something in it that loved him and had waited his whole life and only needed the world to get quiet enough, once, for him to hear it say so.

The lockdown ended. The birds went back under the engine of the world. And Kael Voss carried, out of the silent year, a single secret that he told no one and never forgot:

*My voice can travel. I heard it. When it's quiet enough, and there's a reason, my voice can go for miles.*

He would need that, someday. He didn't know it yet. He just knew the quiet had given him something, and he held onto it, in the warm patient dark behind the door, and waited — the way the thing inside him had always waited — for the world to give him a reason to open his mouth again.

*In spring 2020 the world locked down and went, for a while, genuinely quiet — empty highways, grounded planes, birdsong where engines used to be. For most people it was strange and hard. For Kael Voss, 18, graduating into a cardboard box and a virtual ceremony, the silence was the first time the world matched his own — and in it, for the first time, he could hear the voice he'd spent his life holding back, and it didn't sound like a hazard. It sounded like it had been waiting. No powers "activate." Nothing is explained. A quiet boy hears something in a quiet year, and files it away, six years early.*
