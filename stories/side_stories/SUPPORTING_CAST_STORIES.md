## Scene 1: "The AutoZone Sermon"
### Troy Jackson — Best Friend Since 7th Grade
**Emotional Beat:** Funny, tender, underpinned by something Troy can't name
**What Troy knows:** Something is different about Kael. Has been for twelve years. People *comply* when he speaks. Troy jokes about it. Troy is not joking.
**What Troy doesn't know:** The voice is a cosmic instrument. The compliance is Resonant Command. The friend he's roasting between brake pad displays is carrying the third and final attempt of an ancient soul.

---

The fluorescent lights in AutoZone hummed at a frequency that Troy Jackson had long stopped hearing. He'd worked here four years — long enough that the chemical tang of brake cleaner smelled like home and the Muzak version of "Hotel California" had become a genuine comfort. He was restocking wiper blades on Aisle 3 when his phone buzzed.

**Kael:** *here*

One word. Lowercase. No period. That was Kael — the man communicated in signal flares, not sentences. Troy pocketed his phone and told Marcus at the register he was taking his fifteen.

Kael was sitting on the hood of his Civic in the parking lot, still in his warehouse uniform, the navy polo wrinkled and carrying the ghost of a twelve-hour shift. His headphones hung around his neck like a medical device. He was staring at the sky in that way he had — not looking *at* anything, just looking *through* everything until the world thinned out and something else showed.

"Bro." Troy dropped onto the hood beside him. The car dipped under their combined weight. "You look like you haven't slept since the Clinton administration."

"Just got off."

"At four in the afternoon?"

"Double."

"Twenty-four hours of boxes?"

"Sixteen. Felt like twenty-four."

Troy let the silence sit for exactly three seconds — his maximum capacity. "Okay so I gotta tell you what happened with this dude yesterday because it's WILD."

And he was off. Troy Jackson did not have conversations; he had *performances*. Hands flying, body shifting on the car hood, voice climbing and dropping with the narrative. The story involved a customer who'd come in insisting his '09 Altima needed a catalytic converter from a '14 Mustang, and Troy's escalating attempts to explain that cars were not, in fact, interchangeable, and the man's escalating certainty that Troy was part of a conspiracy to keep him from his Mustang catalytic converter.

Kael listened. That was the thing about Kael — he *listened* like it was a skill he'd trained. Not performing attention. Actually metabolizing every word. His eyes tracked Troy's gestures, the corner of his mouth twitching in the places Troy had timed for laughs.

"—and this man, right, this man looks me in the EYE, and he goes, 'I know you got 'em in the back.' Like I'm hiding catalytic converters. Like there's a black market for catalytic converters and I, Troy Deshawn Jackson, am the kingpin. I'm making six figures off converter crime and I'm choosing to ALSO work at AutoZone for the health benefits—"

Kael laughed. Actually laughed. The low, quiet one that came from his chest, not his throat. It changed the air. Troy could feel it — had always been able to feel it — the way Kael's laugh was *warmer* than a laugh should be. Not louder. Not bigger. Just... warmer. Like the parking lot gained two degrees.

"So what'd you do?" Kael asked.

"I said — I said, 'Sir, I'm going to need you to come with me,' and I walked him to the computer and I pulled up the parts diagram and I SHOWED him, Altima versus Mustang, two completely different—"

"No, I mean. Did you give him the right one?"

Troy paused. "Yeah. Obviously."

"He was happy?"

"Man was ecstatic. Practically hugged me."

"There you go."

And Kael said it in that way — *that way* — where the words were simple but they landed in your chest like instructions. *There you go.* Like the universe had confirmed something. Troy watched a woman walking across the parking lot stop mid-stride, turn her head toward them, and then keep walking — like she'd heard a sound she couldn't place.

Troy had been cataloging these moments since eighth grade. He had no spreadsheet, no data — just a running tally in his gut. The time at the movies when the line was getting hostile and Kael said "We're good" and the entire crowd just... *was* good. The time at Marcus's party when two guys were about to fight and Kael, without raising his voice, said "Sit down" and they sat. The time — the one that really got him — at Kael's grandmother's funeral when Kael stood up and said a few words and the entire church went silent in a way that wasn't grief. It was something else. Something deeper. Like the room had been given permission to stop pretending.

"Can I ask you something?" Troy said.

"You're going to anyway."

"Why are you still at the warehouse?"

Kael looked at him. The headphones hummed faintly. "It's a job."

"It's a *holding pattern*, bro. You've been in the same holding pattern since graduation. Same apartment. Same shift. Same—"

"Same best friend who works at AutoZone?"

"Okay, OUCH, but also fair." Troy grinned. The thing about roasting Kael was that Kael always fired back at exactly the right caliber — just enough to sting, never enough to wound. "I'm serious though. Elena called me."

Something shifted in Kael's posture. The easy lean became less easy. "What did she say?"

"She said you've been — her words — 'retreating into your shell like a depressed hermit crab.' Those were her exact words. 'Depressed hermit crab.' She's studying psychology and this is the clinical language she uses."

"I'm not retreating."

"You played open mic at The Frequency three months ago and then never went back."

"It was one open mic."

"Bro." Troy turned his whole body. "KAEL. When you sang that night — I'm not even playing right now — when you sang, the bartender stopped pouring drinks. Not like, paused. STOPPED. Put the bottle down. The girl at the bar next to me had TEARS. On her face. From one verse of a song she'd never heard. I've seen you do the Jedi mind trick thing a thousand times but that was different. That was—"

He stopped because Kael's face had gone completely still. Not angry. Not sad. *Closed*. Like a door shutting behind his eyes.

"It was just a song, Troy."

"Nah. Nah nah nah. Don't do that. Don't 'just a song' me. Something happened that night. Something *happened* when you opened your mouth. And then you came off that stage and you looked like you'd seen God and He gave you bad news, and you haven't gone back since."

The parking lot was very quiet. The AutoZone sign buzzed. A pigeon landed on the curb and then immediately flew away, which pigeons in this lot never did — they were fearless, obese pigeons who'd stare down a truck.

Kael exhaled. "I don't know what happened."

"But something did."

"Yeah." It was barely a word. A vibration more than a syllable. "Something did."

Troy wanted to push. Every atom in his body wanted to push. But he'd known Kael for twelve years, and he knew that pushing Kael was like pushing a door that opened inward — the harder you shoved, the tighter it sealed. So instead, Troy did what Troy always did. He pivoted.

"Alright. You know what? This is what's going to happen. Saturday night, The Frequency has a lineup. Three bands. Good ones — Kwame told me. You're going to come with me. You're not going to sing. You're just going to WATCH. Like a normal person who goes to shows. And I'm going to buy you one beer. And then you can go home and be a hermit crab. But you're going. That's not a request."

Kael looked at him for a long moment. Then the corner of his mouth moved — not quite a smile, but the architecture of one.

"Fine."

"THAT'S WHAT I'M TALKING ABOUT."

Troy was already off the hood, already in motion, phone out, texting Kwame. He didn't notice the way the parking lot seemed to brighten for half a second when Kael said *fine* — as if the word had mass, had shape, had reorganized the photons.

He didn't notice. But he felt it. He always felt it. And one day — one day very soon — he was going to be able to say *I told you so* to literally everyone who'd ever dismissed him when he said: "My boy has something. I don't know what. But something."

Troy Jackson had been saying it since seventh grade. The universe was about to prove him right.

## Scene 2: "The Parking Lot at 1 AM (Seven Years Later)"
### Nina Osei — The Almost
**Emotional beat:** Tender, electric, terrifying
**What Nina knows:** She felt something at 17 — a pull, a resistance, a door closing behind Kael's eyes when she got too close. Seven years later, she feels it again. Stronger.
**What Nina doesn't know:** The soul-resistance she felt was Kel-Voss protecting the container. The "something" about Kael's voice is Resonant Command waking up.

---

Nina Osei had not planned to be in this parking lot.

She'd planned to be home by eleven, post-shift, mask on, heating pad on her lower back, watching whatever Netflix auto-played while she decompressed from twelve hours of clinic work. She was a nurse practitioner at Mercy General — three years into the job, good at it, proud of it, tired in the specific way that healthcare workers are tired: not just in the body but in the soul, because you spend all day holding space for people's worst moments and then you drive home and nobody holds space for yours.

She'd stopped for gas. That was the first mistake. The Shell station on Archer, the one that was open 24 hours and always smelled like burnt coffee and floor cleaner. She was standing at the pump, watching the numbers climb, when a Civic pulled in two pumps over and a man got out and the world did something it hadn't done in seven years.

It *tilted*.

Not visibly. Not measurably. But Nina felt the axis of the parking lot shift, the way you feel a plane bank when your eyes are closed. She turned her head before she processed why, and there he was.

Kael Voss. Twenty-four years old. Still wearing a warehouse uniform. Still moving like he was trying to take up less space than his body occupied. Still carrying that *weight* — the one she'd spent six months at seventeen trying to name and failing.

He didn't see her. He was putting his card in the reader, headphones on, eyes down. And for three full seconds, Nina considered getting in her car and driving away. Because seven years had passed and she'd built an entire life that didn't include this particular ache, and she was tired, and her back hurt, and she knew — *she knew* — that if she walked over there, the feeling would come back.

She walked over there.

"Kael."

He turned. The headphones came off in one motion. His eyes found her face and there it was — the recognition, the warmth, the immediate closing of something behind the warmth. All in under a second. She'd forgotten how fast he processed. How the intelligence in those eyes moved at a speed his words never matched.

"Nina."

Her name in his mouth. She'd forgotten that too. The way he said it — not special, not performative, just... *true*. Like he was confirming something to the universe. *Yes. That's her name. I know this.*

"Hi." She smiled. It was genuine. She was glad to see him. That was the problem — she was *glad*, and gladness in Nina Osei was not a small thing. She didn't do small emotions. She did directness and full force and the kind of honesty that made lesser men flinch. Kael had never flinched. That was what got her at seventeen. That was what was getting her now.

"How are you?" she asked.

"Good. You?"

"I'm good. I'm — I work at Mercy now. Nurse practitioner."

Something crossed his face. Not surprise — he didn't look surprised. He looked like he already knew. "That's... that's great, Nina."

And the way he said it — *that's great, Nina* — the pump beside them clicked off. Her pump, which had been slowly climbing toward her pre-set limit of forty dollars. It clicked off at thirty-one. She looked at it. Looked back at him.

"Did you just—"

"What?"

"Nothing." She shook her head. Of course not. People didn't make gas pumps click by saying things. That would be insane.

"You want to sit for a minute?" she asked, and she didn't know why she asked it, except that the parking lot was quiet and the air had that specific quality of 1 AM air — honest, stripped, the daytime performances burned away.

They sat on the curb between their cars. The concrete was cold. The Shell sign buzzed overhead, orange and arterial. Nina pulled her jacket tighter and looked at the man beside her and tried to reconcile seventeen-year-old Kael — the quiet boy who'd driven her down empty highways at midnight because neither of them wanted to go home — with this twenty-four-year-old version, who was the same but *more*. More something.

"You look different," she said.

"I look the same."

"No. You *look* the same. But you *feel* different." She wasn't afraid to say things like that. She'd never been afraid to say the real thing. It was what made her good at her job and terrible at small talk.

Kael's jaw tightened. Just slightly. "How do I feel different?"

"Heavier. Not like — not weight. Like... density. Like there's more of you inside you than there used to be."

He didn't answer. She watched him watch the cars on Archer, the red and white lights streaming past like cells through an artery, and she thought about the last time they'd sat like this. Seventeen. His car. The conversation that went three hours without either of them noticing. The moment he'd leaned toward her and she'd leaned toward him and then — the door. The closing. His eyes going somewhere else, somewhere she couldn't follow, and his body pulling back like it was on a string.

"Why did you pull away?" she asked. Not accusatory. Just curious. Seven years of curious.

"Nina—"

"I'm not angry. I was angry at seventeen. I'm not angry anymore. I just want to know. Because I've dated since then, and I've loved since then, and nothing has ever felt like those six months felt, and I have a theory that whatever was between us was real and you knew it was real and something *else* made you leave."

The parking lot went very quiet. Not normal quiet — *dense* quiet. Like the air had become a material. Nina's ears rang faintly, the way they did in the anechoic chamber during her audiology rotation. Pure silence. Unnatural.

"I can't explain it," Kael said.

"Try."

He looked at her. His eyes were dark and deep and there was something behind them that she'd been trying to name since she was seventeen years old. Not sadness. Not fear. Something older. Something that didn't belong in a twenty-four-year-old warehouse worker.

"There's something about me," he said slowly, "that I don't understand yet. And until I understand it, I can't — I couldn't — I didn't want it to touch you."

"Touch me how?"

"I don't know."

"Kael. I'm a nurse practitioner. I spent four years learning to assess what's wrong with people based on what they can't articulate. You're not depressed. You're not avoidant. You're not on the spectrum. I ruled all of those out at seventeen. Something else is going on with you and it's—"

She stopped. Because his eyes had changed. The dark irises seemed to deepen, and for one disorienting second she felt like she was looking at something much larger than a man sitting on a curb. Something vast. Something waiting.

"You're gonna be something," she said. The words came out of her mouth without permission, an exact echo of what she'd said at seventeen — the sentence that had apparently lived in her chest for seven years, fully intact, waiting for this exact parking lot.

"You said that before," he murmured.

"I'll keep saying it until you believe me."

The buzzing of the Shell sign changed pitch. Neither of them noticed. But a man filling his tank three pumps over put down his squeegee and looked around, confused, trying to find the source of a sound he couldn't describe — a low, warm hum that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere, that made him suddenly, inexplicably, want to call his mother.

Kael stood up. Offered his hand. She took it — his palm was warm and dry and she felt something move through the contact, a vibration so subtle it might have been her own pulse.

"Can I call you?" she asked.

"Yeah." A half-breath. "Yeah, you can call me."

She got in her car. He got in his. They drove in opposite directions on Archer Street, and Nina Osei gripped the steering wheel with both hands because they were shaking, and she whispered to the empty car: "What *are* you?"

The radio, which she hadn't turned on, played a single note — a low, clear tone — and then went silent.

## Scene 3: "Raylan and the Forklift"
### Raylan Meeks — Warehouse Coworker
**Emotional beat:** Quiet, eerie, a little funny
**What Raylan knows:** Kael is a good kid. Too smart for this job. People do what he says. That's just natural authority. Some guys have it.
**What Raylan doesn't know:** Anything. Not a single supernatural thing. And that's exactly the point.

---

Raylan Meeks had been working the 6 PM to 6 AM shift at the Regional Distribution Hub for seven years. He was thirty-one, divorced, father of one (Ella, six, the absolute center of the universe), and perfectly, contentedly, irreversibly average. He read James Patterson on break. He smoked Marlboro Lights by the loading dock. He had three tattoos — one he regretted, one he loved, one he forgot about until someone mentioned it. He drove a Silverado with 180,000 miles and a check engine light that had been on since the Obama administration.

He liked working with Kael.

This wasn't a complicated feeling. Some people at the warehouse were loud. Some were lazy. Some needed managing. Kael was none of those things. Kael showed up, did his work, kept his headphones on, and existed in a companionable silence that Raylan — who had exhausted his social capacity during his marriage — found genuinely restorative.

They'd been on the same dock for three years. In that time, Raylan had learned the following about Kael Voss: he was twenty-four, he sang sometimes (under his breath, barely audible over the conveyor), he had a sister in college, and he drank his coffee black from the vending machine, which Raylan considered an act of either courage or masochism.

They communicated primarily through nods.

This was a Monday in October. The trucks were running late — the 2 AM inbound from Columbus was stuck somewhere on I-70, which meant the loading dock sat empty, which meant Raylan and Kael were doing busywork: restacking pallets, running inventory scans, generally performing the theater of productivity that satisfied the cameras without satisfying anyone's soul.

"Gonna rain tomorrow," Raylan said, scanning a pallet of paper towels.

"Yep," Kael said.

This constituted a rich exchange by their standards.

The problem started with the forklift.

Danny Kovacs — no relation to anyone important, just a twenty-two-year-old with more energy than sense — was running the Hyster in Aisle 9 when he clipped a racking upright. The sound was wrong immediately. Not a bang — a *groan*. Metal bending in a way that metal in a warehouse should never bend. Raylan looked up and saw the top rack — three pallets of bottled water, maybe two tons total — start to lean.

Time did something funny. Raylan, who'd been in enough close calls to know the feeling, experienced the moment in the specific slow-motion of adrenaline — his legs already moving, his mouth already opening to yell, his brain already calculating the distance between the falling rack and Danny's forklift.

But Kael was faster.

Not physically faster — he wasn't closer to Danny. He was further away, in fact, a full aisle over. But he spoke.

"Danny. Stop."

Two words. Not shouted — spoken. At a volume that should have been inaudible over the groan of bending metal and the beeping of the Hyster's reverse alarm. But Danny heard it. Raylan heard it. The two guys on the other side of the warehouse heard it. Every person in the building heard Kael Voss say "Danny. Stop." as if he'd spoken directly into each of their inner ears simultaneously.

Danny stopped. Killed the engine. Sat frozen, hands on the wheel.

The rack continued its lean — two degrees, five degrees — and then, with a shuddering *clang*, the safety chain caught and the whole structure rocked back upright. The bottled water shifted, settled, and was still.

Nobody moved for three seconds.

Then Danny stumbled off the forklift, white-faced, and everyone started moving at once — checking the rack, checking Danny, checking for damage. Raylan walked over to Kael, who was standing exactly where he'd been, scanner still in hand, face neutral.

"How'd you know it would stop?" Raylan asked.

"What?"

"The rack. How'd you know the chain would hold? 'Cause you told Danny to stop but you didn't tell him to run. You knew it wasn't going to fall."

Kael looked at him. For a moment Raylan saw something in the kid's eyes that didn't belong there — not fear, not surprise, but a *calculation*. Like Kael was deciding how much to reveal.

"Lucky guess," Kael said.

"That tracks," Raylan said. And he meant it. Because the alternative — that Kael had somehow known, with certainty, that the rack would hold; that his voice had carried impossibly through the noise; that every person in the building had heard two quietly spoken words as if they'd been shouted from a megaphone — the alternative was insane.

Raylan Meeks was not an insane man. He was a thirty-one-year-old who read James Patterson and drove a Silverado. He did not entertain insane alternatives. He filed the incident under "Kael has good instincts" and went back to scanning paper towels.

But later — on his smoke break, 4 AM, standing in the loading dock doorway while the rain Kael had correctly predicted spattered the concrete — Raylan replayed it. Not the near-miss. The *voice*.

He'd heard Kael speak a thousand times. "Hey." "Yep." "Could be worse." Normal words at normal volumes. But the two words in Aisle 9 had been something different. They'd been... *thick*. Dense. Like each syllable carried information beyond its literal meaning. Like "Danny. Stop." actually meant something closer to *you are safe, be still, the world will not hurt you while I'm speaking*.

Raylan lit a second cigarette, which he never did, and thought about his daughter. How when Ella was scared of thunderstorms, he'd hold her and say "It's just noise, baby" and she'd believe him. How a parent's voice could reshape a child's reality. How some people's voices carried more weight than the words they contained.

"Could be worse," he said to the rain, and went back inside.

## Scene 4: "How Bad, Scale of One to Ten"
### Sage Kowalski — The Only One Who Knows
**Emotional beat:** Dark humor covering enormous tenderness
**What Sage knows:** Everything. The entities. The dead musicians. The whole deal. Not the cosmic mechanics, but the truth.
**What Sage doesn't know:** The *scale* of what's coming. They believe Mira. They've always believed Mira. But they think "Mira sees ghosts" is the full story. It's the preface.

---

Sage Kowalski's apartment smelled like rubbing alcohol and incense, which was either a tattoo parlor vibe or a crime scene vibe depending on your worldview. They were halfway through a flash piece on their own thigh — a minimalist moth, wings open — when their phone buzzed three times in rapid succession.

**Mira:** hey
**Mira:** are you up
**Mira:** nvm

The "nvm" was the tell. Mira texted "nvm" when she most needed to talk. It was her emergency flare fired backwards — *please come find me, I'm pretending I'm fine*.

Sage put down the tattoo gun. Wiped their thigh. Didn't even bandage — just pulled on black jeans over the fresh ink, grabbed their keys and a hoodie, and walked two blocks in the rain to Mira's apartment.

The door was unlocked. This was not good. Mira locked everything — doors, windows, the cabinet above her fridge where she kept the good tea. The girl lived in a perpetual state of *sealed*. An unlocked door meant she'd either forgotten or stopped caring, and Sage didn't love either option.

They found Mira on the kitchen floor. Not collapsed — *sitting*. Cross-legged, guitar across her lap, eyes focused on a point in the air approximately three feet in front of her face. The apartment was freezing. Not metaphorically — Sage could see their own breath.

"How bad," Sage said, closing the door behind them. "Scale of one to ten."

"Seven." Mira's voice was distant. Present but thin.

"Seven with the door unlocked or seven and the door was a separate issue?"

"Seven and a half."

Sage sat down on the kitchen floor across from her. The linoleum was viciously cold. They pulled their hood up, crossed their arms, and waited.

The entities were doing something. Sage couldn't see them — had never seen them, in nine years of friendship — but they could feel the room's wrongness. The temperature was the obvious part. The less obvious part was the way sounds behaved in here — their voice had a faint echo that Mira's kitchen acoustically should not produce. Like the space was larger than the walls suggested. Like there were more surfaces for sound to bounce off.

"Keys is being difficult," Mira said.

Keys. The ghost pianist. The one who'd driven away Cam in high school by sabotaging his equipment. The one who was, in Sage's private taxonomy, "the possessive one."

"Difficult how?"

"He doesn't want me to go to the show on Saturday."

"The show at The Frequency? The one you already said yes to?"

"He played dissonance over everything I practiced tonight. Every time I tried to run through my set, he'd — there." Mira's eyes tracked something Sage couldn't see. Something moving in the corner of the kitchen. "He's doing it now."

The guitar string — the low E — vibrated. Once. By itself. Sage watched it move and felt the hair on their arms stand straight up. Nine years. They'd believed Mira for nine years. But *belief* was abstract; a guitar string moving by itself was concrete. It was one thing to believe your best friend could see dead musicians. It was another thing to watch a dead musician pluck your best friend's guitar in your line of sight.

"Keys," Sage said to the empty corner of the kitchen. "Buddy. She's going to the show. You can either be cool about it or you can be a dick about it, but she's going."

The temperature dropped another degree. Mira's breath caught.

"Did that help or hurt?" Sage asked.

"He's... confused. He doesn't know what to do with you. You're not scared of him and it breaks his brain."

"I'm not scared of a dead guy who peaked during Prohibition." Sage pulled out their phone. "I'm ordering pizza. You haven't eaten."

"I'm not hungry."

"That wasn't a question."

They ordered Mira's regular — margherita, extra basil — and then settled back against the cabinet and watched their best friend sit on a cold kitchen floor, talking to ghosts, and tried very hard not to think about the word that kept surfacing in their mind: *escalation*.

Because the entities were getting louder. Sage didn't need to see them to know. Six months ago, the cold spots were rare — once a week, maybe twice. Now they were constant when Sage visited. The guitar strings moved more often. Mira's spacing-out episodes lasted longer. And Mira's playing — the stuff she practiced at home, the recordings she occasionally sent at 3 AM — sounded different. Layered. Like there were three guitars when there was only one. Harmonics that shouldn't exist on a single instrument.

Sage had googled "acoustic anomalies" and "overtone singing" and "harmonic resonance" and none of it explained what they heard on those recordings. Because what they heard wasn't technique. It was *accompaniment*. Other instruments playing alongside Mira that weren't there.

"Can I tell you something?" Sage said.

"Always."

"The recording you sent me Thursday night. The one at, what, 3 AM. The one you said was 'just noodling.'"

"Yeah?"

"There were drums on it, Mira."

Silence. The cold room seemed to contract.

"I didn't—"

"I know you didn't add them. I know you were alone. I know your apartment doesn't have drums. I played it back four times. There are drums. And what sounds like a stand-up bass doing a walking line under your riff. And something I can't identify — it sounds like a human voice but it's not singing words, it's singing *tones*. Like Gregorian chant but sadder."

Mira was staring at her. The guitar hadn't moved.

"How much of what you record at 3 AM," Sage said carefully, "is you?"

The question hung in the cold air between them. Sage watched Mira's face go through four expressions in two seconds — fear, guilt, relief, fear again — and then settle on the one Sage knew best: *exhaustion*.

"Some of it's me. Some of it's them. I can't always tell anymore."

"Is that new? The not-being-able-to-tell?"

"It's getting... blurrier. Like, I'll start playing and then I'm somewhere else and when I come back there's twenty minutes of recording I don't remember making. And it's — Sage, it's *good*. It's better than anything I can do on my own. And that scares me more than anything else."

The pizza arrived. Sage got up, paid the delivery guy (who shivered at the door and said "Your AC's cranked, huh?"), and brought the box back to the kitchen floor. They ate in silence — Sage chewing deliberately, Mira picking at her slice the way she always did when the entities were close, as if eating was a ritual she was performing rather than a need she was meeting.

"I'm going with you Saturday," Sage said.

"You don't have to—"

"I'm going. And I'm going to stand right next to the stage. And if Keys or anyone else starts making the room weird, I'll be there." Sage took another bite. "Also, I want to see you play for a live audience. You've been hiding in this kitchen for too long."

"The audience might notice something."

"The audience will notice a talented guitarist playing her ass off. That's all they're equipped to notice. Humans are really, really bad at seeing things they don't have a category for." Sage paused. "I was, too. Until you gave me one."

Mira's eyes were wet. She didn't cry — Sage had seen her cry maybe four times in nine years — but her eyes were wet, and that was enough.

"Thank you," Mira whispered. "For believing me."

## Scene 5: "The String That Played Itself"
### Iris Delgado — Guitar Student
**Emotional beat:** Joy, wonder, a flicker of unease
**What Iris knows:** Miss Mira is the best teacher she's ever had. A little weird. Spaces out sometimes. The room gets cold.
**What Iris doesn't know:** She is practicing guitar in a room populated by dead musicians who are *critiquing her form*.

---

Iris Delgado was going to nail the Polyphia riff if it killed her.

She'd been working on it for three weeks — the tapping section from "G.O.A.T." that sounded like a video game boss fight and required fingers that moved independently of each other in ways God clearly hadn't intended. Her fingertips were calloused and sore. Her left hand cramped after twenty minutes. She'd watched Tim Henson's tutorial video forty-seven times and developed a parasocial relationship with his fretting hand.

But she was *close*. She could feel it.

"Okay, show me," Miss Mira said, sitting cross-legged on the couch with her own guitar in her lap, green tea steaming on the side table. The late afternoon light came through the window in golden bars. This was Iris's favorite part of the week — Wednesday at 4 PM, Mira's apartment, the only place where music felt like it meant something instead of just sounding like something.

Iris played. Got through the first eight bars clean. Hit the tapping section. Fumbled at bar twelve — her ring finger landing a fret too high — and stopped, groaning.

"UGH. I keep missing the transition."

"You're rushing. Your right hand is a beat ahead of your left at the changeover. Slow it down — feel where your right hand *wants* to go, and then make your left hand meet it there."

"Can you show me?"

Mira picked up her guitar. And this was the thing about Miss Mira that Iris could never adequately describe to her friends: watching her play was like watching someone speak a language they'd been born into. No hesitation. No calculation. Her fingers just *went*. The Polyphia riff came out of her guitar like water out of a faucet — smooth, inevitable, the tapping section flowing without any of the seams that Iris fought against.

But that wasn't the weird part. The weird part was that halfway through the riff, Mira's eyes went somewhere else.

Iris had seen this before. Maybe once a lesson, sometimes twice, Mira would be playing and then her focus would drift — not like she was distracted but like she was *receiving*. Her eyes would track to a specific point in the room — always the same corner, near the bookshelf — and her playing would change. Subtly. Extra notes that weren't in the original. Harmonics that shimmered in the air a half-second after the string should have stopped ringing.

"Miss Mira?"

Mira blinked. Came back. "Sorry. Got lost in it."

"You added stuff."

"Did I?"

"At the end — you did this whole thing that's not in the song. It sounded like... two guitars?" Iris tilted her head. "How did you do that on one guitar?"

"Harmonics. I'll show you some other time." Mira sipped her tea. Quick pivot. Iris noticed but didn't push — Miss Mira got weird about certain things, and Iris had learned to orbit the weirdness rather than collide with it.

They worked through the riff four more times. Iris got closer each attempt — bar twelve was smoothing out, the transition starting to feel less like a cliff and more like a hill. On the fourth try, she played it clean. All the way through. No fumble.

"YES!" Iris threw both hands in the air. "Did you HEAR that?! Lol I literally just—"

"I heard it. That was really good, Iris."

"Really good or 'encouragement' good? Because my school teacher does the encouragement thing where everything is great even when it's garbage—"

"It was really good. Your timing in the tapping section was tight. Your tone was clean. You're ready to try it at tempo."

Iris beamed. She was seventeen and had not yet learned to be restrained about joy. Joy was still a full-body experience for her — the grin splitting her face, the leg bouncing, the urge to call her abuela immediately.

She reached for her water bottle and her elbow knocked Mira's tuner off the side table. "Oh — sorry, let me—"

She bent to pick it up. And stopped.

The E string on her guitar — her guitar, lying flat on the couch cushion beside her, untouched — was vibrating. She could see it. A tiny oscillation, the string blurring faintly, producing a low hum that she could feel more than hear.

"Um." Iris stared. "Miss Mira?"

"Yeah?"

"My string is..." She pointed.

Mira looked. And Iris saw something on her teacher's face that she'd never seen before: a flicker of genuine *alarm*. It lasted half a second — less — and then Mira's expression smoothed into casual interest.

"Sympathetic resonance," Mira said evenly. "When I was playing, the frequencies from my guitar made yours vibrate. It happens with instruments in close proximity. The strings are tuned to the same pitches, so they pick up each other's vibrations."

"But you stopped playing like thirty seconds ago."

"The vibration takes a while to dampen." Mira met her eyes. Calm. Authoritative. The tone of someone explaining physics. "It's normal. It actually means both our guitars are well intonated."

Iris looked at the string. It had stopped moving. She looked back at Mira.

"That is SO cool."

"Right?" Mira smiled. And it was a real smile — the small one she saved for moments when Iris's enthusiasm reminded her of something. "Physics is cool."

They moved on to scales. Iris forgot about the string within minutes, replaced by the challenge of running through A minor at tempo without tripping on the sixth degree. Mira corrected her hand position twice, demonstrated a trick for the pinky stretch, and was her usual focused, slightly intense, completely wonderful self.

When the lesson ended, Iris packed up her guitar — a secondhand Fender her abuela had saved three months to buy — and lingered at the door.

"Miss Mira?"

"Yeah?"

"You're like... the best teacher I've ever had. No cap. You actually make me feel like I can do this."

Mira's face did something complicated. Like she was receiving a gift she wasn't sure she deserved.

"You *can* do this, Iris. You're talented."

"Okay but you have to say that because you're my teacher."

"No. I don't have to say anything. I'm saying it because it's true."

Iris grinned, waved, and practically bounced down the hallway. Mira closed the door and stood in her apartment and listened to the silence.

It wasn't silent. It was never silent.

In the corner by the bookshelf, something shifted. A presence. Keys, maybe. Or the bassist. Or the one Mira simply called The Whisperer, who had no instrument but hummed in a register that made her teeth ache.

"You can't do that," Mira said to the empty room. "You can't move her strings. She's a kid. She's *my* student. You leave her alone."

The temperature dropped one degree. Then rose back. An acknowledgment. Not an apology — the entities didn't apologize — but an acknowledgment that the boundary had been stated.

Mira sat down on the couch and pressed her palms into her eyes and thought about Iris Delgado — seventeen, bright, hungry, unbroken — and made a promise to the room that she would keep this girl safe from whatever she herself had become.

## Scene 6: "Baby, You Need to Go to Church"

"Mom."

"Yes. I'm eating."

"I'm not Rochelle."

"No. You're not. But I watched that happen. I was fifteen when they took her. And when you started saying the same things she said — 'Mama, there's someone in the corner,' 'Mama, they won't stop playing' — do you understand what that felt like? I was terrified. Not of you. *For* you."

The line was quiet for a long time. Sandra could hear Mira breathing — the slightly uneven breath she had when she was trying not to cry.

"The psychiatrists couldn't help because there's nothing wrong with me," Mira said.

"I know."

"The medications didn't help because there's nothing to medicate."

"I know, baby."

"Then what do I DO, Mom? If church can't fix it and doctors can't fix it and ignoring it doesn't make it stop — what do I *do*?"

Sandra closed her eyes. The cornbread was browning. The oven light made orange patterns on the back of her eyelids.

"And you come to dinner on Sunday. I'm making jollof rice and your daddy's making his stir-fry and I don't care if you bring the whole ghost orchestra, you're sitting at this table and eating with your family."

Mira laughed. A real one — wet, broken, beautiful. "You can't just cook away the supernatural, Mom."

"Watch me." Sandra checked the oven. Golden. Perfect. "Six o'clock. Don't be late. And eat something before then — you sound thin."

"You can hear thinness?"

"I'm your mother. I can hear everything."

They said goodbye. Sandra hung up the phone and stood in her kitchen and pressed both hands to her face and breathed. The cornbread smelled like home. The silence of the house pressed against her like water.

*Lord,* she prayed — not formally, not with bowed head, but standing upright at her counter with flour on her apron and tears on her cheeks — *Lord, I don't know what's in that room with my daughter. But You do. And whatever it is, let her be stronger than it. Let her stay. Let her stay here with us.*

The oven light flickered once.

Sandra looked at it. Looked away. Pulled the cornbread out and set it on the trivet and did not think about what might have made the light flicker, because thinking about it would make it real, and real was a country she was not yet ready to move to.

## Scene 7: "The Break Room at 3 AM"

The break room at Mercy General smelled like burnt coffee, vending machine pizza, and institutional despair. Her shift had four hours left.

## Scene 8: "Exceptional Clinical Instinct"

The fluorescent light above him flickered. Once. As if acknowledging.

## Scene 10: "Sunday Jam"
### Darius Cole — The Brother From Another
**Emotional beat:** Warm, grounding, with an edge of something neither of them can name
**What Darius knows:** Jude plays guitar like he's channeling something. There's an edge in the heaviness — a drop where the music stops being music and starts being *weight*. Darius has been pulling back from that edge for twelve years.
**What Darius doesn't know:** The seismic resonance. The fact that when Jude plays at full intensity, the vibrations pass through the instrument into the ground. That the "weight" Darius feels is literal.

Darius Cole's garage was the most organized space in the neighborhood — tools on pegboard, workbench clear, two folding chairs facing each other across a frayed rug that had absorbed enough spilled beer and guitar sweat to qualify as an artifact. The garage door was open to the October evening, the street golden with dying light, the smell of leaves and someone's barbecue carrying on the breeze.

Jude arrived at exactly 5 PM, because Jude arrived everywhere at exactly the time he said he would. Prison had either taught him punctuality or refined what was already there — Darius had never figured out which.

"Hey."

"Hey."

This was the full extent of their greeting ritual. Twelve years of friendship had burned away everything unnecessary. Jude sat down. Plugged in his guitar — an ESP Eclipse in black that he'd saved four months of demolition pay for. Darius was already plugged in, his Telecaster warm and honey-colored across his lap.

"What are we doing?" Darius asked.

"Let's just play."

So they played. No plan, no setlist, no discussion. Darius started a blues progression in E — comfortable, lived-in, the kind of thing their fingers could do while their minds wandered. Jude layered on top, riff fragments and sustained bends, finding the spaces between Darius's chords and filling them with something darker. Not darker as in heavier — darker as in *deeper*. Like Jude's guitar spoke from a lower floor of the same building.

This was what they'd been doing since they were fifteen. Darius had left his acoustic at Jude's house on a Thursday. By Monday, Jude was playing power chords. By the following Thursday, he was downtuning. By the month after that, he was doing things on the guitar that Darius — who'd been playing since twelve — couldn't follow.

Not technically. Technically, Darius was still better on paper. His blues phrasing was cleaner, his bends more precise, his knowledge of theory more developed. But Jude had something Darius couldn't learn: *gravity*. When Jude played, the guitar didn't sing — it *pulled*. Like the notes had mass. Like each chord was a physical event in the room rather than a sonic one.

They played for twenty minutes without speaking. The blues progression evolved — got heavier, more modal, Darius shifting to open tuning while Jude dropped to a low B that made the windows sympathetically buzz. A neighborhood kid rode by on a bike, slowed down, listened for a moment, and then kept going with an expression of confused respect.

"I want to try something," Jude said.

"Go."

Jude closed his eyes. Darius watched him — the broad shoulders settling, the thick forearms relaxing, the steel-toed boots flat on the concrete. And then Jude began to play something Darius had never heard before.

It started as a riff — slow, deliberate, each note placed with the precision of a demolition charge. But it built. Layer on layer, the distortion thickening, the rhythm becoming something beyond 4/4 — a polyrhythmic pulse that Darius couldn't count. His fingers tried to follow. Found the key. Played rhythm beneath Jude's lead.

And then the *drop* came.

Darius knew the drop. He'd felt it a hundred times in these jam sessions — the moment where Jude's playing crossed a threshold and the music stopped being music and started being *weather*. A pressure change. A density shift. The air in the garage became heavier. Darius's ears popped, the way they did on an airplane descent.

He looked down. The beer on the workbench was trembling. Not sloshing — *trembling*. Concentric circles spreading from the center of the liquid, the way they did in *Jurassic Park* when the T-Rex was coming.

"Jude."

Jude didn't hear him. His eyes were closed. The riff was escalating — not faster, not louder, but *deeper*. Darius could feel it in his sternum. In his teeth. The rug beneath their chairs was vibrating. The tools on the pegboard rattled.

"JUDE."

Jude's eyes opened. The riff stopped. The garage went silent — not quiet, *silent*, the way a room goes silent after a thunderclap, all the ambient noise sucked out and replaced with ringing.

They looked at each other.

"What was that?" Darius asked. His voice was careful. Not scared — Darius Cole didn't scare, not from a man he'd loved since he was fourteen — but *careful*. The way you're careful around something you don't understand.

"I don't know." Jude's voice was low. Rough. Like he'd been screaming instead of playing.

"The beer was moving. My tools were rattling. The *floor* was—"

"I know."

"How long has it been doing that?"

Jude was quiet for a moment. Then: "A while."

"A while. Like weeks? Months?"

"Years, D."

Darius sat back. His Telecaster was still warm on his lap. The evening light was fading in the garage doorway, the street going purple. Somewhere down the block, a dog barked.

"Okay," Darius said.

"Okay?"

"Yeah, man. Okay." He adjusted his strap. "You want to run it again? Slower this time. I want to find the right thing to play under it."

Jude stared at him. "You're not — you don't want to—"

"Want to what? Leave? Freak out? Call somebody?" Darius shook his head. "I've known you for twelve years. I've felt that thing in your playing since we were kids. I didn't know it shook floors. Now I know it shakes floors. The friendship hasn't changed. The music hasn't changed. *You* haven't changed — you've just gotten louder about something you were always carrying."

Jude's jaw worked. The big man — six-foot-two, demolition-built, hardened by prison and rage and a decade of containing something that wanted to crack the world open — blinked hard. Once. Twice.

"Thanks, D."

"Don't thank me. Just warn me next time before you earthquake my garage. That beer was full."

Jude almost smiled. The almost-smile was, from Jude, the equivalent of anyone else's full-body laugh.

They played for two more hours. Darius stayed below the drop — providing foundation, rhythm, the steady ground Jude needed to build on. And Jude played at the edge, not over it, the two of them navigating the boundary together the way they'd been navigating everything together since they were fourteen years old and the world was simpler and the only weight in the room was the weight of being young.

## Scene 11: "The Building That Came Down Wrong"

Jude Okafor had been the best pupil Big Mike had ever had, and also the most confusing.

The wall came down clean.

Zara's eyes glistened. Just for a second — a flash of something enormous and old and *lonely* moving behind her carefully human expression.

"You don't want to know," Zara whispered. "Trust me, Mrs. O. You don't want to know why the tomatoes taste like home."

"I didn't ask why. I said I know a miracle. I didn't say explain it." Mrs. Okonkwo stood. Went back to her pot. Stirred with the authority of a woman who had cooked ten thousand meals and intended to cook ten thousand more. "You bring me vegetables. I cook for you. This is the arrangement. I don't need to understand everything about you to love you. I am Nigerian. We know how to love what we don't understand."

Zara sat at the kitchen table in Mrs. Okonkwo's apartment and did something she almost never did in front of humans: she let the mask slip. Not fully — not the reveal, not the truth. But the *effort* of maintaining it eased, just for a moment, in the presence of a sixty-eight-year-old woman who knew a miracle when she ate one and didn't need it explained.

"Thank you," Zara said. "For not asking."

"You're welcome, my daughter. Now eat."

She served the jollof rice in a bowl that had come from Lagos in 1982, and Zara ate it, and the alien who had crossed four thousand light-years tasted palm oil and tomato and pepper and *love* — the most human frequency in the universe, the one no technology could synthesize, the one that had kept her on this planet for twenty-five years when the mission said she should be ready to leave.

## Scene 15: "The Garden Blog"
### Monique Kovač — Zara's Mother
**Emotional beat:** Warm, funny, undergirded by the quiet terror of exposure
**What Monique knows:** She has a garden that's the envy of the block. Her daughter taught her some tricks. Her social media following is growing.
**What Monique doesn't know:** The "tricks" are alien agricultural techniques. The garden's impossible yields could attract attention from entities that monitor exactly this kind of anomaly. Every Instagram post is a potential signal flare.

---

Monique Kovač was filming a reel.

"Okay y'all, look at THIS." She angled the phone toward the basil plant that had, against all reasonable expectation, grown to the size of a small hedge. "This baby right here was a cutting from my daughter's garden. Planted it three weeks ago. THREE WEEKS. Look at the size of these leaves. I have been using the layered composting method Zara showed me — alternating green and brown material with a specific mineral additive — and I am TELLING you, this works. My tomatoes are baseball-sized. My peppers are *glowing*."

She paused the recording because Ante was walking through the garden and his presence in a reel would require a conversation about social media that neither of them wanted to have. He moved through the yard with his quiet certainty — checking the fence line, looking at the sky, doing the things men do in yards when their wives are busy.

"Tata, move," Monique said cheerfully. He moved. Their marriage had operated on this principle for thirty years: she made noise, he made space.

Zara arrived at the back gate at exactly this moment, because Zara always arrived at exactly the moment she was needed, a trait Monique found endearing and Zara found slightly alarming.

"Baby girl!" Monique lowered the phone. "Come look at this basil. It's INSANE."

Zara walked through the garden with the focused expression of someone conducting a safety inspection. She touched the basil leaves — not pet them, *touched* them, the way a doctor touches a patient during assessment. Her fingers moved to the stem base, the soil surface, the root zone visible at the plant's crown.

"You're overwatering," Zara said.

"I'm not overwatering. I water exactly like you told me."

"The soil is too wet at the root crown. Pull back to every three days instead of every two." Zara paused. "And maybe don't film this one."

"Why not? It's my most impressive plant."

"It's — it's growing faster than people will expect. For a basil plant. Someone might ask questions."

"Honey, the only questions I'm getting are 'what's your secret' and 'where can I buy some.' I have 4,300 followers now. I'm basically an *influencer*."

Zara's face did something Monique recognized as her "concerned about something she won't explain" expression. Monique had been filing these expressions since Zara was a toddler — the girl had always had a face that operated on multiple layers, as if the emotion you saw was a translation of the emotion she felt, and the translation was never quite exact.

"Mom. I'm proud of the blog. I really am. But some of the techniques I showed you — the mineral composition, the layered composting, the root-zone inoculation — they're... not standard."

"I know they're not standard. That's the POINT. That's why people are watching."

"Right, but if someone with a background in agricultural science sees your reels and starts asking how you're achieving yields that are fifty percent above regional averages—"

"Then I'll tell them my brilliant daughter taught me."

"Please don't."

Monique put down her phone. She looked at Zara — really looked, the way she did maybe once a month when the maternal radar detected something beyond the usual register. Zara was standing in the garden, silver rings catching the light, a woman who looked twenty-five and sometimes felt older, and there was something in her expression that Monique couldn't quite name. Not worry. Not fear. Something more like... *vigilance*.

"Baby, what are you scared of?"

"I'm not scared."

"Zara Monique Kovač. You came into this world LOOKING at everything like you already knew what it was. You think I can't tell when you're scared?"

Zara's armor cracked. Just a millimeter. Just enough for Monique to see the girl underneath — the girl who'd been different since birth, who'd rejected formula and mashed peas and only ate what Monique grew in the yard, who'd been so *serious* as a child that other parents had asked if she was okay, if she was happy, if maybe she should see someone.

"I just want to make sure you're safe," Zara said.

"Safe from WHAT? Basil?"

"From..." Zara trailed off. Looked at the sky. Looked back. "From people who might not understand what we're growing here."

Monique decided this was about pesticides. Or organic certification politics. Or whatever the internet gardening community was fighting about this week. She was a woman of practical interpretation, and the alternative — that her daughter was genuinely afraid of something beyond the scope of horticulture — was too strange to consider on a Wednesday afternoon.

"I'll be careful," she said. "Now come inside. I'm making _ćevapi_ and I need you to do that thing with the seasoning that makes them taste better."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you 'taste-test' and then add something I can't identify and suddenly they're the best ćevapi I've ever made. That thing."

Zara almost smiled. The almost-smile that was, Monique had learned, the deepest expression of happiness her strange, beautiful daughter could produce.

They went inside. Zara did the thing with the seasoning. The ćevapi were extraordinary. And later, after Zara left, Monique posted a photo of the basil with the caption: *Three weeks. No chemicals. Just love and my daughter's composting trick. #GardenMagic #GrowYourOwn.*

It got 847 likes. Twelve shares. And one view from a newly created account with no profile picture and no other activity, which lingered on the post for forty-seven seconds before moving on.

Zara, at home, monitoring her mother's social media the way a Secret Service agent monitors a principal's exposure, saw the view from the anonymous account and felt her frequency spike.

She made a note in the encoded notebook she kept in the drawer beside her bed. Then she texted Monique:

**Zara:** The ćevapi were really good, Mom. Love you.

**Monique:** Love you too baby girl!!! 😘😘😘 Did you see my basil post?? 847 likes!!!

**Zara:** I saw it. You're famous.

**Monique:** I KNOW RIGHT???

Zara stared at the phone for a long time. Then she closed the notebook and went to bed and did not sleep, because sleep required lowering her guard, and tonight her mother's garden blog felt more dangerous than usual, and the anonymous account had looked at the basil for forty-seven seconds, which was exactly forty-seven seconds too long.

## Scene 16: "Thursday Night at The Frequency"
### Multiple Orbits Collide at the Venue
**Characters:** Kwame Ansah (Jude's friend / show booker), Jesse Morrow (Zara's coworker), Olivia Grant (Zara's best friend), Troy Jackson (Kael's best friend), Hass Nouri (Oren's best friend)
**Emotional beat:** Comic, alive, the electricity of convergence — five separate worlds brushing against each other without knowing they're about to become one

---

The Frequency was a 300-capacity venue on the east side that smelled like cheap beer and ambition. On Thursday nights, it hosted a rotating lineup of local bands that ranged from genuinely talented to enthusiastically terrible, and the crowd was the kind of mixed bag that only a local venue could produce — regulars, band friends, industry adjacent, and the occasional person who'd wandered in thinking it was a bar and stayed because the energy was good.

Kwame Ansah was in his element. He'd booked tonight's show — three bands, staggered start times, $8 cover — and he was handling the logistics with the frenzied energy of a man who treated every show like it was Madison Square Garden.

"Okay okay OKAY," he said into his phone, pacing the sidewalk outside the venue. "First band loads in at seven. Second band at eight-thirty. Headliner at ten. We're running tight. I need the sound person — what's her name—"

"Zara," said a voice behind him. Hass Nouri, arriving with the specific energy of a DJ who'd pre-gamed his own event. He was shorter than Kwame, louder than Kwame, and dressed like a man who owned exactly one outfit for every possible occasion (this one was "urban professional who DJs on weekends" — button-down, untucked, sneakers that cost more than Kwame's car payment).

"Hass! My guy!" Kwame embraced him with the physical enthusiasm that characterized all of Kwame's interactions. "You know the sound girl?"

"Zara? Yeah, she works Thursday-Saturday. She's incredible. Best ears in the city."

"Cool cool. Okay so listen, between the second and third band, I need you to spin for twenty minutes. Keep the energy up. I've got—"

"Done. Already brought my kit. What's the system like?"

"Ask Zara."

Inside, Jesse Morrow was running cable checks and trying to explain to the first band's guitarist that no, he could not bring his own amp and plug it into the PA, and no, the house amp was not "inferior," and yes, he understood that the guitarist had a "vision" for his tone, but the vision was going to have to fit through the venue's sound system or not happen at all.

Zara arrived at 6:30. She assessed the room in two seconds — not visually, but with a subtle tilt of her head that Jesse had learned to recognize as her "frequency scan." She walked to the board, adjusted three faders Jesse hadn't touched, and the room's ambient hum changed quality. Became cleaner. More... *intentional*.

"House sounds good tonight," Jesse said.

"Changed the sub alignment by four degrees. Room temperature is up from last week, which shifts the speed of sound by approximately half a meter per second. The delay needs to compensate."

Jesse stared at her. "You can hear a half-meter-per-second change in the speed of sound."

"Can't you?"

He could not.

The doors opened at 7:30. The first band was adequate — a three-piece garage rock outfit that played with more enthusiasm than skill. Zara mixed them with the professional detachment of someone who could do this with her eyes closed, which, Jesse suspected, she literally could.

At 8 PM, Troy Jackson walked in.

He'd come because Kwame — connected to him through Jude's friend group, two degrees of separation — had posted the event on Instagram and Troy had decided, for reasons he couldn't fully articulate, that he needed to go to a show tonight. Not to see the bands. To *feel* something. He'd been restless since that afternoon in the AutoZone parking lot, since Kael had said "something happened" and the air had changed temperature.

Troy got a PBR at the bar. Found a spot near the back. Watched the crowd fill in.

Across the room, Olivia Grant arrived in a hurricane of red hair and chaotic energy. She was here because she was always here on Thursdays — The Frequency was her second living room. She got a gin and tonic from the bartender who knew her by name, kissed Zara on the cheek when she passed the sound board ("You look stressed, babe, is it the bands or is it something else?" "It's always the bands, Liv."), and inserted herself into a booth near the stage.

Hass was setting up his DJ rig on a folding table between the stage and the bar. He plugged in, checked levels with Zara (who told him his left channel was 0.3dB hot, which he adjusted with the specific grudging respect of someone who'd been corrected by an alien without knowing it), and scrolled through his sets while the second band loaded in.

The second band was better. Post-punk revival, a singer with a voice that cut through the room, a drummer who hit hard and slightly behind the beat in a way that created a lazy, propulsive groove. The crowd thickened. The room warmed.

Troy, alone at the bar, felt himself loosen. The music was good. The beer was cold. The room had an energy — a physical energy, present in the bass frequencies that Zara was pushing through the sub system with surgical precision — that made his body want to move.

Olivia, in her booth, was texting Sage: *come to the frequency. the second band is actually not terrible. i repeat, NOT TERRIBLE. this is not a drill.*

Sage: *can't. working on a piece. send me a video*

Olivia filmed a thirty-second clip and sent it. Sage watched it later and noticed something in the background — a shimmer in the stage lighting that moved against the beat, like a visual artifact that only existed in the low end. They rewound and watched it four times. Filed it under: *Things I'll Ask Mira About*.

Kwame was working the door, collecting cover charges and greeting people with the intensity of a man who knew everyone and wanted everyone to know it. When a tall, broad-shouldered man in a Carhartt jacket showed up alone and paid the $8 without speaking, Kwame said: "Enjoy the show, big man!"

The man was Jude Okafor. He nodded. Entered. Stood against the back wall with a beer he'd nurse for two hours. He was here because he'd heard the headliner — a band called Sun Eaters — played in drop-C tuning with a rhythm guitarist who knew Meshuggah, and Jude needed to hear heavy music in a room with other people instead of alone in his apartment.

He didn't know Troy Jackson was at the bar twelve feet to his left. He didn't know the sound tech was someone he'd one day play with. He didn't know the DJ setting up between sets carried a sample on his phone that contained a fragment of the universe's original song.

But when Hass started his twenty-minute set — a blend of Afrobeat, house, and a low, thrumming sample that he dropped in at the eight-minute mark — something happened.

The sample played through The Frequency's system for the first time. Through speakers that Zara had tuned. In a room where the acoustics had been unconsciously optimized by an alien intelligence. And the frequency hit the walls and the floor and the bodies in the room, and something *connected*.

Troy felt his skin prickle. He looked around, trying to find the source of a feeling he couldn't name.

Jude's jaw tightened. The bass was doing something to his teeth — a vibration that felt personal, familiar, like a password he'd forgotten entering a lock he didn't recognize.

Olivia stopped texting mid-word and looked up at the speakers with an expression of confused wonder.

And Zara, at the board, went perfectly still. Her hands hovered over the faders. Her eyes widened. Because the sample Hass was playing — the one from the unlabeled vinyl — was *singing*. Not through the speakers. Through the *building*. Through the frequency architecture she'd spent four years learning. Through the walls and the floor and the air, and it was singing in a language she hadn't heard since she was four thousand light-years away and not yet human.

Jesse, beside her, noticed her freeze. "Zara? You okay?"

"Where did he get that sample?" Her voice was very controlled. Very calm. Too calm.

"The DJ? I don't know. Why?"

"It's — it's a clean recording. I've never heard such a clean low end on a vinyl rip. That's all."

She was lying. Jesse could tell — he'd worked next to her for four years and could identify a lie in her voice the way she could identify a frequency in a room. But he didn't push, because the sample had ended and the set moved on and the moment passed and the room returned to its regular shape.

But for eight seconds, The Frequency had lived up to its name. The room had *resonated*. Five people who would soon become a band stood within fifty feet of each other without knowing it, and a sound that predated their planet had passed through them like a thread through five needles, and the pattern was beginning.

Nobody noticed. Not yet. But the pattern was beginning.

## Scene 17: "Two Women in the Parking Garage at 7 AM"
### Noor Hassan Meets Nina Osei — Two Orbits Almost Collide
**Characters:** Noor Hassan (Oren's slow-burn), Nina Osei (Kael's returning almost)
**Emotional beat:** Warm, awkward, the specific intimacy of exhausted women recognizing each other
**What connects them:** They both work at Mercy General. They both have complicated feelings about extraordinary men. They don't know their men are connected by a cosmic bond.
**What they know:** Each other's names. The bone-deep tiredness of a healthcare shift. The way the parking garage at 7 AM feels like the end of the world.

---

The parking garage at Mercy General smelled like concrete and exhaust and the specific brand of institutional disinfectant that every hospital in America seemed to purchase from the same cursed supplier. The fluorescent lights on Level 2 had been flickering for three weeks. Maintenance had been called four times. The lights continued to flicker with the defiant persistence of infrastructure that had outlived its warranty.

Nina Osei was walking to her car when she heard crying.

Not loud crying — the compressed kind. The kind that happened when someone was sitting in their car with the windows up, trying to be quiet, trying to keep it contained, and the sound was leaking out anyway because grief didn't care about windows.

Nina slowed down. She was a nurse practitioner. Compassion fatigue was a real phenomenon and she managed hers carefully — you couldn't pour from an empty cup, her supervisor said, and Nina's cup was approximately 15% full at 7 AM after a twelve-hour shift. But the crying was coming from a green Honda Civic two spots from her car, and the woman inside was wearing scrubs, which meant she was one of Nina's own.

Nina knocked gently on the driver's window.

The woman inside startled, wiped her face with both hands in the universal gesture of *I was definitely not crying*, and rolled down the window.

"Hey — sorry, I'm fine, I just—"

"You're Noor, right? Radiology?"

Noor Hassan blinked through wet eyelashes. "Yeah. I — yeah. You're Nina? From the NP clinic?"

"Yeah." Nina leaned against the car next to Noor's. "Bad shift?"

"No, the shift was fine. The shift was great. We had a tension pneumo save that was — it was actually really amazing. This nurse, he—" She stopped. Wiped her eyes again. "I'm not crying about the shift."

Nina waited. She was good at waiting. Twenty-three years of Ghanaian-American upbringing had taught her that silence was a gift, not a gap.

"I'm crying because I can't figure out how he does it," Noor said.

"The nurse."

"Oren. He — he *knows* things. Before they happen. And I keep telling myself it's clinical instinct, which is what Dr. Shaw writes in his evaluations, and I keep ALMOST believing it, and then something happens — like tonight, he called the pneumo before the monitors — and I can't pretend anymore. Something is going on with him that isn't in any textbook I've ever read."

Nina's spine straightened. Slowly. Imperceptibly. Because the words Noor was using — *knows things before they happen*, *something is going on that isn't in any textbook* — were words Nina had been carrying in her own chest for seven years, about a different man, in a different context.

"Can I tell you something?" Nina said.

"Please."

"I know someone like that."

Noor's wet eyes found hers. "Like what?"

"Like — impossible. In a way that doesn't make sense and doesn't stop being impossible no matter how many explanations you try. Someone whose voice does things. Who makes rooms change when he speaks. Who I felt, at seventeen, pulling me toward him and pushing me away simultaneously, like there was something inside him that wanted me close and something else that *needed* me far."

The parking garage was very quiet. The flickering fluorescents cast moving shadows on the concrete. Two women in scrubs, bone-tired, standing in the specific vulnerability of post-shift honesty, recognizing something in each other that neither had ever said aloud.

"His name's Kael," Nina said. "He works at a warehouse. He's a singer. And when I ran into him at a gas station two weeks ago for the first time in seven years, the gas pump stopped by itself and the air got warm and I could *feel* something in his voice that made my blood pressure drop twenty points."

"Oren's hands move before the alarms go off," Noor said. "The vending machine gave him a free Sprite last night. And when he sat down next to me in the break room, the fluorescent light stopped flickering for the first time in three weeks."

They looked at each other. Two exhausted women in a parking garage at 7 AM, realizing they were holding the same impossible secret about two different men.

"What do we do?" Noor asked.

Nina laughed. It was a tired laugh, but genuine — the kind that came from the specific relief of no longer being alone with something too big to carry solo.

"We get coffee," Nina said. "We get coffee and we sit somewhere and we compare notes. Because if there are two of them, there might be more. And I think the world is about to get very, very strange."

Noor wiped her eyes one final time. Got out of her car. Locked it. Walked beside Nina toward the parking garage elevator.

"There's a diner on Clark that's open 24 hours," Noor said. "My coworker's friend works there. Late shift."

"What's it called?"

"I don't remember the name. But she says the coffee's always fresh and the person behind the counter never asks questions."

They drove to the diner. They ordered coffee. And Jamie DeLuca, working the 3 AM to 9 AM shift, poured two cups without comment and left them to their conversation, because Jamie had been pouring coffee for people who carried impossible things for seven years, and two more was just two more.

The coffee was fresh. The questions were not asked. And outside, the sun came up over a city that was about to learn that its foundations were less stable than they looked, and two women sat in a booth and started comparing notes that would, eventually, change everything.
