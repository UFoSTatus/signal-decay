## THE SOCIAL REALITY

Oren Malik arrived at Kenwood Academy three months after burying his father. Freshman year, September 2013. Fourteen years old. Chubby. Quiet. Pakistani kid with thick glasses and a grief nobody could touch.

He was invisible for the first two months. Not bullied — you can't bully what you can't see. He sat in the back of classrooms. Ate lunch in the library. Walked the halls like a ghost who hadn't realized he'd died. The guidance counselor flagged him — "grief response, monitor for depression" — and assigned him to the school therapist, Dr. Morana Vega, who would see him every Tuesday for four years without ever understanding what was actually wrong.

By October, the strangeness started. And by "started" — what people noticed was this: Oren knew things before they happened.

Not dramatically. Not like a fortune teller. Just... he was always slightly ahead. Turned in assignments before teachers announced extensions. Left rooms before fire drills triggered. Answered questions that hadn't been asked yet — then caught himself, eyes widening, mumbling "sorry, I thought you said—"

Teachers labeled him "gifted but unfocused." Students labeled him "that weird kid who's always right." Neither label captured it. What Oren was, in those first months, was a boy whose internal clock had jumped forward by half a second and was slowly, inexorably gaining more ground.

By sophomore year, people knew him. Not popular — never popular. But KNOWN. "The kid who knows." The one you sat next to during tests because he radiated calm and somehow your answers came easier. The one who'd text you "don't go to that party" an hour before the cops showed up. The one who was never surprised by anything, which made him either extremely cool or extremely creepy depending on who you asked.

He had friends. Real ones. Hass (his best friend since freshman year — Hassam Nouri, Iranian-American, loud, chaotic, the future DJ) found him in the library on day three. Walked up. Said: "You look like you need someone annoying in your life." Oren almost smiled. That was enough for Hass.

By junior year, Oren had developed his LOOK. The dresses started at sixteen. The thigh-high socks. The platforms. Not all at once — it evolved. First it was just softer fabrics. Flowing things instead of rigid ones. Then it was his sister Layla's old skirt, tried on in his room at midnight, the fabric settling against his thick thighs like permission. Then it was school. Walking in wearing a black dress over leggings, glasses pushed up, fluffy hair uncombed, and a face that said: "what?"

He got shit. Of course he got shit. High school boys are not known for their acceptance of gender non-conformity. But here's the thing that protected him — the same thing that protected him from everything: you couldn't LAND a blow on Oren Malik because he'd already felt it coming and made peace with it before it arrived. The insult was old news before it left their mouths. The shove was anticipated before the hand moved. He'd look at them with those big brown doe eyes, completely unbothered, and say something dry like "original" or "yeah, the dress is new, thanks for noticing" — and the aggressor would feel, somehow, that they'd been outmaneuvered by someone who hadn't even tried.

By senior year, he was an institution. "That's just Oren." The chubby Pakistani femboy in the knee-length dress and combat boots who knew your test grade before you did and could critique any drummer on YouTube with terrifying precision. People stopped questioning it. You don't question weather.

## THE ABILITY LEAKS

### Year One: Freshman Year (2013-2014)

**September 15, 2013 — The First Day Back**

Three months after the algebra class time-stop. Oren doesn't know what happened that day — his conscious mind has filed it under "shock response, grief hallucination." But his body knows. His body has been different since 2:15 PM on May 22nd, 2013.

The first leak happens in homeroom. Ms. Chandler is calling roll. She says "Malik, Oren" and he answers "here" before she says it. Not a half-second before — a full three seconds before. The room doesn't notice (kids talking, chaos of first day). But Ms. Chandler's pen hovers over the attendance sheet. She looks at him. Shakes it off.

**October 3, 2013 — The Fire Alarm**

Kenwood Academy. Third period. Oren stands up, puts his books in his bag, and walks toward the door. His teacher, Mr. Quintero, stops him: "Where are you going?" Oren blinks. Looks confused. "The fire drill..." Mr. Quintero frowns. "There's no—"

The fire alarm triggers. Two seconds later.

Oren's face goes white. He sits back down. Doesn't explain. Mr. Quintero watches him for the rest of class. Files it under "odd coincidence."

**November 2013 — The Hallway Fold**

Walking between buildings. Oren is late to biology (lost in thought, as usual). The bell rings when he's still in the east hallway — biology is in the west building. A four-minute walk. He arrives 30 seconds after the bell. The distance is wrong. He COVERED it wrong. Like the hallway compressed. Like space folded.

He doesn't remember walking fast. He remembers leaving the east hallway and then being in the biology lab. The middle is... blank. Not blacked out — ABSENT. Like someone cut a section of tape.

This happens three more times freshman year. Always when he's late. Always covering distances that should take minutes in seconds. He starts calling it "the skip" in his own head. Doesn't tell anyone.

**March 2014 — The Test Vision**

Pre-calculus final exam. Oren sits down. Mrs. Patterson (same teacher from the day his father died — she doesn't know why she feels uneasy around him) hands out the test face-down. "Don't flip until I say go."

Oren has already seen the test. Not through the paper — THROUGH TIME. A flash that morning in the shower: question 7 is a limits problem with a coefficient trick. Question 12 is the hardest — double integration. Question 15 is a gimme.

He scores 98%. Nobody suspects cheating — his work is shown, his method correct. But he didn't STUDY for this. He didn't need to. He'd already taken it, somehow, in a timeline that ran three hours ahead of this one.

He feels sick about it. Writes in his journal (the green one, the one nobody's ever read): *"Am I cheating? Is it cheating if I don't know how I know? If the answers just APPEAR? I didn't study. I know I didn't. But I KNOW the answers."*

### Year Two: Sophomore Year (2014-2015)

The time-drift intensifies. The 0.1-0.4 second precognition his father's soul had — now it's 0.3-2.1 seconds in Oren. And growing.

**September 2014 — The Dodge**

Gym class. Dodgeball (the eternal horror of gym class). Oren is the last person you'd expect to be good at dodgeball — chubby, unathletic, soft. But he doesn't get hit. EVER. Not once. Not all year. Balls that should nail him center-mass miss by inches. He moves before the throw. Not flinching — MOVING. Calmly. Like he saw the trajectory three seconds ago and is simply not being in that space.

The gym teacher, Coach Rivera, pulls him aside after the third game. "Malik. You've got reflexes. You ever think about—"

"No." (He knows what's coming: sports. And sports require being WATCHED. And being watched means people notice when you do impossible things.)

"Your loss."

It's not reflexes. It's time.

**December 2014 — The Birthday Prediction**

Hass's birthday party. Sixteen kids crammed into the Nouri basement. Music. Snacks. Someone's older brother bought Mike's Hard Lemonade. Normal teenage party.

Oren is sitting on the couch, not drinking (he never drinks — alcohol makes the time-thing LOUDER). Hass's cousin, Maryam, sits next to him.

"You're the quiet one, huh?"

"Just watching."

"What are you watching?"

And Oren — without thinking, without CHOOSING to — says: "In about thirty seconds, Hass is going to try to do a backflip off the table and knock the TV off the wall. The TV won't break but the wall mount will crack and his dad's going to ground him for a month."

Maryam laughs. "You're weird."

Twenty-eight seconds later, Hass attempts a backflip off the coffee table. His foot clips the wall-mounted TV. It swings wildly — doesn't fall, but the mount CRACKS. The party goes silent. Everyone stares at the dangling TV. Then at Hass. Then at Oren — because Maryam is POINTING at him, mouth open. "He SAID that was going to happen! Like thirty seconds before!"

Hass, still on the floor: "Bro WHAT?"

Oren's stomach drops. "I was joking. Lucky guess." He forces a laugh. Changes the subject. For the rest of the night, Maryam watches him like he's a puzzle. She doesn't bring it up again.

But she remembers. And she tells people. And by junior year, there's a low-level mythology around Oren: *"that kid predicts stuff."* Most people think it's confirmation bias. Hass thinks it's pattern recognition. Oren knows it's neither.

**February 2015 — The Clock Incident**

Oren's bedroom. 2 AM. He can't sleep (he never can — 3-4 hours maximum since his father died). He's staring at his alarm clock. Red digital numbers: 2:14.

And then the numbers freeze. Not the clock — THE ROOM. The hum of the heater goes silent. The tick of the kitchen clock (audible through thin apartment walls) stops. A car passing outside stops mid-engine-hum. Everything stops.

Except Oren. He's awake. Moving. THINKING. In stopped time.

It lasts eleven seconds (he counts). Then everything unsticks. The heater hums. The car passes. 2:14 becomes 2:15. Eleven seconds of Oren's life happened in zero time.

He lies in bed shaking. Thinks about his father's heartbeat. The 7/8 rhythm. The frozen algebra class.

Writes in the green journal: *"It happened again. Time stopped. I didn't stop. I don't know if I'm doing it or if it's happening TO me. I'm scared."*

It happens four more times that year. Always between 2-3 AM. Always lasting 8-15 seconds. He never tells anyone.

### Year Three: Junior Year (2015-2016)

**September 2015 — The Pattern Awakening**

Junior year is when the PATTERNS become inescapable. Oren has always heard rhythm in things (since the father's death — the faucet, the train, the hospital). But now it's ORGANIZING. His brain isn't just hearing patterns — it's ASSEMBLING them. Layering. Creating full drum arrangements from ambient sound.

He's in English class. Ms. Yoon is lecturing about Hamlet. Oren hears: the fluorescent light buzz (hi-hat, steady eighth notes), the AC cycle (kick drum, quarter notes), Ms. Yoon's pacing (snare, syncopated), a student's pen clicking (ghost notes between snare hits). His brain puts it together — a groove in 7/8 that LOCKS. It's COMPLETE. Every element in its right place.

He's frozen. Eyes unfocused. Breathing shallow. INSIDE the pattern. Not hearing a class anymore — hearing a drum performance constructed from nothing.

"Oren? OREN."

He blinks. Ms. Yoon is looking at him. The class is looking at him. He was gone for — checks the clock — four minutes. Staring at nothing. Completely locked into an internal experience nobody else could perceive.

"Sorry. Spaced out."

"Are you feeling okay?"

"Yeah. Sorry."

This happens CONSTANTLY from junior year on. The pattern-locks. Moments where his brain seizes on an ambient rhythm and DISAPPEARS into it. Teachers note: "frequently distracted." Dr. Vega, the school therapist, notes: "possible dissociative episodes — continue monitoring." Nobody considers: "he's hearing music in everything and sometimes it takes him away."

**January 2016 — The Drum Critique Begins**

Oren discovers YouTube drum covers. Specifically: a video of a 19-year-old playing a Tool cover. He watches it. And something happens — his brain DECONSTRUCTS it. In real time. Like a film editor watching raw footage: "That fill is 12ms late. His left hand isn't independent — it's following the right. The ghost notes are too loud. The kick pattern breathes in the wrong place."

He watches another. Same experience. Another. Same. By 2 AM that night, he's watched forty drum covers and CRITIQUED every single one with precision that professional session drummers would envy.

He starts texting Hass about it. Long paragraphs. Technical breakdowns. "This guy's double bass is good but his flamadiddles aren't clean." "This drummer plays like a metronome which SOUNDS good but it's actually lifeless — you need to push and pull." "Nobody does proper polyrhythmic independence anymore. It's all programmed. It's all dead."

Hass, at 2:30 AM: *bro how do you know all this you don't even play*

Oren stares at the text. Doesn't answer for a long time.

*I just hear it.*

**April 2016 — The Band Room (The Attempt)**

The single worst moment of Oren's high school years.

He's been watching the jazz band rehearse for months. Standing at the door. Leaning on the frame. Invisible. The director, Mr. Osei, has noticed but stopped asking if Oren wants to join.

Today the room is empty. After school. Equipment still set up. The drum kit — a Pearl Masters Maple Complete — sits in the back, sticks resting on the snare head. Waiting.

Oren walks in. He doesn't decide to. His body just... goes.

He sits on the throne. The height is wrong (he's shorter than most drummers, legs shorter, thick thighs pressing against the snare). He adjusts without thinking — like his body knows the exact angle. He picks up the sticks.

And his hands start shaking.

Not nervousness. REJECTION. The soul — Ori-Tahn — slamming every door in his body at once. His vision goes grey. His stomach lurches. And the FLASH comes:

*A battlefield. Not Earth — somewhere else. Red sky. Black rock. A drum made of bone and stretched skin. HIS hands — not his hands — holding sticks carved from exoskeletal material, PLAYING while soldiers march into a canyon trap. Screaming. So much screaming. And he can't stop playing because stopping means the survivors lose cohesion—*

He drops the sticks. They clatter on the floor. He's off the throne, three feet away, breathing hard, shaking, COLD. The room spins. He grabs the door frame.

Mr. Osei appears in the hallway. "Oren? You alright?"

"Fine. Dropped something." He's white. Sweating. His hands won't stop trembling.

"You want some water?"

"No. I'm good. Gotta go."

He doesn't go near the band room for six weeks. At night, he dreams of bone sticks and red skies and a rhythm that killed forty thousand.

### Year Four: Senior Year (2016-2017)

**September 2016 — The Aesthetic Solidifies**

Senior year Oren walks into school on day one wearing: a black pleated skirt over dark leggings, chunky platform boots, an oversized band tee (Meshuggah — for the polyrhythms, obviously), thick-framed glasses, and his hair grown out into a fluffy, uncombed cloud.

He looks EXACTLY like himself. For the first time. And the confidence of it — the absolute settled-ness of a person who has stopped trying to be anything other than what they are — radiates.

Nobody says anything. Not because they're scared. Because Oren by senior year carries an energy that says: "I've already had this conversation with myself. You can't tell me anything I haven't already accepted."

The time-drift helps. He's felt the reactions before they happen. He's pre-processed the mockery, the confusion, the admiration, the indifference. By the time it arrives, it's old. Everything is old when you live half a second in the future.

**November 2016 — The Car Accident That Didn't Happen**

Hass is driving. Oren is shotgun. Late night, coming home from a show (Oren watches shows obsessively — never participates, just WATCHES). Hass's old Honda Civic on Lake Shore Drive.

Oren says: "Brake."

Hass: "What?"

"BRAKE. NOW."

Hass brakes. Hard. The Honda squeals to a stop.

Three seconds later: a delivery truck runs a red light through the intersection they would have been in. At speed. If Hass hadn't braked — T-bone. Driver's side. Hass's side.

Silence in the car. For a long time.

"How?" Hass whispers.

Oren is shaking. Not from fear — from the CLARITY of what he saw. Three seconds ahead. The truck entering the intersection. The impact. The glass. Hass's body. He saw it. FULLY. In detail that no precognitive flash should provide.

"I just had a feeling."

"That wasn't a FEELING, Oren. That was—"

"I don't want to talk about it."

They never talk about it. But Hass starts listening when Oren says "brake" or "wait" or "not that way." Every time. Without question. Because whatever Oren is — whatever that was — it saved his life. And Hass has decided that the explanation matters less than the survival.

**March 2017 — Senior Prom (The Loneliness)**

Oren goes to prom. In a floor-length emerald dress his sister Layla helped him pick. With platform heels that make him almost average height. With his hair actually styled for once, thick and soft and framing his face.

He goes alone.

Not because nobody asked — a girl named Rhiannon offered, genuine, friendly. But Oren said no. Because here's the thing about living 0.3-2.1 seconds in the future: conversations are SCRIPTED. He knows what people will say before they say it. The surprise of human interaction — the spark, the discovery, the OH THAT'S WHAT YOU THINK — is gone. Has been gone since sophomore year.

Rhiannon's next three sentences were already in his head before she finished the first. The whole evening would be predetermined. Every joke pre-heard. Every slow dance pre-felt. Every moment of "connection" actually just him performing responses to stimuli he already knew were coming.

So he goes alone. Stands near the wall. Watches. Drinks punch. Hears the DJ (terrible — beat-matching is off by 6ms, transitions are predictable, song selection is formulaic). Hears Hass across the room, laughing about something that happened 3 seconds from now. Feels the entire evening like watching a movie he's already seen.

At 10:15 PM, he leaves. Walks home in the emerald dress and platforms, heels clicking on the sidewalk (a rhythm — 94 BPM — that his brain automatically layers with the distant traffic and the night insects into a groove in 5/4). He's seventeen. He's alone. Not because he wants to be — because being WITH people is being alone anyway, when you've already heard everything they're going to say.

He writes in the green journal that night: *"I think I'm going to be alone for the rest of my life. Not because people don't like me. They do. But I can't be surprised by anyone. I can't discover anyone. Everyone is a song I already know all the words to. I'm seventeen and I'm already bored of people."*

Then, underneath: *"Except Hass. Hass still surprises me sometimes. I don't know why."*

## THE DEFINING MOMENT

**The Death Rhythm — February 14, 2016**

Valentine's Day, junior year. Oren is at the hospital visiting his father's memorial plaque (he goes monthly, takes the bus, sits in the lobby for an hour). The ER is busy — Valentine's Day always is (suicide attempts, domestic violence, heart attacks from stress).

He's sitting in the lobby when he hears it.

A patient in the ER — behind the doors, behind the wall, fifty feet away — their heartbeat shifts. From 4/4 to something else. 5/4. Unsteady. Wrong. And then, beneath the wrong rhythm, a SIGNATURE. A personal rhythm emerging. The departure rhythm.

Oren has never heard this before. Not consciously. But his body RECOGNIZES it. Like hearing his father's last heartbeat again — that shift to 7/8 — but this time it's someone else. A stranger. And Oren can feel them LEAVING.

He stands up. Walks toward the ER doors. Security stops him. "Can't go back there, kid."

"Someone's dying."

"That's what hospitals are for. You can't—"

"No, I mean RIGHT NOW. Room... I don't know the room. But someone's code is changing. They're about to—"

The code alarm goes off. Behind the doors. Fifteen seconds after Oren said it.

Security stares at him. Oren stares at the doors. He can hear it — the rhythm collapsing. The departure rhythm playing its last measure. And then: silence. Flatline. Someone just died and Oren HEARD IT HAPPEN from the lobby.

He leaves. Takes the bus home. Doesn't sleep that night. Writes in the green journal:

*"I can hear people die. Not death itself — the rhythm BEFORE death. The last beat their soul plays before it leaves. Dad's was 7/8. This person's was 5/4. Everyone has one. I can hear them all.*

*I'm going to be a nurse. I'm going to work in the ER. I'm going to be in the room when it happens. Because nobody else can hear it. And maybe hearing it means something. Maybe it means I can DO something.*

*Or maybe it just means I have to witness it. Either way — that's where I belong."*

He applies to nursing programs in senior year. Everyone expected pre-med (like his father, like his sister). The guidance counselor: "Nursing? But your grades—" Oren: "I want to be IN the room. Not above it."

He never explains what that means.

## THE MUSIC CONNECTION

Oren's relationship with music in high school is the cruelest thing about his arc.

He LOVES it. Obsessively. Can't stop listening, watching, analyzing. Drum videos consume hours of his nights. He critiques with a precision that impresses actual musicians who encounter his analysis (he posts anonymously on a few drumming forums — screen name: **seveneight** — and working drummers DM him for advice without knowing he's a sixteen-year-old who's never touched a kit).

But he CANNOT PLAY. The one time he tried (the band room, April 2016), the soul-flash nearly broke him. He's never tried again.

The music connection manifests instead as: the everyday orchestra. Every sound is rhythm. Every room is a groove. His brain won't stop assembling percussion patterns from ambient noise. By senior year, he can walk into any space and tell you its BPM, its time signature, and its groove within three seconds. He does this compulsively. Involuntarily.

Music enters his life not through MAKING it but through HEARING it in everything that isn't music. The universe is his drum kit. He's been playing it since his father died. He just doesn't know that's what he's doing.
