## 🎤 KAEL VOSS — Age 24, Present Day

### "The Warehouse"

The alarm goes off at 5:45 AM. Same as yesterday. Same as tomorrow.

Kael doesn't hit snooze anymore. He used to, back when he first started at the warehouse at 19. Now he's up before it rings — body clock locked in. Feet on cold floor. Bathroom. Brush teeth without looking in the mirror because the guy in the mirror looks like someone who's waiting and Kael doesn't want to think about what he's waiting for.

The drive to work is 22 minutes. He doesn't play music in the car. That's the thing nobody knows — Kael doesn't listen to music. Hasn't for two years. Not because he doesn't like it. Because it makes him feel something so overwhelming that he has to pull over. A song comes on — any song, doesn't matter the genre — and his chest opens up like a door and behind that door is something so BIG that he can't breathe. So: silence. The sound of tires on asphalt. The 5:50 AM dark.

At the warehouse, he unloads trucks. Scans boxes. Drives the forklift. The work is physical and brainless and PERFECT. Nobody needs him to talk. Nobody needs him to be anything. He's a body moving boxes. His coworkers like him — they don't know why, they just do. They invite him to things. He mostly says no.

But here's the thing about the warehouse:

When the morning shift starts and the loading dock is chaos — twelve guys, three trucks, pallets everywhere, someone's radio playing, someone arguing about football — Kael says one thing. "Let's go, left bay first." Just that. Normal volume. Not a shout.

And everyone moves. Immediately. Without question. Without the half-second of processing that normal communication requires. His words bypass something. They land in a place below thought.

His supervisor has noticed. Not the supernatural part — just the result. "Voss, you should be a team lead." Kael shrugged. "I just move boxes." But the supervisor keeps putting him in charge without officially promoting him because things GO SMOOTHER when Kael speaks.

Every time I try to fill my life with STUFF — career, relationship, plans — my body rejects it. Like an immune system attacking a transplant. Whatever I'm supposed to do, it's not this. But it's not anything else I've tried either.

What he says is: "I'm good, man."

Troy doesn't push. He never does. That's why they're still friends.

### "The Thing Nobody Talks About"

Elena is home from college for the weekend. She's in the kitchen with their mom making Sunday dinner when Kael comes in from his run.

"Kael." Elena's holding her phone. "I need you to call my professor."

"What? Why?"

"He's refusing my extension request. I emailed twice. He said no both times."

"So? Call him yourself."

"I DID. He said no. I need you to call."

Marissa (their mom) looks up from the stove. She knows what Elena's asking. She's seen it too. She doesn't say anything.

Kael shakes his head. "I'm not calling your professor."

"PLEASE. Just — say you're my brother and you're asking him to reconsider. That's all. Just SAY it."

Silence in the kitchen. The thing they don't name hanging in the air.

"He'll say yes if you ask." Elena says it quietly. Like it's a fact. Because it is.

"That's weird, Elena."

"I know. But I need the extension."

He calls. Thirty seconds. "Hi, I'm Elena Voss's brother. She's dealing with some family stuff this week — could you reconsider the extension? I'd really appreciate it." Polite. Normal. Nothing unusual.

The professor says yes before Kael finishes the sentence. Practically falls over himself. "Of course, of course, tell her take all the time she needs."

Elena mouths "TOLD YOU" from across the room. She's grinning. Like it's a party trick.

Kael hangs up and feels sick. Because it's NOT a party trick. And every time he uses it — even for something small — the door in his chest opens a little wider. And he's not ready for what's behind it.

## 🎸 MIRA CHEN — Age 22, Present Day

### "Guitar Lessons"

Tuesday, 4 PM. Mira's teaching a 14-year-old named Sophie to play barre chords.

"Push harder with your index finger. No — lay it flat. Like a bar across the—" 

She sees it before Sophie does. The flicker of frustration in the girl's aura — a dull red pulsing at her temples. Not visible. Not to anyone else. To Mira it's clear as a traffic light.

"Hey. Put it down for a sec."

"I suck at this."

"You don't suck. Your hand is tired. That's different. Let's try something." Mira picks up her own guitar. Plays the chord progression Sophie's been struggling with — but slowly. Like honey. Like it's easy. Like it's breathing. "See? It doesn't have to be a fight. You're wrestling the guitar. Stop wrestling. Just... hold it."

Sophie tries again. Gets it. Grins.

Behind Mira, in the corner of the room that's always slightly colder than it should be, Keys approves. A phantom chord — just the ghost of one — resolves underneath Sophie's attempt. Nobody hears it but Mira.

Teaching is the one thing that works. The one thing the entities don't ruin. They like watching her teach. They don't get jealous of students because students aren't THREATS. Students leave after an hour. They don't get close. They don't stay.

### "The Things She Paints"

Mira paints. Not professionally — just for herself. Watercolors, mostly. Small pieces on cheap paper.

She doesn't plan them. Her hand moves and she watches. Like automatic writing but visual. Landscapes of places she's never been. Skies in colors that don't exist in this atmosphere. Architecture that obeys rules she can't name.

She has 200+ paintings in a box under her bed. She's never shown anyone.

Three months ago she painted something that scared her: five figures on a stage. Backlit. One behind a drum kit. One with a microphone. Two with guitars. One with a bass. She couldn't see their faces. But the one with the bass had a green instrument and stood perfectly, impossibly still.

She shoved it in the box and didn't paint for two weeks.

The entities were VIBRATING that night. Excited. Like dogs who heard the car in the driveway.

### "The Student Who Cried"

Not all lessons are easy.

A boy — 17, named Marcus — has been coming for three months. He's decent. Motivated. But last Tuesday he came in and couldn't play. Hands shaking. Eyes wet. Wouldn't say why.

Mira didn't ask. She just played. Soft. Something she made up. Something that came from the place she doesn't control. And the room got warmer and the boy's shoulders dropped and after twenty minutes he said "my parents are getting divorced" and cried.

She didn't touch him. Didn't hug him. Just kept playing. Very quiet. A melody that somehow exactly matched the shape of his grief. Notes she couldn't explain choosing. Intervals that shouldn't work but DID.

He left calmer. Said "thanks" at the door. Meant it.

Mira sat alone after and thought: *I didn't choose those notes. THEY chose the notes. Through me. For him.* She doesn't know if that's beautiful or terrifying. She decides: both.

## 🎸 JUDE OKAFOR — Age 26, Present Day

### "Demolition Day"

7 AM. Steel-toed boots. Hard hat. Sledgehammer.

The building is a former elementary school. Built in 1962. Asbestos in the ceilings, mold in the walls, condemned three years ago. Jude's crew is taking out the interior walls before the structural guys come in.

He swings. The wall gives. Plaster and lath and sixty years of paint crack open like an egg. The vibration goes up his arms, through his shoulders, into his spine. And something in him EATS it. Not physically. Something BENEATH the muscles. The impact feeds something that's always hungry.

"Jude! East wall next. Double studs, you'll need the—"

He's already there. Already swinging. The sledgehammer is twelve pounds but in his hands it moves like six. His coworkers have stopped commenting on how fast he works. They just let him go. Give him the hardest walls. The concrete block. The support beams that need jackhammering.

There's a moment — every demo day — where the rhythm takes over. Swing. Impact. Pull back. Swing. Impact. Pull back. It syncs with his heartbeat. And the thing inside him that's always PRESSING against the walls of his body gets a moment of relief. Like scratching an itch that goes bone-deep.

He finishes the east wall twenty minutes before anyone else finishes theirs. Sits on a pile of rubble. Drinks water. His arms aren't shaking. They should be.

A new guy watches him. "How long you been doing this?"

"Seven years."

"You... like it?"

Jude drinks more water. How do you explain to a normal person that demolition is the only time you feel HUMAN? That building things (his dad's wish) makes him feel like he's lying, but destroying them makes him feel like he's being honest? That the impact is the closest thing to peace he's found?

"It's alright."

Darius pulls off the strings. "There you go again."

"Nah man, it's sick. I just can't... follow you there. You know? Where do you GO?"

Down. He goes DOWN. Below the note. Below the sound. Into a place where frequency becomes FORCE and music becomes physics. He can't explain it. He just knows that every time he hits that low, chunky, percussive space — things around him respond. Physically.

They switch to Darius's blues thing and pretend it didn't happen. Like every Saturday.

## 🎸 ZARA KOVAČ — Age 25, Present Day

### "The Frequency (The Venue)"

Thursday night. Local band. Mid-set. Zara is behind the mixing board and she's in pain.

Not physical pain. FREQUENCY pain. The guitarist is running through a Mesa Boogie at maximum gain and his low mids are CLASHING with the bassist's fundamental and the kick drum is tuned too low and the whole thing is creating a standing wave at 120Hz that's making the venue walls vibrate in a way that nobody else notices but feels like NAILS to Zara.

She adjusts. Pulls 120 out of the mains. Nudges the guitar down 2dB. Pushes the bass up an octave in the monitors so the bassist plays lighter. Adds a high-pass on the kick at 60Hz.

The room breathes. The band doesn't know what changed. The audience doesn't know. But everyone relaxes 5% without knowing why. The bartender serves faster. People order another drink. The energy shifts from "this is fine" to "this is GOOD" and nobody can explain the difference.

Zara can. The difference is 120Hz. But explaining that would mean explaining HOW she knows — that she doesn't hear frequencies, she FEELS them. In her body. In her bones. In a place that isn't quite human. That the room's resonant frequency is always louder for her than the actual music. That she mixes by feel — literally, physically, FEEL.

After the set, the guitarist comes to the board. "Hey, we sounded really good tonight. Better than practice."

"Your tone was nice." She lies. His tone was garbage. She fixed it from the board without him knowing. Like she does for every band. Like she's done for three years at this venue.

"Thanks!" He walks off. Happy.

She pours herself water from the venue tap and thinks: *I am the most overqualified sound tech on this planet. And possibly any planet.*

She picks up the Yamaha. The TRBX304 in Mist Green. Plugs in. Turns the amp low — it's 9 AM, the neighbors.

She plays.

She deleted all the recordings. 

But she still plays them. Alone. On Sunday mornings. With the amp low and the windows closed and nobody listening.

Except — and she doesn't know this — the frequencies travel. Through the floor. Through the foundation. Into the earth. And out. Every Sunday morning, a signal leaves her apartment that shouldn't be possible from a $300 Yamaha through a $200 amp. A signal that goes further than sound should go.

Somewhere, something is listening. 

She doesn't know yet.

## 🥁 OREN MALIK — Age 27, Present Day

### "Night Shift"

2:14 AM. Emergency department. Three patients waiting. One ambulance inbound.

Oren is checking vitals on a 67-year-old chest pain in Bay 4 when the phone rings. He already knows what it is. Not because of the ring tone or the caller ID. He just... knows. Two seconds before the phone rings, his body moves toward it.

"Copy." He hangs up. Turns to the charge nurse. "Three MVA coming. One's critical — chest trauma. Prep Bay 1 with a thoracotomy tray."

The charge nurse looks at him. "How do you know the severity? They didn't—"

"Just a feeling." He's already moving. Already setting up.

Seven minutes later: three patients. One critical. Chest trauma. Needs a thoracotomy tray.

The charge nurse stares at him. He pretends not to notice.

This happens three or four times a week. His colleagues have a name for it: "Oren's instinct." They write it off as pattern recognition. They're wrong. It's not prediction. It's PERCEPTION. He perceives the timeline slightly ahead. Not far — seconds, sometimes a minute. Enough to be ready. Enough to save lives.

### Near-Miss #2: The Accident

Four months ago. Highway 9. 11:30 PM.

Oren was driving home from a shift. He swerved around a stalled car he somehow knew was there before his headlights found it. Behind him, a truck didn't see the stalled car. Hit it. Both vehicles spun across three lanes.

Jude was in the truck behind the truck. Slammed his brakes. Got out. Ran to help. He pulled the stalled car's driver out with one arm — a move that should have required two EMTs and a backboard.

Oren was already U-turning. Already calling 911. Already there.

They stood ten feet apart at the accident scene. Jude with blood on his shirt (not his). Oren with a flashlight checking the truck driver's pupils.

"Nice work pulling him out," Oren said.

"You called 911 fast," Jude said.

They nodded at each other. The ambulance came. They went their separate ways.

Neither remembers the other's face. But both remember: something at that accident scene felt RIGHT. Like a rehearsal for something they'd do together again.

### Near-Miss #3: The Show

Two months ago. The Frequency. Thursday night. A local band's last show before breaking up.

Zara was mixing. Obviously. It's her venue.

Oren was in the audience. Hass dragged him out. "You never come to shows anymore. You NEED this." Oren stood in the back. Tapping the rhythm on his thigh. Perfectly in time.

Mira was there too. Alone. Standing to the side. Watching the guitarist with professional interest. The entities were quiet — which was unusual at a venue. Like they were BEHAVING. On purpose. Like something important was happening and they didn't want to disrupt it.

Kael walked past the venue. Didn't go in. But he stopped on the sidewalk for thirty seconds. Listened to the muffled sound coming through the walls. Felt the door in his chest swing wide open. Walked away fast. Heart pounding.

Jude wasn't there. But he was three blocks away. At a bar with Darius. He said, out of nowhere: "You hear that?" Darius said: "Hear what?" Jude didn't know. He heard SOMETHING. Low. Below the music. A frequency that wasn't the band playing — it was the ROOM. The room where three of his future bandmates were standing without knowing each other.

The night passed. Nothing happened. But the next morning, all five of them woke up with the same feeling: *soon.*

None of them could explain it. None of them told anyone.

But the word was there. Behind their eyes when they opened them. In the shower. In the mirror. During the commute. At work. At dinner.

*Soon.*
