## THE CORE TRUTH

Lena Kovač is a completely, entirely, unremarkably human seventeen-year-old girl who is beginning to remember lives she has never lived.

She is not an alien. She has no cosmic mission. She doesn't carry a soul name from another world or a frequency assignment from a crystalline lattice. She is a girl from Chicago with a Croatian dad and a Black mom and a brother who leaves his basketball shoes in the hallway and a sister who works at a music venue and makes really, really good food.

She is ordinary.

Except.

Except she can close her eyes in AP History and smell a kitchen she's never been in — woodsmoke and animal fat and something sweet boiling in a pot she's never seen, in a house she's never visited, in a city she can't name. Except she knows what cobblestones feel like under bare feet, even though she's never walked on cobblestones. Except the name *Marguerite* means something to her, and she doesn't know what, and she's never met a Marguerite, and when she writes it in the margin of her notebook it hurts in a place she can't point to.

She's not losing her mind. She's not having a breakdown. She's not even particularly scared — not yet. She's just... confused. In the way you're confused when you wake up from a dream that felt more real than the alarm clock, and for three or four seconds you don't know which one is true.

That confusion used to last seconds. Now it lasts minutes. Sometimes longer.

Nobody knows. Not her parents. Not her friends. Not her sister.

Especially not her sister.

Because Zara has her own secret. And the irony — the deep, structural, devastating irony of the Kovač household — is that both sisters are carrying something impossible, and neither one has said a word, and every Sunday dinner they sit across from each other and smile and pass the bread and the distance between their silences could fill an ocean.

## PART 1: WHO LENA IS — "A Tuesday in Junior Year"

6:15 AM. Phone alarm. The song is "Midnight City" by M83 — she's had it as her alarm since freshman year and she'll change it when she finally hates it, which hasn't happened yet. The house is already alive — she can hear the coffee maker downstairs (Ante's timer, 5:45 every morning), the muffled sound of a news podcast from her parents' room, and the complete silence from Marko's room because Marko would sleep until 2 PM if the world let him.

Lena Kovač: five-foot-six and still not sure if she's done growing. Brown skin — lighter than Monique's, darker than Ante's, the exact middle that strangers can never categorize, which means she gets "What are you?" from adults who should know better about four times a year. The gold chain with the tiny cross that her Baka (grandmother) sent from Split for her sixteenth birthday, which she wears every day not for religious reasons but because it makes her feel tethered to something.

### The Thing Nobody Sees

Here is something about Lena that doesn't show up in her grades or her friend group or her Instagram (which she barely uses — "lurker energy," Priya calls it):

She is paying attention in a way that most seventeen-year-olds aren't.

Not to school. Not to social dynamics. To EVERYTHING.

She watches people. Not consciously — not the way Zara does, calculating and monitoring from behind a mask. Lena doesn't even know she's doing it. But she notices things that her friends miss:

The way Mr. Kovacs (no relation — the name is a coincidence she's been explaining since freshman year) always touches his left ring finger where a wedding band used to be when he talks about the Civil War. The way the cafeteria worker with the blue hairnet hums a specific melody every Thursday — the same melody, the same notes, always on Thursdays. She thinks this is normal. She thinks everyone notices these things and just doesn't talk about them.

They don't. But she's been marinating in her sister's frequency field for seventeen years, and the part of her brain that perceives pattern and rhythm has been subtly, gradually, irrevocably enhanced. She's not superhuman. She's not powered. She's just... wider. Like someone who grew up at altitude and has slightly larger lungs without knowing it.

## PART 2: THE KOVAČ KITCHEN — "Seventeen Years of Something in the Food"

### What Zara Doesn't Know She's Doing

Every Sunday, Zara comes for dinner.

She brings things. Always has. Since she moved out at twenty, she's shown up every Sunday with a bag of produce — herbs from her apartment garden, vegetables from the grow shelves, fruits from whatever magic she's working in that greenhouse space the family pretends is normal. She brings jars of things she's "trying out" — hot sauce, preserved lemons, fermented honey, tinctures she calls "tea" that taste like nothing Monique can replicate.

"Zara, what's IN this?" Monique asks every few weeks, holding up a jar of something green and luminous.

"Just herbs, Mama."

"WHICH herbs?"

"The usual ones." Zara's half-smile. The deflection that Lena has noticed her whole life without ever naming it.

The food arrives. Monique cooks with it. The family eats it. This has been happening for as long as Lena can remember.

What nobody in the family understands — including Zara, on some level — is what this food IS.

Zara's plants aren't just well-grown. They're frequency-calibrated. Grown in soil amended with compounds that don't exist in human agriculture. Exposed to light spectrums that mimic a star from another solar system. Tended by a being who understands plant biology not at a human PhD level but at a CIVILIZATIONAL level — who knows what plants were designed to do because her people designed some of them.

The food carries frequency. Not metaphorically. Not in a "good vibes" way. Literally. The cellular structure of Zara's herbs and vegetables contains harmonic resonances that are imperceptible to human taste but very much perceptible to human biology. The endocannabinoid system — the receptor network that humans carry specifically for interacting with plant-derived molecules — responds to Zara's food the way a radio responds to a signal. Subtly. Below the threshold of conscious awareness. But consistently. Over years. Over decades.

Lena has been eating this food since she was six months old, when Monique started mashing Zara's garden peas into baby food.

Seventeen years.

### The Frequency Field

And it's not just the food.

Zara maintains a dampening field around Lena. Has since the day Lena came home from the hospital. A constant, low-level suppression frequency — 15% of Zara's energy, every hour of every day — designed to keep Lena's complement frequency from broadcasting.

But suppression is not the same as silence.

Think of it this way: if you hold your hand over a candle flame, you stop the light from reaching the ceiling. But you don't stop the HEAT. The warmth still radiates. It goes into your hand. It changes YOUR temperature.

Zara's dampening field suppresses Lena's outward signal. But the field ITSELF is a frequency. A persistent, low-level, alien-origin frequency that Lena has been bathing in since birth. It's in the walls of her bedroom. It's in the air she breathes at the dinner table. It's in the vibration of the house's foundation on the nights Zara hums her shielding frequency.

The field wasn't designed to change Lena. It was designed to PROTECT her. But seventeen years of constant exposure to a non-terrestrial frequency has had effects that Zara never predicted and doesn't know about.

### The Plants

And then there are the plants themselves.

The Kovač house is full of them. Monique's Instagram-famous garden. The vertical herb wall. The sunroom with its impossible avocado tree and its cherry tomatoes that produce twelve months a year. The trailing pothos in the living room with leaves the size of dinner plates. The fiddle-leaf fig that has never dropped a leaf.

These plants are, in a very quiet way, alien. Not their DNA — they're Earth plants, grown from Earth seeds. But they've been cultivated with Zara's techniques, watered with Zara's amendments, exposed to Zara's frequency during critical growth stages. They carry a subtle resonance — the faintest echo of the star spectrum that Zara's flowering lights emit. Not enough to detect with any human instrument. But enough, over years, to create an ambient field in the house.

Lena's bedroom is surrounded by plants. Below her (the kitchen garden), beside her (Monique's hanging planters in the hallway), above her (the roof deck where Monique grows peppers in summer). She sleeps in a cocoon of Zara's frequency. Has for seventeen years.

The food. The field. The plants.

Three vectors. All accidental. All converging on one seventeen-year-old girl who was born completely, ordinarily human — and who is now, slowly, quietly, beginning to remember things that no one in her current life should know.

## PART 3: ZARA — "My Sister the Mystery"

### The Eight-Year Gap

Eight years is a chasm when you're a kid and a bridge when you're older. Lena is on the bridge now, but she remembers the chasm.

When you're five, your twenty-five-year-old sister isn't a sister — she's a benevolent alien presence who shows up with cool things and knows how to French braid and leaves before bedtime. When you're ten, she's the person you FaceTime to show your new shoes. When you're thirteen, she's the only adult who doesn't treat you like a child. When you're seventeen, she's the person you want to be but can't quite figure out how.

Lena loves Zara. This is not complicated or fraught or conditional. It's structural. Load-bearing. The way you love the ground you walk on — you don't think about it, but remove it and you'd fall forever.

But she also doesn't KNOW Zara. Not really. And she's starting to notice.

---

### What Lena Sees

Here is Zara, as Lena perceives her:

**The cool factor.** Zara is effortlessly, annoyingly cool. She works at a live music venue. She wears vintage leather jackets and boots that have actually been places. She knows sound engineering, which is a job that most of Lena's friends didn't know existed until Lena explained it. She plays bass in what appears to be a VERY intense band that practices above the venue. She has a best friend named Olivia who is loud and funny and once called Lena "mini-Z" at a barbecue, which Lena pretended to hate and secretly loved.

**The garden.** Zara's apartment is a jungle. Lena has been there — it's small and warm and smells like earth and rosemary and something deeper that she can never identify. The plants are EVERYWHERE. Some of them are in rooms that get no direct sunlight, which Lena knows from biology class should be a problem, but she's learned not to ask Zara questions about her plants because the answers are always vague and the subject always changes.

**The deflection.** This is the thing Lena can't articulate but feels in her body: Zara is always slightly... elsewhere. In every conversation, there's a layer that Lena can sense but can't access. It's like talking to someone through a screen door — you can see them, you can hear them, but there's a membrane. Zara is warm and present and funny and caring, and she's also NOT FULLY HERE. Some part of her is always watching from somewhere deeper than the conversation.

Lena doesn't know the word for this. She might call it "mysterious" if pressed, but that sounds like a YA novel and she'd roll her eyes at herself. What it actually feels like is: *My sister loves me completely and holds me at a distance simultaneously, and I've never been able to figure out which one is the real thing.*

Both, obviously. But Lena doesn't know that yet.

Because pushing means risking her own secret. And both of them would rather carry their weight alone than risk what happens if they set it down in front of each other.

### What Lena Doesn't Know About Zara

Lena doesn't know her sister is an alien.

She doesn't know about the crystalline lattice world, the frequency highways, the thousand-year consciousness crammed into a human body. She doesn't know about Za-Rah.

She doesn't know that the reason her food tastes like it does is that Zara is gardening with technology from another civilization. She doesn't know any of this. And she doesn't know that Zara spends 15% of her energy every second of every day protecting her.

What she knows is: Zara is the safest person in the world. Being near her feels like breathing after holding your breath. She can't explain why. She just knows.

## PART 4: MOM — "The Gardener with 34,000 Followers"

### Monique Kovač, née Richardson

Monique is fifty. Black, from Shreveport originally, moved to Chicago for nursing school at twenty-two, met Ante at a jazz bar at twenty-six, married him at twenty-eight, had Zara nine months later in what she describes as "God's timing and nothing else." She's a nurse practitioner at a community health clinic in Uptown. She is the loudest person in any room and the kindest person on any block.

And she is, since 2020, accidentally internet-famous.

**@MoniquesGardenKitchen** is a gardening-and-cooking account that started during lockdown and never stopped. Monique posts videos of her garden — the impossible garden, the one that produces like it's in a different climate zone — and recipes using its output. She does it in her scrubs half the time, talking directly to the camera the way she talks to everyone: fast, warm, Southern-inflected despite twenty-five years in Chicago, full of endearments.

"Now look at this basil, baby. LOOK at it. You cannot tell me this isn't the most beautiful thing you've ever seen. I've been growing basil for thirty years and this one? This one is DIFFERENT. I don't know what it is — the soil, the water, the prayers, I don't know. But it's four feet tall and it smells like a Mediterranean vacation and I didn't pay for a passport."

Her followers love her. The comments are full of "queen" and "teach me your ways" and "my basil dies every time, what is your SECRET."

Her secret is her oldest daughter, but nobody knows that.

### Lena and Monique

Lena's relationship with her mother is warm, slightly exasperated, fundamentally safe.

Monique is a PRESENCE. She fills rooms. She asks questions. She notices things — not with Zara's alien perception, but with the keen, practiced eye of a woman who raised three children, works in healthcare, and has been paying attention to human behavior for fifty years.

"You're quiet today," Monique will say, and Lena will say "I'm always quiet," and Monique will say "No, you're THOUGHTFUL usually. Today you're quiet. There's a difference," and Lena will feel simultaneously seen and invaded.

The invasion is the problem. Not because Monique is intrusive — she's not. She knows when to back off. But her AWARENESS is constant. She clocks Lena's moods, her appetites, her sleep patterns, her social calendar. She knows when something is wrong before Lena does, sometimes. And this means Lena has to work harder to hide the thing she's hiding.

Because Monique would worry.

Not in the abstract way you worry about a teenager — *are they drinking, are they depressed, are they in over their heads.* Monique would worry in the SPECIFIC way that Monique worries, which involves action: phone calls to the pediatrician, conversations with the school counselor, appointments scheduled before Lena could finish the sentence.

Lena imagines telling her mother: "I think I'm remembering lives I haven't lived."

Monique's response, in order:
1. Concern.
2. Questions.
3. Google.
4. A phone call to Dr. Adewale (Lena's pediatrician since birth).
5. An appointment with a psychologist.
6. Possibly a mention to the school counselor.
7. Possibly a mention to Zara ("Your sister said something strange, can you talk to her?").

Lena can't do that to her. Won't.

So she smiles at breakfast. She eats the eggs. She tells her mom about the chem quiz and the cello recital and whether Tomás said anything new (Monique asks about Tomás; Monique has been asking about Tomás since Lena mentioned him ONCE in September and will never stop asking). She performs Normal Daughter, and she's good at it, and it costs her something small every time she does it.

### The Cooking

But here's the thing about Monique that matters most for this story: Monique feeds people.

Not just her family. Everyone. The neighbors. The coworkers. The UPS driver who delivers packages in December gets a container of gumbo. The woman at the dry cleaner gets a jar of Monique's peach preserves (from Zara's peach tree; the peach tree should not exist in Illinois; Monique does not question the peach tree).

Monique cooks with Zara's ingredients the way a painter uses expensive pigments — she doesn't know WHY the colors are richer, she just knows the result is extraordinary. Her gumbo is legendary. Her jerk chicken has made grown men cry. Her collard greens — braised with herbs from Zara's garden, finished with a vinegar that Zara makes from a process Monique has never asked about — have been described by Ante's Croatian friends as "better than anything in my grandmother's kitchen, and if you tell her I said that I'll deny it."

Lena has been eating this food her entire life. Every meal, every snack, every cup of tea that Monique brews from Zara's dried herbs. Seventeen years of frequency-calibrated nutrition entering her body, interacting with her cells, subtly nudging her biology in directions that no one anticipated.

The food didn't CAUSE the memories. But it opened a door. Thinned a wall. Lowered a threshold that is normally thick enough to keep human consciousness firmly anchored in a single lifetime.

Lena is remembering because the barrier between THIS life and the ones before it has been gently, lovingly, accidentally eroded by her sister's cooking — filtered through her mother's hands.

## PART 5: THE FIRST MEMORY — "The Kitchen That Isn't"

### September, Sophomore Year (Age 16)

It starts small. Everything does.

AP History. Third period. Mrs. Delaney is talking about the French Revolution. The Reign of Terror. Bread shortages. Lena is taking notes — or rather, she's writing in her notebook while Mrs. Delaney's voice becomes pleasant background noise, because Lena has already read the chapter and is mostly thinking about whether she left her water bottle in the car.

Mrs. Delaney puts an image on the projector: a period illustration of a Parisian street. Cobblestones. Narrow buildings. Laundry hanging between windows. A cart in the foreground.

Lena looks at it.

And the floor drops out.

Not literally. She doesn't faint. She doesn't gasp. She doesn't do anything visible at all. But something inside her — some internal surface that's always been solid — goes thin. Like ice you can suddenly see through.

She knows that street.

Not the illustration — the STREET. The real one. The one under the illustration. She knows what the cobblestones feel like under wooden shoes. She knows the smell — bread, horse dung, something acidic from the tanner's workshop on the corner. She knows that if you turn left at the end of this block there's a fountain that doesn't work, and the baker's wife hangs her apron on a hook by the door, and the building on the right has a staircase with a broken third step.

She knows these things the way you know your own address. Not learned. KNOWN.

The moment lasts four seconds. Then Mrs. Delaney changes the slide and Lena is back in her chair, in her school, in her life. Heart rate slightly elevated. Hands slightly damp. A feeling in her chest like homesickness — but for WHERE?

She writes in the margin of her notebook, without thinking: *Rue de la Huchette.*

She stares at it. She doesn't know what it means. She's never heard of it. She crosses it out.

(It's a real street. In Paris. She'll find this out later, when she Googles it at 1 AM. And when she does, she'll close her laptop and lie in the dark for a long time.)

### The Second One (October)

Three weeks later. Different class. Different trigger.

Orchestra rehearsal. They're working on Dvořák's "New World" Symphony — the Largo. Lena's cello part is beautiful but repetitive, and she's playing it from muscle memory while her mind drifts.

The oboe comes in. The famous English horn melody. That slow, aching, going-home melody that Dvořák wrote because he missed Bohemia.

Lena's hands stop moving.

She is in a field. Not imagining a field — she IS there. The grass is up to her waist. It's warm — summer, late afternoon, the light golden and thick. There's a farmhouse behind her. Stone walls. Thatched roof. She can hear chickens. She can feel the sun on her arms — both arms bare, sunburned, the skin rough and freckled and NOT HER SKIN. These are someone else's arms. Someone else's body. She is inside someone else's life.

*Maren.*

## PART 6: WHAT THE MEMORIES FEEL LIKE — "Not Dreams. Not Imagination."

### The Taxonomy

By the time she's been having them for six months, Lena has developed an unconscious classification system. She doesn't write it down — that would make it real. But in her head, the memories sort themselves:

**Flashes.** The most common. A sensory fragment — a smell, a texture, a quality of light — that arrives without warning and lasts one to five seconds. Flashes are disorienting but manageable. They're like getting a text from an unknown number: you glance at it, you don't recognize it, you put the phone down.

Examples: The cobblestone street. The lavender. The hands that aren't hers. A taste of something — fermented, sharp, alcoholic — that she's never drunk. A sound — horse hooves on packed earth, moving fast, and a feeling of fear. A word in a language she doesn't speak but understands: *hızlı.* (Turkish: *hurry.*)

**Scenes.** Less common. Full-immersion experiences with a beginning, a middle, and an interruption (never an end — they always end because something pulls her back to the present). Scenes involve a different body, a different place, a different TIME. She is always someone specific. She always knows things — practical knowledge that belongs to the person she's inhabiting.

Examples: The kitchen. The field. The rooftop (this one is new — she's sitting on a rooftop in a warm city, at night, and there's music somewhere below, and she's young in this memory, maybe her age, and she's holding someone's hand).

**Names.** The rarest and the worst. Not memories, exactly — arrivals. A name appears in her consciousness the way a word appears on a screen: fully formed, already there, demanding attention. The names MEAN something. They carry emotional weight. They ache.

Names she's collected: *Marguerite. Elif. Maren. Saoirse. Hana.* Five names in eight months. All women. All strangers. All accompanied by a grief she can't source — the specific, particular grief of missing someone who died, except Lena doesn't know who these people are or when they died or why she mourns them.

---

### The Quality of Remembering

Here is what makes the memories different from imagination, and why Lena can't dismiss them even though she desperately wants to:

**They arrive.** She doesn't create them. She doesn't choose them. She can't summon them or predict them. They show up uninvited, like weather. She can be in the middle of a conversation, a class, a shower, a text to Priya, and — without transition, without warning — she's somewhere else. There's no gateway, no tingling, no Hollywood shimmer. One second she's here. The next second she's ALSO there. Both at once, briefly. Then here again.

**They're embodied.** She doesn't SEE the memories like a movie. She FEELS them. She's inside a body — a specific body, with specific hands and specific aches and specific weight. She knows how this body sits and stands and breathes. She knows its habits. Its limitations. The bad knee that flares in rain. The way the neck cramps from sleeping on straw.

**They're detailed in the wrong way.** If you imagine a Parisian street, you imagine what you've seen in movies: wide boulevards, the Eiffel Tower, café tables. Lena's Parisian street is NARROW. It smells BAD. The cobblestones are uneven and the gaps between them are filled with something she doesn't want to identify. The buildings are close enough to touch both walls by extending your arms. There's no Eiffel Tower — there WOULDN'T be, because the tower wasn't built until 1889 and this street is from before that. Lena knows this not because she checked the history but because the MEMORY doesn't include the tower. The absence is data.

**They carry emotion.** Not dramatic, movie-score emotion. The small, ordinary, almost invisible emotions of daily life. The kitchen memory didn't carry existential dread — it carried the bone-deep tiredness of a woman who's been kneading bread since dawn while a toddler cries on her hip. The field memory carried contentment — the plain, physical satisfaction of being outside in good weather after a long winter. The rooftop memory carried yearning — the specific, teenage, full-body ache of holding someone's hand for the first time and wanting to freeze the moment.

These emotions are what convince her. Because you can't imagine the specific quality of tiredness that belongs to a premodern woman making bread with her child. You can't CONSTRUCT that. You can only remember it.

### The Physical Aftereffects

After a major memory (a Scene, usually), Lena's body responds:

- **Fatigue.** Not sleepy-tired. Wrung-out tired. Like she's been running two operating systems at once and her hardware is overheating.
- **Hunger.** Intense, specific, almost animalistic hunger. After the kitchen memory, she ate three servings of chicken and two pieces of bread and was still hungry. Her body seems to need fuel to process whatever just happened.
- **Tingling.** In her hands, especially. The fingertips buzz for ten to twenty minutes after a Scene. Like they're still feeling the other hands. The other textures. The flour. The grass. The parchment.
- **The hum.** This is the strangest one. After a major memory, she hums. Involuntarily. A low, steady tone — always the same note, always in the same register. She's not aware she's doing it until someone points it out ("You're humming again, Lena." "Am I? Sorry."). She doesn't know what the note is. She doesn't know that it's 31 Hz. She doesn't know that it's the exact complement of her sister's base frequency. She just hums it. Like breathing out.

## PART 7: THE CONFUSION — "Am I Making This Up?"

### The Internal Debate

Lena is smart. Lena is rational. Lena got a 96 on her AP Psych midterm last year. She knows about confirmation bias. She knows about false memories. She knows that the brain is a pattern-completion machine that will happily construct vivid, emotionally compelling experiences out of fragments and fill in the gaps with fiction.

She also knows what she knows.

The debate runs constantly. Like a background app draining her battery:

**FOR "This is real":**
- The specificity. The details are too granular, too consistent, too historically accurate (when she checks, which she tries not to do but can't help) to be fabrications.
- The embodiment. She can't imagine the sensation of kneading bread in a body she's never had. She can't CONSTRUCT the weight of a three-year-old on a hip she doesn't own.
- The names. The names arrive complete, with emotional resonance attached. They don't feel like words her brain generated. They feel like people she's LOST.
- The consistency. The same memories return. The same details. They don't shift or morph the way dreams do. The street is always the same street. The kitchen is always the same kitchen.

**FOR "This is my brain being weird":**
- She's seventeen. Seventeen-year-olds are dramatic. Their brains are still developing. Hormones are doing things. Maybe this is just an elaborate form of daydreaming.
- She reads a lot. She watches period dramas. Maybe she's constructing memories from media she's consumed and forgotten.
- The memories are all of women. All from the past. All from Europe or the Middle East. Maybe she's subconsciously creating a narrative of past selves because she's searching for identity — a common teenage thing. The counselor at school talked about "identity formation" at the beginning-of-year assembly. Maybe this is that. Weird, but that.
- She's stressed. Junior year. College prep. The Tomás situation. Her parents' expectations. Stress does things to the brain. Maybe this is dissociation. Escapism. A coping mechanism that happens to look like reincarnation.

**THE TIE-BREAKER (the thing that won't let her dismiss it):**
- Rue de la Huchette is a real street in Paris. She Googled it. It exists. It's in the 5th arrondissement. It's one of the oldest streets in the city. The description she wrote in her notebook — before Googling — matches the historical appearance of the street in the pre-modern era. She described the tanner's workshop. There WAS a tanner's district nearby in the medieval period. She described the fountain. There was a fountain at the intersection of Rue de la Huchette and Rue de la Harpe. It was removed in the 19th century.

She didn't know any of this. She's never studied Parisian geography. She's never been to France.

How do you explain knowing the layout of a street that was demolished 200 years ago?

You don't. And that's the problem.

### The 3 AM Stare

Some nights, after a memory, she lies in bed and stares at the ceiling and has the conversation with herself:

*Okay. Let's say it's real. Let's say, just for the sake of argument, that I'm remembering past lives.*

*What does that MEAN?*

*It means I've been alive before. Multiple times. As different people, in different places, in different centuries.*

*Why am I remembering NOW?*

*I don't know.*

*Why ME?*

*I don't know.*

*Am I special?*

*I don't WANT to be special. I want to pass AP Chem and figure out if Tomás likes me and get into a decent college and have a normal life.*

*But—*

*No. No "but." I'm not a protagonist. I'm not chosen. I'm a girl from Rogers Park who plays cello and has a weird thing happening in her head and is probably overthinking it.*

*But the street—*

*Shut up about the street.*

She rolls over. Closes her eyes. The hum rises in her chest — 31 Hz, steady, automatic — and she doesn't fight it because fighting it keeps her awake. She lets it carry her toward sleep.

In the bedroom below hers (when she was younger) or across the city (now), Zara hears the hum. Not with her ears. With the part of her that's always listening. She strengthens the shielding frequency slightly. Lena's hum settles. The house goes quiet.

Neither sister knows the other is awake.

The top comment:

Lena closes her laptop. Opens it again. Reads more.

### The Taxonomy She Finds

What she learns, over the course of several late-night research sessions:

**Past life regression.** A therapy technique (usually hypnosis-based) where practitioners guide people to access past-life memories. Practiced by actual licensed therapists in some cases, though the mainstream psychological establishment is skeptical. Research by Dr. Ian Stevenson (University of Virginia) documented over 2,500 cases of children who claimed to remember previous lives, with verified details matching deceased individuals.

**Spontaneous past-life recall.** What Lena has. Memories that arise without hypnosis, meditation, or any deliberate practice. More common in children (ages 2-7, usually fading by 8). Extremely rare in teenagers. Even rarer starting at sixteen.

**Xenoglossy.** The ability to speak or understand a language one has never learned. Lena doesn't speak French or Turkish or whatever Slavic language was in the field memory — but she UNDERSTOOD them, in the memories. The meaning came through even though the words didn't translate.

**Birthmarks and phobias.** Some researchers claim that past-life trauma manifests as birthmarks in the corresponding location or as inexplicable phobias. Lena doesn't have birthmarks she can connect to anything. But she's always been afraid of house fires — an intense, disproportionate fear that has no basis in her actual experience. She wonders.

**The age question.** Most documented cases of spontaneous recall begin in early childhood and END by age seven. Lena's started at sixteen. This doesn't fit any pattern she can find. There's no documented case she can locate of someone beginning to spontaneously remember past lives in their LATE TEENS.

This scares her more than anything else. Not the memories themselves — the fact that they don't match the established pattern. Because if she were a standard case, she could point to Stevenson's research and say "This is documented." But she's an anomaly within an anomaly. A person experiencing a phenomenon that most people don't believe in, at an age when that phenomenon doesn't normally occur.

She is, as far as she can tell, the only person like her.

(She's not. But the reasons she's not are written in a frequency she can't perceive, in a language her sister speaks fluently, in a part of the story neither of them has access to yet.)

---

### The Things That Help

What she reads that makes her feel less alone:

- The r/pastlives community. 480,000 people. Even if half of them are delusional, that's 240,000 people who have had experiences they can't explain. Lena doesn't post. She doesn't comment. She reads. The reading is enough.

- A book she finds at the library: *Many Lives, Many Masters* by Dr. Brian Weiss. A Yale-educated psychiatrist who accidentally regressed a patient to past lives during standard therapy. She reads it in two days. She doesn't know if she believes it. But she doesn't NOT believe it. And the fact that a Yale psychiatrist wrote it makes her feel less crazy.

- A podcast episode (found through Reddit) about children who remember past lives. An interview with a researcher who says: "The question isn't whether these memories are real. The question is: what do we mean by 'real'? If a four-year-old can provide verifiable historical details about a deceased person they've never heard of, the SOURCE of that information is less important than the FACT of it."

What she reads that terrifies her:

- Stories of people whose memories intensified over time. Who went from flashes to scenes to full immersion. Who lost the ability to distinguish between THIS life and the recalled ones. Who had breakdowns.

- A post titled: "I remember 7 lives and I can't find the exit." The poster described a feeling of being LAYERED — multiple selves stacked on top of each other, each with their own emotions, their own attachments, their own grief. "It's not that I'm confused about who I am," they wrote. "It's that there's TOO MUCH of who I am."

Lena reads this at 2 AM and has to get up and stand at the window and breathe until her heart rate drops. Because *too much of who I am* is exactly what she's afraid of. She's seventeen. She has one life. She can barely manage the college application process. She cannot absorb five more lives on top of this one.

She closes the browser. Clears the history. Gets into bed.

The hum starts. 31 Hz. She lets it carry her.

## PART 9: WHO SHE ALMOST TELLS — "And Why She Doesn't"

They're in Priya's car. Parked outside a Dunkin' Donuts because they went inside and the line was too long so they're eating the donuts Priya keeps in her glove compartment (Priya keeps emergency donuts in her glove compartment; this is one of many reasons Lena loves her).

Kitchens. Warm. Inside her chest.

### Why She Can't Tell Anyone

Lena has reasons. Good ones. Or at least, reasons that feel good at 3 AM when she's arguing with herself:

**1. She doesn't want to be diagnosed.** If she tells a therapist, she'll get a label. Depersonalization disorder. Dissociative episode. Something with a DSM-5 number. The memories will become pathology. She'll get medication. The medication might stop the memories. And she doesn't know if she wants them to stop.

(This is the complicated part. She's scared. She's confused. She also, secretly, doesn't want to lose them. Because the memories, for all their strangeness, feel like BELONGING. Like she's more than she thought she was. Like there's a depth to her existence that extends beyond Rogers Park and AP Chem and the question of whether Tomás likes her. The memories are terrifying. They're also the most real thing that's ever happened to her.)

**2. She doesn't want to worry her parents.** Monique will mobilize. Ante will go quiet in the way he goes quiet when he's scared. The household will reorganize around Lena's "problem" and she'll become the center of concern and she can't STAND being the center of concern.

**3. She doesn't want to be "the weird girl."** She is seventeen. High school is a social ecosystem with razor-thin margins. If Priya tells Dylan and Dylan tells his girlfriend and his girlfriend tells someone in orchestra, Lena becomes "the girl who thinks she lived past lives." This is social suicide of the quiet, permanent variety. Not bullying — just distance. The slow withdrawal of people who decide you're too strange to be around comfortably.

**4. She doesn't want to burden Zara.** This is the deepest reason and the one she examines least. Zara already carries something — Lena can feel it, has always felt it. Some weight. Some secret labor that shows up as fatigue and guardedness and the way she sometimes looks at Lena with an expression that's too big for the moment. Lena doesn't know what it is. But she knows she doesn't want to add to it.

So she carries it alone. Seventeen years old. Past lives pooling in her chest like water in a basement. Rising slowly. Not yet dangerous.

Not yet.

## PART 10: SCHOOL AND FRIENDS — "The Mask and the Music"

### How It Changes Things

The memories don't break her life. They just... change the color of it.

She's still Lena. Still goes to school, does homework, texts Priya, watches Netflix, eats dinner with her family. The external architecture of her life is untouched.

But the INTERIOR is shifting.

**In class:** She can't look at history the same way. Mrs. Delaney talks about the Industrial Revolution and Lena feels the phantom ache of factory work in someone else's back. They read about colonialism and Lena's chest tightens with a grief that doesn't belong to this century. She writes better papers now — her AP History essays have a specificity and an emotional depth that Mrs. Delaney has noticed. "Lena, your essay on the French Revolution was remarkably vivid. Where did you get the detail about the baker's wife?" Lena shrugs. "Research." (It wasn't research.)

**With friends:** She's slightly more present and slightly more distant simultaneously. Present because the memories have taught her that MOMENTS MATTER — that the specific, ordinary details of a life are what you remember when the life is over. So she's more attentive. More likely to actually LISTEN when Priya talks about her mom or Dylan does his trumpet impression or Amara reads a poem. She's also distant because she can't share the biggest thing happening in her interior life, which means every conversation has a floor she can't let people through.

**With Tomás:** This is where it gets complicated. Because the memories include love.

Not the romantic, movie kind. The EMBODIED kind. She remembers holding someone's hand on a rooftop. She remembers the specific feeling of being in bed next to someone who's already asleep and listening to their breathing and thinking: *I would stay in this moment for a thousand years.* She remembers what it feels like to KNOW someone — really know them, over years, through difficulty, through children, through loss.

She's seventeen. She's never been in a relationship. But she carries the emotional knowledge of multiple loves that lasted lifetimes.

This makes her... strange to be around, romantically. Not in a way Tomás can identify — he just knows that Lena Kovač makes him feel seen in a way that no other girl at Sullivan does. That when she looks at him, she's looking at something BEHIND his face, and it's unsettling and magnetic in equal measure.

"You look at people weird," he told her once, smiling to take the edge off.

"Weird how?"

"Like you already know them."

She laughed it off. But her hands were shaking under the table.

### The Cello

The cello is changing too.

Lena started cello at nine. Walked into the music room at school and put her hand on it and said "This one." No deliberation. No comparison. She didn't try the violin or the viola or the bass. She knew.

(She didn't know WHY she knew. Zara knows why: the cello's fundamental range — 65 Hz to 1,047 Hz — sits directly in the complement zone of Zara's 62 Hz base frequency. Lena's soul chose the instrument that resonates with her sister. It's not coincidence. It's not preference. It's physics wearing the costume of taste.)

She's good. Not prodigy-good — she's never been the kind of musician who makes people gasp. She's technically proficient, musically sensitive, and practices with the disciplined consistency of someone who finds peace in the repetition. First chair since last spring. Her tone is warm. Her vibrato is natural. Mr. Reiner says she has "an old sound" and means it as a compliment.

Since the memories started, the cello has become something else.

She plays the Dvořák Largo and she's in the field. She plays Bach's Suite No. 1 in G Major and her hands move with a certainty that isn't hers — they belong to someone who's played for longer than Lena's been alive. She plays Barber's *Adagio for Strings* and the grief that comes out of the instrument is too big for her seventeen years and Mr. Reiner stops the rehearsal and says "Lena, that was... where did that come from?" and she says "I just felt it" because what else can she say?

The cello has become a channel. Not in the supernatural sense — she doesn't produce frequencies she shouldn't or make impossible sounds or resonate through solid matter (that's her sister's territory, and she doesn't know that). But in the emotional sense: the cello lets her EXPRESS the memories without revealing them. She can pour the weight of five lifetimes into a Bach suite and what comes out sounds like "a talented teenager with unusual emotional maturity" instead of "a girl who remembers being a grandmother."

The cello is the one place the memories are a gift instead of a burden.

She practices more now. An hour a day has become two. She tells herself it's because the recital is coming up. It's not. It's because the cello is the only space where she doesn't have to pretend.

## PART 11: BAKA — "The Grandmother Who Sees"

### Ivana Kovač

Ante's mother. Seventy-four. Lives in Split, Croatia, in the same apartment she's lived in since 1978. Comes to Chicago every other year. Calls on Sundays. Sends gifts for birthdays — always something specific, always something right. The gold cross for Lena's sixteenth. A leather journal for Lena's seventeenth ("For your thoughts, mala." How did she know Lena needed a journal?).

Baka Ivana is the kind of old woman who makes a room go quiet when she enters — not from volume or presence, but from WEIGHT. She carries the weight of a life lived through a war, through displacement, through the rebuilding of a country. She has opinions about everything and voices them in a mix of Croatian and heavily accented English that somehow carries more authority than either language alone.

She noticed Zara first.

"That child is not a child," she told Ante when Zara was three. They were sitting in the kitchen of the Chicago house — Ivana's first visit to America. Zara was on the floor, supposedly playing with blocks but actually building structures that followed the geometric principles of crystalline lattice formation (she was three; nobody noticed the geometry).

"She's just smart, Mama," Ante said.

"Smart is not what I said. I said she's not a child. She's something older wearing a child's face. I've seen this before."

"Where have you seen this?"

Ivana crossed herself. Didn't answer.

Over the years, Ivana watched Zara the way you watch a flame — with appreciation and a healthy distance. She noticed the growing talent, the effortless musicality, the way food responded to Zara's hands as though the ingredients WANTED to please her. She noticed the plants. She noticed that the Kovač household was different from any household she'd ever visited — warmer, more alive, humming with something she couldn't name but could feel in her bones.

She never told Ante her theory. But she has one. It involves the old stories — the ones her grandmother told her in a fishing village on the Dalmatian coast, about beings who come to Earth in human bodies and bring the garden with them. She doesn't call them aliens. She calls them *poslanici* — the sent ones.

---

### Baka and Lena

Ivana's relationship with Lena is different from her relationship with Zara.

With Zara, there is reverence and distance. Ivana treats Zara with the careful respect you show something powerful and not entirely comprehensible. She loves her granddaughter. She also watches her the way a farmer watches the weather.

With Lena, there is warmth and recognition of a different kind.

Ivana sees something in Lena that she recognizes from her OWN mother — Lena's great-grandmother, Kata, who died in 1989. Kata had what the village called "the memory" — she would sometimes speak of places she'd never been, describe scenes from centuries past with a specificity that couldn't be explained by education (she had none) or imagination (she was the most practical woman in the village). The priest said she had a gift. The neighbors said she was odd. Kata herself said nothing about it. She just lived with it the way you live with a crooked knee — not complaining, not explaining, just accommodating.

When Lena was born, Ivana held her in the hospital and felt something she couldn't articulate — a vibration, a resonance, a sense that this baby carried more than one life's worth of... what? Soul? Weight? History?

She said, to Ante, in Croatian: "This one has been here before."

Ante, who was already carrying the cassette and the awareness of Zara and the weight of everything he couldn't say, just nodded.

### The Phone Calls

Lena calls Baka every other Sunday. They talk for fifteen to thirty minutes, in a mix of English and Croatian. (Lena's Croatian is passable — better than Marko's, not as fluent as Zara's. She understands more than she speaks.)

The calls are usually about school, family, what Lena is eating (Ivana is convinced that American food is poison and that Monique is the only thing standing between her grandchildren and nutritional collapse). But sometimes they wander.

**A call from February, junior year:**

"Baka, can I ask you something?"

"Always."

"When you were young... did Prabaka Kata ever talk about remembering things from before?"

Silence on the line. The kind of silence that comes from Split, from a seventy-four-year-old woman sitting in her apartment with the Adriatic visible through the window and the weight of three generations of unsaid things on her shoulders.

"Why do you ask this?"

"Just curious. Mama Monique was talking about family history and—"

"Your mother doesn't know about Kata's memories."

"...what memories?"

Another silence. Lena can hear the clock in Baka's apartment — a wall clock that's been ticking since before Lena was born.

"Kata had an old soul," Ivana says carefully. "She remembered things. Places. Names. She would say things that nobody understood — describe a street in a city she had never visited. Know the name of a church in a country she had never been to."

Lena's hands are tight on the phone. "Did people think she was crazy?"

"Some people. Not the people who mattered."

"What did you think?"

"I thought my mother was the bravest woman I knew. Because she lived with two lives inside her and she never let either one win."

Lena is crying. Silently. Tears running down her face, phone pressed to her ear, sitting on her bed in the room that used to be Zara's.

"Baka?"

"Yes, mala?"

"Did it... was it hard for her? The remembering?"

"I think," Ivana says slowly, "that it was like carrying two bags of groceries. Manageable. Heavy sometimes. You learn which shoulder to use."

"But what if it gets heavier?"

A long pause. Ivana's voice, when it comes back, is softer. Warmer. The voice of a grandmother who knows exactly what her granddaughter is asking and why.

"Then you call your Baka. And she helps you carry it."

Lena can't speak for a moment.

"Baka, I—"

"I know, mala. I know. It is the same for you, isn't it?"

Lena doesn't answer. She doesn't need to.

"You listen to me," Ivana says. "You are not broken. You are not sick. You have a big soul and it is remembering the road it has walked before. This is not a problem. This is a gift. But like all gifts, it is heavy, and you need to learn to hold it."

"How?"

"You live your life, Lena. THIS life. You go to school. You play your cello. You love your family. You let the memories come when they come and you let them go when they go. You don't chase them. You don't run from them. You walk BESIDE them. Like old friends who visit without warning."

"Did Prabaka Kata do that?"

"She did. For eighty-seven years. And she died at peace."

Lena wipes her face. Breathes.

"Thank you, Baka."

"Every other Sunday, mala. I am here."

She hangs up. Sits in the quiet. And feels, for the first time since the memories started, something other than confusion.

She feels *held.*

### What Baka Doesn't Say

Ivana hangs up the phone and sits for a long time.

She doesn't tell Lena that Kata's memories started late, too — not in childhood but in her thirties, after she married Ivana's father and moved to the coast. That something about the new house — the garden, the salt air, the specific quality of the light — triggered what had been dormant.

She doesn't tell Lena that Kata's memories came with a hum. A low, steady tone that Kata would produce in her sleep. Always the same note.

She doesn't tell Lena about the theory she's been building for thirty years — that the Kovač family carries something. Not all of them. Not every generation. But SOME of them, in SOME lines, have a thinness to them. A permeability. As though the wall between this life and the ones before it is thinner in their blood.

She doesn't connect Lena's remembering to Zara's presence. She doesn't know about the frequency field or the food or the plants. She attributes Lena's gift to the family line — to Kata's blood, running true.

She is wrong about the cause but right about the fact: Lena Kovač is remembering. And it will get stronger. And the girl will need more help than a phone call can provide.

Ivana says a prayer. Crosses herself. Goes to the window. Looks at the Adriatic.

Somewhere in Chicago, her granddaughter is sitting in a bedroom full of alien-enhanced plants, in a house that hums with non-terrestrial frequency, eating food grown from alien-calibrated soil, holding a cello whose fundamental resonance matches the deepest note of her soul.

And the memories are coming faster.

## PART 12: HER OWN PERSON — "Lena, Not Zara's Sister"

### The Things That Are Hers

It would be easy — and wrong — to define Lena by her sister or her memories. She is more than both.

**She is funny.** Not Zara's bone-dry, survival-mechanism humor. Lena's humor is warm and self-deprecating and occasionally devastating. She's the friend who says the thing everyone was thinking but wouldn't say, and she says it gently enough that it lands as comedy instead of cruelty. Priya calls it "Lena's honesty thing" — the ability to tell the truth in a way that makes people laugh instead of wince.

Example: Marko, after a bad basketball game, slamming around the kitchen. Lena, without looking up from her homework: "You know the ball is supposed to go IN the basket, right? Just checking."

Marko: "I will END you."

Lena: "You missed six free throws. You couldn't end a sentence."

Monique, from the other room: "LENA."

But Marko is already laughing. And the tension is broken. And Lena goes back to her homework with a tiny smile, because making her brother laugh after a bad day is one of the small, good things she's good at.

**She reads.** Voraciously. Omnivorously. Novels, mostly — literary fiction that her classmates think is boring and that she finds nourishing in a way she can't explain. She's read Toni Morrison three times. She cried through *Beloved.* She read *One Hundred Years of Solitude* at fifteen and carried it around for weeks like a holy object. She's working through Ursula K. Le Guin right now — *The Left Hand of Darkness* — and it's doing something to her brain that she can't articulate. Something about the way Le Guin writes about OTHER WORLDS makes her feel less alone in this one.

She doesn't connect this to the memories. She doesn't notice that her taste in literature gravitates toward stories about displacement, about exile, about people who don't quite fit in the world they've been given. She just knows what she likes.

**She draws.** Not well. Not with training. She doodles in her margins — faces, buildings, plants. The drawings are quick and rough and nobody would frame them. But the faces she draws are specific. They're PEOPLE. Not generic figures — individuals with particular noses, particular jawlines, particular expressions. She draws them without thinking and then looks at the page and doesn't recognize them.

She has a notebook — the leather journal from Baka — that she draws in at night. The pages are filling with faces she can't name. Women, mostly. Different eras. Different features. All drawn with a certainty that suggests portraiture rather than imagination.

She doesn't show anyone the notebook. It lives under her mattress. Some nights she falls asleep holding it.

**She bakes.** This is the newest thing — started in January, after the kitchen memory. She came downstairs one Saturday morning and started making bread. Not from a recipe. From MEMORY — but not her memory. Her hands knew the process. The kneading, the folding, the shape of the loaf. She used Monique's kitchen (Monique's flour, Zara's yeast — even the yeast carries frequency, though nobody knows this) and produced, on her first try, a loaf of bread that made Ante put down his newspaper and stare.

"Where did you learn this?" he asked.

"YouTube," she said.

"This is not from YouTube. This is your Baka's bread. This is Kata's bread."

She didn't know what to say. She hadn't TRIED to make Kata's bread. She'd just... made bread. And it came out tasting like a dead woman's recipe.

She bakes every Saturday now. Bread, mostly. Sometimes pastries. The recipes aren't from books or videos — they arrive the way the memories arrive. Her hands know what to do. She follows them.

Monique has started featuring Lena's bread on Instagram. "My daughter made this. She's SEVENTEEN. I don't know where she learned this. It tastes like time travel." 47,000 likes.

**She walks.** Long walks, alone, after school. Through Rogers Park, down to the lake, along the shore path. She wears her earbuds but doesn't always play music — sometimes she just wants the seal of headphones without the input. The walks are processing time. She walks and thinks and lets the memories surface and settle and fade. It's not meditation. It's just movement. The body in motion, the mind allowed to wander, the thin places between lives permeable and quiet.

She's been walking more since the memories started. Two miles has become four. Four has become six. Priya worries she's "over-exercising." She's not. She's just giving her internal world enough space to breathe.

### The Identity Question

Here is the question that lives underneath everything else:

*Who am I?*

This is a normal seventeen-year-old question. EVERY teenager asks it. Identity formation. Developmental stage. Totally standard.

But for Lena, the question has a second layer:

*WHO am I — when "I" might include Marguerite, and Elif, and the woman in the kitchen with Maren, and the old woman hanging linens in the Mediterranean sun, and the girl on the rooftop holding someone's hand?*

She is Lena. She is CERTAIN she is Lena. The memories don't displace her — they don't take over, don't erase her current self, don't make her feel like she's losing herself. She is firmly, stubbornly, completely Lena Kovač.

But she is also carrying the emotional residue of lives she didn't choose and can't release. The tenderness of the kitchen mother. The yearning of the rooftop girl. The tiredness of the old woman. The grief of whoever mourned Elif. These feelings layer on top of her own feelings and make her... more. Denser. A seventeen-year-old with the emotional weight of a much longer existence.

She's not old. She's not wise beyond her years (she hates that phrase — what does it even mean? Wise is a word for trees and grandmothers, not for girls who can't figure out college applications). She's just... bigger on the inside than she should be. Like a room that's been expanded without moving the walls.

And she doesn't know what to do with all the space.

## PART 13: THE DRAMATIC IRONY — "Two Sisters, Two Secrets, One House"

### What's Really Happening

Here is the story that neither sister can see because they're both inside it:

**Zara** believes she is protecting Lena from something external. She maintains the dampening field. She shields the complement frequency. She scans for threats. She believes that Lena is dormant — either an alien soul in deep sleep or a natural human whose complement resonance is a cosmic coincidence. Either way, the job is the same: keep Lena hidden. Keep her safe. Keep her NORMAL.

But Zara is the CAUSE. Not the protector. The cause.

Seventeen years of frequency dampening, seventeen years of alien-enhanced food, seventeen years of proximity to a being who operates on non-terrestrial frequencies — this is what's opening Lena's memories. Zara's presence hasn't kept Lena dormant. It's done the opposite. It's been slowly, gently, accidentally peeling back the layers of Lena's soul like sunlight opening a flower.

The dampening field was supposed to suppress Lena's outward signal. And it does. But the field itself IS a signal — and Lena's been absorbing it. The food was supposed to nourish. And it does. But the frequency content of the food has been quietly recalibrating Lena's neural receptors, widening the bandwidth of her consciousness, thinning the barrier between this life and the ones that came before. The plants were supposed to be... plants. But they carry alien resonance, and Lena has been sleeping in their ambient field for seventeen years.

Zara is the most protective, most devoted, most self-sacrificing sister in the history of any species. And she is, through the sheer accumulation of good intentions, responsible for the very thing she's trying to prevent: Lena's awakening.

But it's not an ALIEN awakening. That's the twist. Lena isn't remembering Za-Rah's world. She's not downloading cosmic histories or frequency highway blueprints. She's remembering EARTH. Her own planet. Her own species. Her own soul's long, winding, deeply human road through incarnation after incarnation of ordinary life.

The frequency exposure didn't make her alien. It made her MORE HUMAN. Fully, deeply, multiply human — connected to the vast chain of her own existence in a way that most people never access.

**Lena** believes she is experiencing something abnormal. Something that might be a gift, might be a pathology, might be both. She doesn't know why it's happening. She doesn't connect it to Zara, to the food, to the plants, to the hum she produces in her sleep. She thinks it's genetic — Kata's gift, passed down through the Kovač blood. She thinks she's the latest in a line of women who remember.

She's half right. There might be a genetic component — Kata DID have the gift, and Ivana's theory about the family line has some truth. But genetics alone wouldn't explain the TIMING. The memories started at sixteen, not at four. They started after a lifetime of exposure to Zara's field. The genetic predisposition was the kindling. Zara's presence was the match.

---

### The Parallel

Both sisters sit at the same dinner table every Sunday and both of them are hiding:

**Zara** hides: her alien identity, her cosmic consciousness, her frequency powers, her 17-year vigilance, her structural fury at the Resonants, her homesickness, her loneliness, the fact that she's been protecting Lena since birth.

**Lena** hides: her past-life memories, her confusion, her late-night Googling, her notebook of faces under the mattress, her Saturday baking that comes from a dead woman's hands, her phone calls with Baka where they talk about things the rest of the family doesn't know about.

They text every day. They love each other completely. They know each other better than anyone else in the world.

And they're both lying.

Not cruelly. Not deceptively. They're lying the way you lie when the truth is too big for the space between two people. When telling would require more than words — it would require a rewriting of the relationship, a reconfiguration of what "sister" means, a confrontation with something that might be too heavy to share even though carrying it alone is unsustainable.

They'll get there. Eventually. The story demands it — two parallel secrets building pressure in the same household, approaching the same inevitable rupture.

But not yet.

For now: Sunday dinner. Pass the bread. How's school? How's work? The chicken's good, Mama. Zara, you need to eat more. Marko, get off your phone.

Normal. Warm. Full of love and full of silence.

And underneath the table, the house hums at 31 Hz, and Lena's hands tingle with the flour of someone else's bread, and Zara's energy expenditure ticks at its constant 15%, and neither sister looks at the other because looking might break the spell — the spell of normalcy, the spell of safety, the spell that says: *We can do this. We can hold our weight. We can carry our secrets alone.*

They can't. Not forever. But they'll try. Because they're Kovač women. And Kovač women carry.

## CODA: A NIGHT IN MARCH

### 11:47 PM

The house is quiet.

Lena is in bed. Earbuds in. Listening to Yo-Yo Ma play the Dvořák — the Largo, again, always the Largo. She has the leather journal on her chest. She drew a face earlier — a woman, middle-aged, stern mouth, soft eyes. She doesn't know who it is. She drew it in twenty minutes and it looks like a photograph.

The Largo reaches the part where the melody opens up — the part that always takes her somewhere.

She closes her eyes.

*She is in the field again. The grass is waist-high. The farmhouse is behind her. The voice is calling — come inside, it's getting late. But she doesn't go inside. Not this time. She stays. She looks up. The sky is enormous — wider than any sky she's seen in Chicago, wider than any sky she's seen in this life. Stars. Thousands of them. A sky so thick with light that it looks like someone spilled milk across the dark.*

*She knows these stars. Not their names — not the astronomical designations. But she knows them the way you know a neighborhood. By feel. By familiarity. By the specific quality of their light.*

*And she feels, standing in this field in a body that isn't hers, in a life that ended centuries ago — she feels HOME. Not homesick. HOME. She is exactly where she belongs. She has always belonged here. On this planet. In these fields. Under these stars.*

*This is not alien nostalgia. This is not cosmic longing. This is the deep, aching, ordinary love of a human being for the Earth she's been walking on for longer than she knows.*

The music fades. She opens her eyes. She's in her bedroom. The tapestry. The string lights. The cello in the corner.

She puts her hand on the journal. On the face she drew. On the woman she doesn't know.

"Who are you?" she whispers.

No answer. Just the hum. 31 Hz. Rising from her chest like breath, like music, like a road that goes backwards and forwards at the same time.

She lets it carry her.

Across the city, in an apartment full of plants, Zara feels the complement frequency pulse — faintly, barely, like a candle flickering in another room. She strengthens the dampening. Checks the field. All clear.

She goes back to her bass. Plays a low note. 62 Hz. The complement of 31.

If you could hear both notes at once — Lena's hum and Zara's string — they would make a complete wave. A signal without gaps. A sound that says: *We are here. We are together. We don't know each other's secrets but we know each other's frequency and that is enough.*

*For now.*

The house settles. The city hums. Two sisters carry their weight.

The story continues.
