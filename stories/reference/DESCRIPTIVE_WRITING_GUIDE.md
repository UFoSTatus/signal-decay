# SIGNAL DECAY — DESCRIPTIVE WRITING GUIDE
## How to Be Richly Descriptive WITHOUT Boring the Reader

---

> *"Description done well creates immersive experiences that make stories feel real without readers noticing they are reading description at all."*
> — Chandler Supple, River Editor

> *"Writing descriptively means selecting the 1-3 sensory details that do the most work, not photographing every surface of a scene."*
> — BestWriting.com

---

## THE GOLDEN RULE: Description Must WORK

Every piece of description in Signal Decay must serve **at least 2 purposes simultaneously**:

| Purpose 1 | + Purpose 2 | = Good Description |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Shows what a place looks like | + Reveals character personality | ✅ |
| Creates sensory immersion | + Establishes mood/atmosphere | ✅ |
| Grounds the reader in location | + Advances emotional arc | ✅ |
| Describes a spirit appearing | + Shows Sunny's emotional state | ✅ |
| Shows what someone's wearing | + Reveals their economic status/identity | ✅ |

**Single-purpose description = boring:**
❌ "The room had a couch, a TV, and a coffee table." (Documentation only. No one cares.)

**Multi-purpose description = captivating:**
✅ "The couch cushions still held the shape of whoever had been sitting there — a deep dent on the left side, like someone heavy had lived in that spot for years. The TV was on but muted, blue light pulsing over everything, and on the coffee table: three days of mugs, each one with a different ring of dried coffee at a different height." (Tells you someone depressed lives here without SAYING depressed.)

## THE 9 TECHNIQUES FOR CAPTIVATING DESCRIPTION

### 1. THE SPOTLIGHT, NOT THE FLOODLIGHT

Don't photograph every surface. Pick the 1-3 details that carry the most weight and let the reader's imagination fill in the rest.

**The principle:** Readers aren't blank slates. Write "a kitchen" and they already HAVE a kitchen in their mind. Your job is to add the 2-3 details that make THIS kitchen different from every kitchen they've ever imagined.

❌ "The kitchen had white cabinets, granite countertops, a stainless steel refrigerator, a gas stove with four burners, a window over the sink looking out at the backyard, and a tile floor."

✅ "The kitchen smelled like burnt rice and Pine-Sol, and there was a Virgin Mary candle on the windowsill with a scorch mark on the wall behind it where the flame had gotten too close."

The second version = you know EXACTLY whose kitchen this is without a single piece of furniture described.

---

### 2. FILTER THROUGH THE CHARACTER

What a character NOTICES tells you who they are. Sunny doesn't see rooms the way other characters do — she sees the emotional residue, the cold spots, the things that aren't quite right.

**The rule:** Describe only what THIS character would actually notice in THIS moment given their emotional state, expertise, and fears.

- A **scared** character notices exits and threats. Not aesthetics.
- A **chef** entering a kitchen notices the smell of overheated fat. Not the paint color.
- A **grieving** character notices everything that's WRONG because something is always wrong now.
- **Sunny** notices: temperature shifts, areas where light bends, the spot where a spirit stands, the hum of frequency — BEFORE she notices normal stuff.

❌ (Generic description): "Sunny walked into the library. It was a large building with tall shelves and quiet carpets."

✅ (Filtered through Sunny): "The library hit her in the sinuses first — old paper and binding glue and something underneath like pennies in a wet palm. The fluorescents buzzed a half-tone flat. Two dead zones: one by the encyclopedias (cold, old, benign — a Loop, probably) and one in the basement stairwell that she'd learned at age nine to never, ever investigate."

---

### 3. THE RIGHT SENSE FOR THE MOMENT

Most writers over-rely on SIGHT. The power move: **open scenes with a non-visual sense.**

**Smell** → Routes through the limbic system (brain's emotional/memory center). One smell places a reader faster than three paragraphs of visual description.

**Sound** → Builds atmosphere and tension. The ABSENCE of sound is even more powerful.

**Touch/Temperature** → Carries emotional tone that visuals can't. Cold = dread. Warmth = safety.

**Taste** → The most intimate sense. Rarely used. When you DO use it, it hits hard.

**The exercise:** For your next scene, BAN sight for the first two sentences. Force smell, sound, or temperature to carry the entry.

❌ (Sight-first): "The hallway was dim with flickering fluorescents."

✅ (Smell-first): "The hallway smelled like floor wax and wet sneakers and then — underneath — something older. Cold. Like opening a window in January when you're not expecting it."

---

### 4. STRONG VERBS > ADJECTIVES

The hierarchy from weakest to strongest:
1. **Generic adjective:** "beautiful" (means nothing specific)
2. **Specific adjective:** "blood-red" (more information)
3. **Specific noun:** "raven" instead of "bird" (visual + connotation)
4. **Strong verb:** "strutted" instead of "walked" (personality + motion)

**The rule:** For every adjective in your draft, ask: could a specific NOUN or a strong VERB do this job better?

❌ "She walked nervously into the large, intimidating room."

✅ "She stood in the doorway longer than she needed to, one hand still on the handle, cataloguing exits."

Zero adjectives about the room. Zero adverbs on her movement. The VERB CHOICE and BEHAVIOR carry everything.

---

### 5. SHOW EMOTION THROUGH BEHAVIOR, NOT LABELS

**Never write:** "She was sad." "He was scared." "They were angry."

These are CONCLUSIONS the reader should reach — not premises you announce.

**Instead:** What would a security camera see? What's the visible behavior?

❌ "She was devastated when she heard the news."

✅ "She set the phone face-down on the counter and stood there for a moment, one hand flat on the tile, before she turned on the faucet and started washing dishes that were already clean."

❌ "Sunny was terrified."

✅ "Sunny's hands went cold and she pressed them flat against her thighs through her jeans, the way she'd learned to do when the shaking wanted to start. She counted backwards from ten in Spanish — diez, nueve, ocho — but only got to siete before the temperature in the room dropped enough to see her breath."

---

### 6. INTERNAL SENSATION (The Felt-Body Sense)

Beyond the five external senses: what does the character's BODY do in response to emotion? The involuntary physical responses that live below conscious control.

- The weight in your stomach before bad news
- The involuntary flinch
- Hands going cold when genuinely frightened
- The hollow ache behind the sternum
- Ears ringing after a shock
- The way your jaw tightens when holding back tears
- Goosebumps that aren't from cold

These AUTONOMIC responses are recognized as TRUE by readers in a way that emotional labels never achieve.

❌ "He was terrified."

✅ "His hands were cold and steady — the worst kind of calm, the kind that comes when the brain stops believing it can do anything useful."

---

### 7. METAPHOR & SIMILE (But Make Them SPECIFIC)

A good comparison does two things: describes the thing AND reveals the character.

❌ Generic: "The room was cold as ice."

✅ Character-specific: "The room was freezer-aisle cold — the kind that hits you when you turn the corner at the grocery store and you're still in your tank top from outside."

That's a comparison a 12-year-old Latina girl would make. It's HER metaphor. It tells you about her world.

❌ Generic: "The spirit looked terrifying."

✅ Sunny's voice: "He looked like someone had left the TV on a dead channel and shaped it into a man. Static given legs."

---

### 8. MOTION KILLS THE CATALOG FEELING

If you describe while characters MOVE, it never feels like you've paused the story.

❌ (Static catalog): "The classroom had rows of desks, a whiteboard at the front, posters of presidents on the walls, and a window with broken blinds."

✅ (Description through motion): "Sunny slid into her desk third row back, the metal legs scraping that sound she hated, and pulled her Trapper Keeper close like a shield. The poster of Lincoln above Ms. Davis's desk had one corner curling down — she'd been staring at that curl all semester — and through the broken blinds, the afternoon light made stripes across Kayla's arm one desk up."

Same information. But the character is DOING something. The description is happening AS she moves. The story never stops.

---

### 9. KNOW WHEN TO SKIP

- High-tension scenes: readers racing through action don't need three paragraphs about architecture. They need to know where to HIDE and whether the DOOR is locked.
- Generic locations: "a coffee shop" is enough if nothing unusual matters.
- Return visits: you described this room already. Don't redescribe. Maybe notice one thing that CHANGED.

**When to go DEEP with description:**
- Establishing a new, important location for the first time
- Emotional beats where you WANT the reader to slow down
- Moments of beauty or horror that earn lingering
- Transitions (walking into a room where something bad happened)
- First appearance of a spirit/entity

## DESCRIPTIVE WRITING MASTERS (Who to Study)

| Author | Their Skill | What to Steal |
|--------|------------|---------------|
| **Cormac McCarthy** | Landscape as emotional state. No quotation marks. Biblical weight. | Description that makes you feel existential dread or awe through LAND |
| **Donna Tartt** | Atmosphere. Immersive sensory density. Every room is a mood. | How to make a reader FEEL the temperature and light of a space |
| **Toni Morrison** | Poetic density. Metaphor as emotional weapon. | How to say something devastating in 11 words |
| **Stephen King** | Specific brand-name details. Everyday made uncanny. | How to make a normal room terrifying through ONE wrong detail |
| **Ray Bradbury** | Liquid, sensory, mythic. Everything alive. | How to make prose feel like music without losing clarity |
| **Gabriel García Márquez** | Lush, sensory, mythic. The mundane made magical. | How to describe everyday things as if they're miraculous |
| **Marilynne Robinson** | Stillness. Light. The sacred in the ordinary. | How to make QUIET description as powerful as action |
| **Virginia Woolf** | Interiority. Time flowing like water. | How to describe the INSIDE of experience |

## SIGNAL DECAY-SPECIFIC DESCRIPTION RULES

### How to Describe Spirits Appearing:

Never just "she saw a ghost." Always describe the ARRIVAL through sensation:

1. **Temperature first** — what the air does before the visual
2. **Sound change** — what drops out, what enters (a hum? silence? pressure in ears?)
3. **The felt-body response** — goosebumps, cold hands, pressure behind eyes, ears popping
4. **THEN the visual** — and make the visual SPECIFIC (not "a ghost" but exactly what she sees: age, clothing, condition, behavior, where they are in the room)
5. **Character reaction** — what Sunny DOES (not feels — does)

**Example:**
> The library smelled like old carpet and something chemical — the air freshener they pumped through the vents that smelled like no flower that ever existed. Sunny was three pages into her book report when the temperature dropped. Not gradually. INSTANTLY. Like someone opened a door to December. Her fingers tightened on her pencil and she stopped breathing for exactly one Mississippi because she'd learned that the first three seconds told you everything. If the cold warmed back up: just a Loop passing through. If it stayed cold and got heavier: someone was here. Someone who knew she could see them.
>
> It stayed.
>
> She put her pencil down carefully, the way you put things down when you don't want to spook a deer, and looked up. Third stack from the left, between Biography and World History, a man was standing sideways with one hand on a book he couldn't pull from the shelf. Old. Maybe seventies when he died. Tweed jacket, the kind professors wear in movies. His fingers kept passing through the book's spine — reaching, closing, reaching, closing — like he'd been trying to grab that book for a very long time.
>
> Loop or Stuck? She watched for the giveaway. One minute. Two.
>
> He turned and looked at her.
>
> Stuck.

### How to Describe the 90s:

Not generic "90s nostalgia." SPECIFIC:
- Brand names: Trapper Keeper, Lisa Frank, FUBU, Capri Sun, Dunkaroos, Blockbuster, AOL
- Sounds: dial-up screech, VHS rewinding, the click-whir of a Walkman opening, the static between channels, the specific beep pattern of a cordless phone low on battery
- Smells: Bath & Body Works cucumber melon, the plastic smell of a new VHS case, the chemical sweetness of fruit Gushers
- Touch: the sticky plasticity of a jelly sandal, the rough velcro of a Trapper Keeper, the weight of a corded phone receiver
- Light: the blue glow of a TV in a dark room (channels signed off at night in the early 90s — test pattern or static), the orange sodium streetlights, the specific green of a Game Boy screen

### How to Describe Food (Important for Sunny):

Food is love, comfort, and grounding for her. Make every food moment VISCERAL:

❌ "She ate empanadas."

✅ "Abuela's empanadas were still warm from the oil, the shell golden and just past crispy where it had puffed, and when Sunny bit in, the filling hit her tongue — beef and sofrito and the tiniest sweetness from raisins that Mami said was wrong but Abuela said was right, and Abuela was always right about empanadas. The oil shone on her fingertips and she licked them one by one because napkins were for quitters."

## THE TEST: READ IT BACK

After writing any descriptive passage, ask:

1. **Can I see/hear/smell/feel it?** If I can only SEE it, add another sense.
2. **Does it reveal character?** If not, filter it through someone's perspective.
3. **Does the story still MOVE?** If description paused the action, weave it into motion.
4. **Is it specific?** Replace every generic word with a SPECIFIC one ("Ford Taurus" not "car").
5. **Would I skip this?** If YES, cut it or make it do more work.
6. **Does it serve at least 2 purposes?** Setting + mood? Character + foreshadowing? Atmosphere + tension?

If it passes all 6: keep it.
If it fails any: rewrite until it works harder.

*"Great description is invisible. Readers finish scenes with vivid impressions without remembering reading descriptive passages. They remember how places FEEL, not how you described them."*
