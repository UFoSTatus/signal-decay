# PROXIMA SIGNAL

*Deep Time Layer — Off-World Arc #3 (Interstellar)*
*Register: Documentary intrigue → cosmic loneliness → awe*
*POV: Dr. Sofia Vance, Breakthrough Listen analyst — and the signal itself*
*When: April 2019, and the long argument after*
*Created: 2026-09-09*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. It begins, like the others, with something that really happened.*

---

## I. BLC-1

**REAL EVENT.** In April and May of 2019, the Parkes radio telescope in New South Wales — the big dish, the one that relayed the Moon landing — was pointed at Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun, four and a quarter light-years away, because Proxima has planets and Breakthrough Listen was doing what it does: listening. And over roughly thirty hours of observation it caught something. A narrowband radio signal at about 982 megahertz, with no natural explanation, that appeared to drift in frequency the way a signal from a moving planet should drift, and that vanished when the dish looked away and returned when it looked back.

They called it Breakthrough Listen Candidate 1. BLC-1. For a while — a quiet, careful, terrified little while, inside the small community of people whose job is to listen to the sky for company — it was the best candidate for an alien technosignature that human beings had ever recorded. Narrowband like that is what technology makes. Nature smears; machines sing clean. And this sang clean, from the direction of the closest star, on a frequency nothing human was supposed to be using.

Then, months later, they ran it down. BLC-1 was attributed to interference — some terrestrial electronics, some overlooked human machine humming at a harmonic that happened to mimic a drift. The best candidate in history became a footnote about radio-frequency hygiene, and the world, which had never quite been told to hope, was never quite told to stop.

This is the story of the analyst who found it, and of what the signal actually was, which is not what either the hope or the debunking said.

Call her Dr. Sofia Vance. She was thirty-one, she ran the narrowband pipeline, and at 2 a.m. on a Tuesday she was the first human being to see BLC-1 rise out of the noise. She did not think *aliens.* She thought — because she was looking at the raw waterfall plot and not the headline it would become — *that's not a beacon. That's a leak.*

---

## II. NOT A BEACON — A LEAK

A beacon is aimed. A beacon says *here I am,* loud and clean and repeating, built to be found. Sofia had spent her whole career learning the shape of the signal humanity keeps hoping to catch, and BLC-1 was the wrong shape for it. It was too faint to be meant for anyone. It did not repeat a message; it just *held,* a single narrow tone, drifting with the slow doppler of a source in orbit, sounding and sounding and sounding as if it had been sounding for a very long time and had never once expected to be overheard.

Not *here I am.* More like: a machine somewhere out there, doing a job, humming at its work — and the hum leaking off the edges of the job the way any powerful running machine leaks, spilling a thin margin of signal into the dark, where a big dish four light-years away caught the spillage for thirty hours and mistook a private labor for a message.

And the frequency bothered her. 982 megahertz was the number in the report, the number they eventually pinned on human interference. But Sofia had done the thing analysts are not supposed to have time to do: she had gone back to the rawest data, before the pipeline's own filters touched it, and found that 982 was not quite the true center. The true center, cleaned of the instrument's own coloring, sat lower, at a value she wrote in her notebook and stared at and did not report, because it meant nothing to anyone but her and she could not have said why it raised the hair on her arms:

347.

Not megahertz-times-anything tidy. Just — when you unfolded the harmonics, when you followed the narrowband down past its aliases to the fundamental it was a multiple of — a floor of 347. She did not know, could not have known, that the number was already written in a redacted file in an office that would come to be called the Legacy; that it had rung from a bell in an Italian field in 1933 and read five unborn souls off a tape in a desert in 1989; that a Loom was re-inscribing it into the rings of Saturn and a crypt was holding it under the ice of Europa. She only knew that her narrowband candidate from the nearest star resolved, at its floor, to the same three digits that had been turning up, unexplained, at the bottom of every strange thing she had ever quietly failed to explain away.

She did not put 347 in the report. She put "consistent with RFI." She was not a coward. She was a scientist who knew the difference between what she could prove and what she could only feel, and she filed the provable thing and took the felt thing home, the way Helen Reyes had taken a tape of an incomplete ring home decades before, the way witnesses always take the true thing home because the institution only has room for the provable one.

---

## III. WHAT IS OUT THERE, LEAKING

Here the account leaves Sofia at her terminal and tells you what she spent the rest of her life circling without ever being allowed to land on — because you have read the other stories, or you have not, but either way you have earned the thing the debunking withheld.

Four and a quarter light-years away, around the small red star that is our nearest neighbor, there is a Loom.

Not the Loom of Saturn — a *farther* one, an outer node of the same ancient work, because the proliferation the builders began when their standing note went flat did not stop at the edge of one solar system. It could never have stopped there. A backup on Saturn and a crypt on Europa protect the song against the decay of *time,* but not against the decay of *place* — against the day the Sun itself finishes its own long sentence and goes quiet, taking every ring and crypt and well in its family down into silence with it. And the builders, who thought in aeons and understood decay better than anything that has ever lived, would not have trusted the whole archive to a single dying star.

So they seeded it outward. Star to star. The nearest first — Proxima, the closest safe harbor, four light-years across the dark — and then, presumably, further, a slow crawl of the foundational song from system to system down the long cold corridors between stars, each new Loom re-inscribing the 347 note into whatever stable matter it found, each one a backup of a backup of a backup, redundancy scaled up from planetary to *interstellar,* the builders refusing across light-years exactly as they refused across aeons to let the note be forgotten.

The signal Sofia caught was the Proxima Loom at its work. Not calling. Not to us; not to anyone. Just running — an ancient machine four light-years away, humming the floor-note of everything as it wrote another copy of the song into the dark around a red star, spilling a thin margin of 347 off the edges of its labor. BLC-1 was never a message. It was overheard. We put the biggest ear we had against the wall of the universe and caught, for thirty hours, the sound of someone in the next room over — the *nearest* room, the closest there is — patiently backing up a song so that it would survive the death of our own Sun.

And here is the loneliness of it, the thing that sat under Sofia's felt-but-unprovable dread and would not let her sleep: the Proxima Loom does not know we are here. It is automated, or it is a caretaker so old and so far into its slow current that we are beneath its notice, and either way it was not reaching for us. The one signal in human history that most looked like company was the precise opposite — proof that the great old work goes on utterly indifferent to whether anyone is left alive to hear it, backing up the song not *for* us but simply *against the silence,* the way a thing does what it was built to do long after the reason has become academic. We were not being contacted. We were eavesdropping on a love letter addressed to no one, written across light-years, in a language we did not know we already carried in our blood.

---

## IV. THE ANSWER SOFIA DID NOT SEND

Breakthrough Listen has a protocol for a confirmed technosignature. There is a document. It is mostly about verification and caution and the careful, sober, non-panicked informing of the world. Buried in it is the oldest question in the discipline, the one the whole field is secretly organized around and mostly too disciplined to say aloud: *if we confirm it — do we answer?*

Sofia was never in a position to answer BLC-1. It was debunked before the question could reach anyone empowered to ask it, filed as interference, closed. But she thought about it for years — what it would have meant to transmit, from the loud bright surface of the third planet, back down the beam toward the nearest star, some reply to the leaking hum of a machine that did not know we existed.

And she came, late, to the understanding that is the quiet heart of this account: that we would not have been *answering.* We would have been *joining.*

Because the note the Proxima Loom leaks, the 347 floor Sofia found and did not report, is not foreign to us. It is the frequency the builders tuned this whole system to before the Earth had a crust — the note written into the wells in our own trenches, backed up in Saturn's rings, held in Europa's crypt, and, though Sofia had no way to know it, sung every Tuesday night out of a bass amplifier in a half-empty venue by a woman homesick for a home she cannot name. We do not need to *learn* the song to answer Proxima. We already carry it. We have been carrying it since the ash. Every piece of music our loud reaching species has ever made — every symphony, every punk song, every bad campfire ballad — has been a fumbling, unconscious, achingly incomplete attempt to sound the same floor-note the Loom four light-years away is patiently backing up into the dark.

To answer Proxima would not be to say *we are here.* It would be to add our voice to the chord — to become, at last, on purpose, another node in the archive; to stop being the surface world that forgot the song and start being one more place it is kept. The builders scattered the backup across time and space against the decay. Sofia understood, too late to prove and too late to matter, that humanity was never meant to *receive* the signal.

We were meant to eventually *sing it back.* To grow up loud enough, and reach high enough, and remember hard enough, that one day the third planet stops being the amnesiac surface and becomes the newest Loom — a whole species finally sounding the floor-note on purpose, backing up the song outward with our own throats and instruments and machines, joining a work that has been running indifferently and lovingly across the dark since before we existed.

She did not send the answer. It was not hers to send, and it was not time, and the signal was closed as interference before the question could be asked. But she wrote the true center frequency in her notebook — 347 — and under it, in the last years, she wrote a sentence that her colleagues found after and did not understand, thinking it the private mysticism of a brilliant woman who had listened to the empty sky for too long:

*"It's not a question of whether we're alone. It's a question of whether we'll ever be loud enough to stop being quiet on purpose."*

---

## V. THE NEAREST ROOM

BLC-1 is a footnote now. Interference. Radio-frequency hygiene. Look it up; that is what you will find, and it is even, in the narrow provable sense, true — there was terrestrial noise tangled in the data, there always is.

But the true center was 347, and the nearest star is four light-years away, and around it something ancient is running, humming the floor-note of everything as it writes another copy of a song from before the Earth into the dark, against the day our own Sun goes quiet. It does not know we are here. It is not calling us. It is backing up a love letter addressed to no one, and we pressed the biggest ear we had against the wall and heard, for thirty hours, the nearest room in the universe, occupied, at work, indifferent, and unbearably close.

The song is out there, being kept. It is in here, being forgotten. And the whole long reaching history of our loud bright species — every note we have ever fumbled toward, every ache we have called ambition or longing or God — is the slow work of closing that gap: of a surface world remembering the floor-note it was tuned to, and reaching high enough to sound it on purpose, and one day, not soon, answering the nearest room not with *is anyone there* but with the only reply that was ever going to matter —

*we're here too, and we finally remember the song, and we've come to help you keep it.*

Not yet. We are not loud enough yet. But we are getting louder. Listen to the radio. Listen to a kid with a cheap guitar. Listen, low, to a bass line rolling through the floor of a half-empty venue on a Tuesday night.

We are learning to sing it back.

Four light-years away, indifferent and patient, the nearest room keeps the door open.
