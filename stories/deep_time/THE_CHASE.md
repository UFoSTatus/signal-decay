# THE CHASE

*Deep Time Layer — Season 3: The Outcome Files · #1*
*Register: Thriller*
*POV: Capitaine "Ghost" Deprez, Belgian Air Force F-16 pilot*
*When: The night of 30–31 March 1990, over Belgium*
*Created: 2026-09-10*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. It dramatizes a real, documented event — the night two Belgian F-16s chased radar locks they could not see — and tells you what they were actually chasing.*

---

## I. SCRAMBLE

The thing about the Belgian UFO wave that the later documentaries always undersold is how *ordinary* it started. Not a klaxon and a red light. A phone call, to a duty officer, from a police gendarme who sounded embarrassed to be making it: *we have multiple citizens, and now two of our own patrol units, reporting a large triangular object with three lights, hovering, silent, over the Ardennes. Radar at Glons has it. Radar at Semmerzake has it. It is not us. It is not NATO. Can you check.*

Capitaine Deprez — callsign Ghost, earned for a night-landing record nobody had asked him to set — was one of two F-16 pilots scrambled from Beauvechain a little after midnight. He had 1,400 hours. He had intercepted Soviet probing flights over the North Sea. He was, by temperament and training, the least superstitious man in the Belgian Air Force, which is exactly why the account is told through him: because what happened that night broke something in a man built specifically not to break.

They went up into a clear cold sky. Ground radar vectored them toward the contact. And Ghost's own radar — the APG-66 in the nose of his aircraft, a good radar, a radar he trusted with his life on a routine basis — locked onto something.

The lock was solid. The symbology was clean. There was an object out there in the dark, and his radar was holding it, and he turned his aircraft toward it and looked through the canopy at the exact patch of sky the radar insisted held a target the size of a small building.

There was nothing there. Just stars.

## II. THE LOCK THAT LIED

You have to understand what that does to a fighter pilot, because it is the whole of this story.

A pilot's radar is not a suggestion. It is the extension of his senses into a dark his eyes cannot pierce. When the radar holds a lock, there is a thing there — that is the contract, that is the physics, that is the ground under a combat pilot's entire professional existence. Ghost had a lock. The lock said: object, that bearing, that range, that closure. And his eyes said: empty sky.

He did the only thing training allows. He trusted the instrument over the eye. He pursued the lock.

And the lock *ran.*

The object — the thing that was not visibly there but that his radar swore was — began to move in ways that the account will simply report, because they are in the real record and they are the point. It changed altitude by thousands of feet in a single second, a rate of descent that would have reduced any crewed aircraft, and its crew, to a smear. It accelerated past speeds his own airframe could not survive. It made a turn with no arc — not a tight turn, *no turn,* a direction change with no curve between the two headings, as if the object had simply stopped being one vector and started being another without passing through the space between. His radar, faithfully, tried to follow. The lock broke. He re-acquired. It ran again. Down toward the ground this time, down toward the lights of a sleeping Belgian town, and his radar followed it down to an altitude that should have meant impact, and there was no impact, and then it was behind him.

Nine times. The account will hold to the record: over the course of that night, between Ghost's aircraft and his wingman's, they achieved lock after lock — the figure that entered the official Belgian Air Force report was, in effect, *we chased something our instruments could hold and our eyes could not find, through maneuvers no aircraft can perform, and we never once saw it, and it was always, effortlessly, gone.*

Toward the end, drenched in his own sweat inside a pressurized cockpit, Ghost did a thing he never reported and told only one person, years later, near the end of his life. He stopped chasing the lock. He leveled his wings, and he flew straight, and he spoke out loud inside his helmet to the empty sky his radar swore was occupied.

He said, in Walloon French: *"What are you?"*

And the lock — which had been running, evading, treating his multi-million-franc fighter as a curiosity to be effortlessly slipped — the lock came and sat off his wing.

---

## III. OFF THE WING

There was nothing to see. That never changed. But the radar held it now, steady, matched to his exact speed and heading, close — the symbology said *close,* closer than a wingman flies, a thing the size of a building holding formation off his left wing at a distance where he should have been able to reach out and touch a hull, and there was only night air and stars.

And Ghost understood, in the animal part of him that his training had spent fifteen years suppressing, that he was being *looked at.* That the thing off his wing had stopped running not because he had caught it — he could no more catch it than a man catches the wind — but because he had, at last, done something other than pursue. He had asked. And the thing had come to consider the asker.

He felt it in his teeth first. A vibration, sub-audible, coming up through the airframe, through the seat, into his molars and the bones of his skull — a deep sustained tone below the reach of the engine noise, below anything the aircraft could produce, a frequency he did not hear so much as *was briefly played by.* And with the tone came, unbidden, uninvited, not a voice but a *knowing,* dropped into him whole the way the whole shape of a nightmare arrives in the instant of waking:

*We are not hiding from your machines. Your machines cannot be hidden from. We are hiding from you — from what your kind does with a thing once it can point at it. The lock you hold is a courtesy. We let your radar keep us so that you would chase and not fire. A pilot chasing a mystery does not shoot. A pilot who has lost the mystery does.*

And then the deepest part of the knowing, the part that took the mystery away and left Ghost, for the rest of his life, unable to look at a clear night sky without a specific grief:

*You were never the hunter tonight. You were the herded. We drew you up here, away from the towns, and let you chase, so that your ground would log a chase and not a landing — because the ones who read your logs must believe this was a thing you pursued and lost, not a thing that came down among your people and was seen. Fly home, Ghost. Report the lock you could not hold. It is the truest thing you can do for us, and you will never know you did it.*

The tone stopped. The lock, gently, slid away — not fleeing this time, just *leaving,* the way an adult leaves a room where a child has finally fallen asleep. His radar went clean. The sky was only sky.

Ghost flew home. He landed his record-quiet landing. And he wrote the report exactly as it happened, every impossible maneuver, every broken lock, the whole humiliating truthful catalogue of a fighter pilot outclassed by something he never saw — and in writing that true report, he did the precise thing the knowing had told him he would do, and served the precise purpose he had been herded to serve, and never once suspected it.

## IV. THE FILE THAT SAVED THE COVER

Here is why the Belgian wave matters more than any single sighting, and why it is a Season Three cornerstone.

The Belgian Air Force did something almost no institution ever does with an event like this: *it told the truth.* It released the radar data. It confirmed the F-16 chase. It said, publicly, in the sober language of a professional military, *we tracked objects we could not identify performing maneuvers we cannot explain, and we could not catch them.* Honesty, from the one kind of witness — trained, instrumented, credible — that the debunking apparatus normally relies on to stay quiet.

And it changed nothing.

Because the Legacy had learned, by 1990, the deepest lesson of the whole cover: that the truth, released *as a mystery,* is the safest possible outcome. A confirmed, official, unexplained chase is not a disclosure. It is an *entertainment.* It becomes a documentary, a forum thread, a "we may never know," a styrofoam-hoax photo that lets the lazy dismiss the whole thing and the credulous feel vindicated and *no one, anywhere, takes the single actionable step* the truth would demand — because a mystery, however official, asks nothing of you but wonder. The Legacy did not need to suppress the Belgian data. They needed only to let it be *a mystery* rather than *a fact,* and the difference between those two words is the entire architecture of eighty years of cover.

The craft understood this. That is what the knowing off Ghost's wing was really about. It herded a fighter pilot into logging an honest chase precisely because an honest, unresolvable chase is the perfect cover story — better than any lie, because it is *true,* and truth that leads nowhere is the most durable concealment there is. The night the Belgian Air Force told the truth was the night the cover worked best.

Ghost figured this out eventually. It is why he only told the story once, near the end, to one person. Not because no one would believe him — the opposite. Because everyone would believe him *pleasantly.* They would love it. They would call it fascinating. And that reaction, he had come to understand, was the trap: the mystery that delights is the mystery that never acts. He had been made into a storyteller against his will, and the story he carried was engineered to be enjoyed and dismissed, and there was nothing he could do with the truth that would not simply feed the machine that used truth as camouflage.

Except, maybe, this. To tell it once, plainly, to someone who would write it down not as a mystery to be enjoyed but as an *outcome* to be understood: that the craft are real, that they are not hiding from our instruments but from our reflexes, that they let us chase so we won't fire, and that the greatest weapon in the cover-up's arsenal is not the lie but the shrug.

## V. WHAT THE RADAR STILL HOLDS

Go and look; the Belgian wave is in the record. Two F-16s, the night of 30 March 1990, radar locks and impossible maneuvers, an air force honest enough to say so. It is presented, everywhere, as a wonderful unsolved mystery.

That presentation is the cover. Not a lie bolted over the truth — the truth itself, carefully left *unresolved,* so that it delights and demands nothing. Every time you read the Belgian case and feel that pleasant shiver of *we may never know,* you are completing the exact circuit a craft engineered off a fighter pilot's wing thirty-six years ago: mystery received, wonder felt, action declined, cover intact.

Ghost knew. He flew straight and asked *what are you,* and the answer he got was the saddest possible one — not a threat, not a wonder, but a quiet correction of his entire understanding of the sky: *we are not the mystery. The mystery is the cage they keep you in so you never move. Fly home and file it. You are helping us more by wondering than you ever could by knowing.*

He filed it. He wondered. He helped.

And now you know the outcome the mystery was built to hide — which means, for the length of this one story, the circuit is broken. Not received as wonder. Received as fact.

That is the only thing a herded pilot ever wanted, and the only thing the cover cannot survive: someone reading the true chase and, instead of shivering pleasantly, going very still, and understanding.
