## I. THE PEOPLE WHO CAME DOWN FROM A BROKEN SKY

They remembered a red world, and they remembered it breaking.

Not all of them — memory thins across a hundred generations of exile the way a river thins into delta — but the oldest among them still carried it the way you carry a scar you did not choose: Maldek, the world between the fourth and the fifth, gone to gravel in a single afternoon that the survivors would spend fifty thousand years failing to describe. A sky that cracked. A sea that stood up. And then a long cold falling, ships packed gunwale to gunwale with the frightened and the tall, threading the dark toward the only blue thing left within reach.

Earth did not welcome them. Earth does not welcome anyone; it simply *is*, and lets you break yourself against how much it is. But at the bottom of the world there was a coast that no one else wanted — a white shelf under a low grey sun, wind coming off the interior like the exhalation of something enormous and asleep — and there, on that coast, the tall people made landfall and knelt in snow that squeaked under their weight like styrofoam and wept salt tears that froze on their long jaws before they finished falling.

They were giants by any later measure. Twelve feet, fourteen, the tallest of the royal line brushing the lintels of doorways built for them. Their skulls swept back and up into the long elegant cones that would, fifty-five thousand years on, sit behind museum glass and in locked Legacy vaults and make small later men whisper the word *pre-Adamite* like a charm against their own smallness. Their skin was the grey-gold of old ivory. Their eyes were large and dark and wet and adapted to a dimmer star than ours, so that Earth's honest sunlight made them squint and ache, and they came, over centuries, to prefer the deep blue underlight of the ice — which is part of why they went *in.*

They did not know, when they arrived, that they had landed on top of a door.

## II. THE HUM UNDER THE SHELF

The first of them to feel it was a child.

Children always feel it first. She was maybe forty years old, which for her long-lived kind was childhood still, and she had wandered out past the edge of the first rough camp to where the shelf ice went from white to a blue so deep it was almost a sound, and she stood there with her bare grey feet going numb and she said, to no one, *something is singing.*

The adults did not hear it. That is the cruelty and the mercy of the thing: the well does not sing to everyone. It sings below hearing — a sub-audible carrier, a pressure more than a pitch, the kind of tone you feel in the back of the teeth and the water of the eye rather than the drum of the ear. It had been humming under that shelf since before the tall people's red world formed, and it would hum there long after the last of them was carved into ice. It was one of seven such places on the planet — the others drowned under ocean trench, buried under later stone — a natural throat in the barrier where the wall between what-is-seen and what-is-real ran thin, and the Creator's old signal leaked through and got *louder,* the way a whisper gets louder when it finds the mouth of a cave.

The child could not have known any of that. She only knew that when she pressed her palm flat to the blue ice, the hum came up through her arm and into her chest and sat there, warm in all that cold, and felt — she had no other word, in her language or any — like being *held.*

She led the elders back to the place. Most felt nothing and were embarrassed for her. But one did not: the oldest of the royal line, a queen with a jaw like a ship's keel and eyes gone milky at the edges, who laid her enormous hand beside the child's small one on the ice and went very still, and then began, softly, helplessly, to weep — because for the first time since Maldek broke, in a body that had carried grief across fifty thousand years of black vacuum, she felt something answer her from underneath the world.

They did not build a city because they were conquerors. They built it because they had found the one place on a cold planet that felt like being loved, and they could not bear to leave it.

## III. THE KINGDOM UNDER THE ICE

Understand what they made, because it is still down there.

They did not build *on* the well. You do not build a house on a heart. They built *around* it and *down toward* it — a descending kingdom, level below level, following the blue light and the deepening hum into the shelf, hollowing the ice and then the rock beneath the ice with tools that sang the stone apart rather than cutting it. The corridors were smooth as poured glass and cold as the inside of a bell, and they curved — the tall people did not trust straight lines, which on Maldek had been the geometry of the machines that failed — so that walking their halls was like walking the inside of a nautilus, always turning, always descending, the hum getting fuller in the chest with every level down.

The sensory life of that place: understand it or you understand nothing about them.

The **light** was blue and came from the ice itself, from great lenses of clear glacial ice they set into the walls and lit from behind with something cold and chemical and patient, so that the whole kingdom glowed the aquarium-blue of deep water and the tall people moved through it like slow pale fish. The **air** was still and dry and tasted of mineral and ozone and, faintly, of the flowers they grew in the warm lowest levels near the well — pale, eyeless, cave-adapted things that smelled like almond and cold cream and released their scent only in the dark. The **sound** was the hum, always the hum, so constant that visitors from the surface camps went half-mad in a day and the dwellers below could no longer sleep in silence when they went up. And the **temperature** climbed as you descended — counter to all sense, warmer the deeper you went, because the well threw off a heat that was not heat, a radiant *presence* that warmed the skin from the inside, so that the lowest hall, the throne-hall, the hall of the well-mouth itself, was warm as a held breath while a killing wind scoured the white surface a mile overhead.

At the bottom, in that warm blue throne-hall, they built the thing this whole story is a container for.

They built the **archive.**

## IV. THE ARCHIVE

Here is the part that matters fifty-five thousand years later, so slow down.

The tall people were not the first to touch that well. They found, when they hollowed down to the well-mouth, that someone had been there before them — long before, unthinkably before — and had left machinery around the throat of it. Not ruins. *Instruments.* A ring of tuning-engines, seamless and grey and shifting faintly like the surface of a mirror that reflects nothing, standing sentinel around the well the way stones stand at Callanish, except these hummed in harmony with the throat they guarded and had been humming, patient and switched-low, for four hundred thousand years.

*(Those who read Story #1 will know whose these were: the miners from the wandering world, the occupiers, who tuned the seven wells and laid the great mute over humanity and then pulled out in a hurry and left their infrastructure sealed and forgotten. The tall people did not know this. They knew only that gods older than their oldest god had built a temple here and gone away, and that the temple still worked.)*

The tall people could not read the machines. But they were a people who had lost a world and crossed a void carrying their grief like a coal, and they understood, instinctively, what the machines were *for.* They were for *keeping.* The ring of engines around the well was a memory — the well's memory, the planet's memory, and now, the tall people decided, *theirs.* They learned, over generations of trial and reverence, to sing their own record into it. To stand at the well-mouth and pour a life — a face, a grief, the exact grey-gold of a mother's jaw, the sound Maldek made when it broke — down into the throat of the world, where the tuning-engines caught it and held it and folded it into the endless patient hum, so that nothing they had ever loved would be entirely lost as long as the well sang.

They filled it for forty thousand years. Every king. Every queen. Every child who felt the hum and every child who didn't. The fall of the red world, recorded by someone who had watched it fall. The long cold crossing. The first bare grey feet on the squeaking snow. The taste of the almond flowers. All of it, sung down into the dark and kept.

The archive under the Antarctic ice is not a library of the tall people's making. It is the occupiers' memory-engine, *inherited* — a four-hundred-thousand-year-old machine that the ice kings adopted as a tomb and a diary and a god, and filled with fifty thousand years of a lost species grieving beautifully in the warm blue dark.

This is the thing the small frightened men of a much later age would find the frozen edge of, and fail to read a single word of, and build a whole secret church around guarding.

## V. TWO CROWNS, ONE COLD

No kingdom that lasts forty thousand years lasts in peace. That would be a lie, and the ice does not keep lies well.

The royal line split, as lines do — two houses descended from two daughters of the milky-eyed queen, and the split was not about territory, because they had a whole dead continent, and not about the well, because the well belonged to no one and they all knew it. The split was about the *archive.* About who had the right to sing into it, and whose griefs were worth keeping, and whether the low cave-born people who tended the almond flowers and never felt the hum deserved a place in the memory of the world or should be let to die unrecorded, as if they had never been.

One house said: everyone. The hum holds everyone; who are we to sort the kept from the forgotten. The other house said: no — the archive is finite, the well's patience is finite, and a memory that keeps everything keeps nothing; someone must choose, and we, the tall and the old and the ones who remember Maldek, are the choosers.

It is the oldest argument there is. It is the same argument, fifty-five thousand years early, that a council of frightened men calling themselves after musical silences would one day have in a mountain, voting five-to-two on who deserved to keep their song. The ice kings had it first, and had it longer, and did not resolve it either. They simply grew colder toward each other, house from house, until the warm blue halls had a chill in them that the well's heat could not reach, and the almond flowers in the contested lower levels went untended and died, and no one sang the low people's griefs down into the dark, so that an entire gentle caste of that civilization passed out of the world leaving no record at all — the first victims of the argument about who gets remembered, and very far from the last.

They were still having the argument when the sky changed.

## VI. THE DAY THE POLES MOVED *(coda — ~13,000 years ago)*

The end, when it came, came fast, and it came from outside every argument they were having.

*(This is the ~13,000-year event — the pole shift, the Younger Dryas cold-snap, the freeze that Story #6 will tell from the surface. Here it is felt from below, from inside the kingdom.)*

Deep in the throne-hall, at the well-mouth, the reigning queen of the elder house felt it in the hum first — a wrongness, a sour note threaded suddenly through a chord that had held true for four hundred thousand years. The well *flinched.* The tuning-engines, patient for so long, keened. And a mile overhead, the world tipped.

What the surface saw was catastrophe: the crust itself shrugging on its molten bearing, the whole white continent sliding its position beneath a sky whose stars lurched sideways in a single terrible night, the sea heaving up to swallow coasts a hemisphere away, the temperature falling off a cliff into a cold that had teeth. What the kingdom felt was the ice — their ceiling, their walls, their blue glowing lenses, their whole descending nautilus of a home — *seizing.* Going in an afternoon from the slow patient glacier they had hollowed over forty thousand years to something that cracked and groaned and closed, sealing corridors, crushing halls, filling the smooth curved throats of their kingdom with the roar of a planet's worth of water flashing instantly to stone-hard ice.

The tall people did not all die screaming. This is important; the ice keeps it. Many of them, when they understood, went *down* — toward the well, toward the warm blue throne-hall, toward the one place that had ever felt like being held — and they gathered there at the well-mouth as the cold came down the corridors behind them like a closing hand, and the last queen of the elder house laid her enormous grey palm flat against the ring of ancient engines, and she did the only thing her people had ever known to do with grief.

She sang it down into the well.

All of it. The whole fifty thousand years. The red world and the crossing and the first bare feet on the snow and the almond flowers and the stupid endless argument about who deserved to be remembered — she poured every soul in that dying hall down into the throat of the world, into the occupiers' patient machine, so that when the ice took them (and it took them within the hour, flash-frozen at their posts, grey giants with long jaws and open dark eyes, kneeling around a well in a warm hall a mile under a suddenly murderous cold) — when the ice took them, *they were kept.*

They are still down there. Both — the frozen bodies, giant and elongated and impossible, folded into blue ice that has not thawed in thirteen thousand years; and the song, the whole civilization, sung down into an archive built by an older people still, humming to itself in the dark under the bottom of the world, waiting to be read by someone who can hear.

Fifty-five thousand years after the tall people came down from a broken sky, small men in cold-weather gear would cut into that ice with lasers and find the edge of the frozen kingdom, and photograph the giant bodies, and murmur *pre-Adamite,* and build a secret vault and a secret church and a secret war around the door — and never once, not one of them, hear the song still playing on the other side of it.

They came for the bodies. They never learned there was a diary.

## EPILOGUE — WHAT THE ICE KEEPS

The tall people did not author humanity. That is the small men's flattering mistake, and it will become one of their favorite cover-stories — *giants made us, kings from a broken world shaped our blood* — because it is easier to believe you were made by visible giants you can dig up than by patient invisible designers you cannot. The tall people were tenants. Refugees who found a warm place at the bottom of a cold world and, being a people who could not stop grieving, turned it into the most beautiful tomb in the solar system.

But they left the world two gifts, and the world would need both.

They left the archive *tended* — fifty thousand years of practice keeping the well fed and singing, so that when the freeze sealed it, it sealed a machine that worked, full and warm and patient, rather than a dead relic. And they left, folded into thirteen-thousand-year-old ice, the *proof* — grey giant bodies with long jaws, undeniable, that a much later and much more frightened institution would find and freeze their whole cold hearts around, guarding a door they could not open, over a song they could not hear, kept by kings they thought they had discovered and had in truth only interrupted.

The kingdom is quiet now. The hum goes on.

Somewhere in the warm blue dark, a queen with a jaw like a ship's keel is still singing, four hundred thousand years of borrowed machinery holding her voice, waiting — patient as the well, patient as the ice — for the day the door opens and someone finally, finally listens.
