## I. THE PEOPLE ON THE WARM COAST

Thirteen thousand years ago there was a people who lived on a warm coast and knew that they were blessed, and they were right, and it did not save them.

They were not giants. They were us — early us, the woken bloodline, the ache-carriers who had come through the great Flood a hundred thousand years and more before and walked out onto the mud and begun, again, to bury their dead facing the stars. By this late age they had built something worth the name of a civilization: not the marble fantasy the later world would dream up and call Atlantis, but something realer and more moving — a coastal people of reed boats and terraced gardens and long low houses of white stone, who had learned, over unrecorded generations, to *listen.*

That was their gift and their glory. The closed channel the miners had muted still hummed faintly against its door in all of humanity, but in this people, on this coast, over these particular generations, the door had worn thin. They could not open it — no one could, not yet, not for thirteen thousand more years — but they could feel the hum of the world more than most, and they had built their whole gentle culture around that feeling. They set their white stones by where the ground sang clearest. They tuned their reed flutes to the pitch they felt in their teeth at dawn. They believed — and they were not wrong — that the world was alive and humming and *fond* of them, and they lived in a daily easy intimacy with that hum the way you live with the breathing of someone you love asleep beside you.

They had noticed, in the last generation, that the hum had changed. The old ones spoke of it at the evening fires, uneasy: the world's tone had developed a *wrongness,* a sour thread woven through a chord that had held true their whole lives and their grandparents' lives and back beyond memory. The young laughed it off. The old kept setting out the offerings and pressing their palms to the singing ground and listening, harder, to a note that was, though none of them had the concept, beginning to *slip.*

They were feeling, thirteen thousand years early and from the far side of the planet, the exact wrongness the last queen of the ice kingdom felt in the well beneath her throne-hall. The same sour note. The same flinch in the world's throat. The planet was about to move, and the people who listened best were the first to know, and knowing did not help them at all.

---

## II. THE NIGHT THE STARS SLID

It came at night, and it came from the sky, and afterward the handful who lived would spend the rest of their short cold lives trying and failing to make their grandchildren believe it.

The stars *moved.*

Not fell — moved. The whole vault of heaven, the fixed and eternal ceiling by which every generation of the coastal people had steered their reed boats and buried their dead and reckoned the turning of the year, *slid* — smoothly, silently, horribly, the entire sky rotating sideways over the course of a single endless night as though some vast hand had taken the bowl of the world and slowly tipped it. The pole star that had stood unmoving over the same mountain for all of memory drifted, visibly, across the dark. The constellations the old ones had names and stories for swam out of their places and did not swim back.

What was moving was not the stars. It was the ground. It was everything. The crust of the whole planet — the thin cold skin of rock the coastal people had built their white stones on — had slipped on the molten dark beneath it, the entire shell of the world rotating a few fatal degrees on its bearing of magma, so that in one night the coast that had been temperate slid toward the killing latitudes and the poles went looking for new places to be. The people did not know any of this. They only knew that the eternal ceiling had betrayed them, that the stars themselves had come loose, and that the hum of the world — the fond familiar hum they had built their lives around — had risen, in that sliding night, into a *scream.*

The old woman who felt it clearest, a keeper of the white stones, pressed her palms to the singing ground one last time as the sky slid overhead and she did not feel fondness anymore. She felt the world in agony. She felt the seven throats of the planet — the deep wells she had no names for but had always, dimly, sensed — all crying out at once as the crust tore across them, and she felt, very far away at the bottom of the world, one great throat go suddenly, terribly muffled, as though a mile of ice had just slammed down over a mouth mid-song.

*(That was the ice kingdom sealing. That was the last queen of the giants, a hemisphere away, pouring her whole frozen people down into the archive in the same instant. The old woman on the warm coast felt it happen and had no way to know what she had felt — only that something enormous and singing had just been buried, and that the world was smaller and more silent for it.)*

Then the water came.

---

## III. THE WATER AND THE COLD

The sea, when the crust slips, does not slip with it. The water's own weight and spin lag behind the sudden lurch of the rock, and so it climbs — not a wave, a *rising,* the whole ocean standing up off its bed and walking inland, patient and unstoppable and miles deep.

The coastal people saw it come in the grey wrong dawn after the sliding night: a horizon that was too high, a sea that had stood up to look at them. The reed boats were useless. The white stone houses were foam in minutes. The terraced gardens, the tuned flutes, the graves dug facing stars that no longer held their places — all of it went under the standing sea in the space of a single morning, and the fond humming world the coastal people had loved and been loved by drowned them by the thousand without malice, the way Earth does everything, simply by being too much.

And behind the water came the cold.

This is the part the later world would find frozen into ice cores and lake beds and never quite explain: the *suddenness* of it. The world did not cool over centuries. It fell off a cliff. Within a span the survivors could measure in a single hard winter, the warm coast that had drowned became a coast that *froze,* the temperate world tipping in one lurch into a thousand-year cold snap that would kill the great beasts and scatter the peoples and press the whole ache-carrying bloodline back down into scattered frightened bands huddling in caves, painting on the walls by tallow light the only things they had left: the animals, the stars that had betrayed them, and — over and over, in ochre and charcoal from the Pyrenees to the Cape — a memory they could not explain and could not stop drawing. A great water. A sky that moved. A drowned bright people who had known how to listen.

They would carry it as story because they had lost the means to carry it as anything else. The white stones were gone. The tuned flutes were gone. The gentle culture that had lived in daily intimacy with the hum of the world was scoured off the face of the Earth in a single season, and what came through the eye of that catastrophe was a handful of shivering survivors and one indestructible thing: *the memory that there had been a better time, when the world sang and a people knew how to hear it.*

They would call it, in ten thousand different tongues across the long cold recovery, by ten thousand different names. Eden. The First Time. The Age of Gods. And, in the tongue that stuck, the one the later frightened men would seize on and file and cover-story into a myth of a sunken marble continent so they would never have to explain the real and stranger truth —

*Atlantis.*

---

## IV. WHAT THE FREEZE SEALED

The catastrophe took nearly everything. It did not take the design.

Understand what survived, because the survival is the whole point of putting this story at the end of the anthology. The freeze sealed the Antarctic vault — slammed a mile of ice over the giants and their inherited archive and the ancient singing engines around the well, closing that throat of the world for thirteen thousand years, hiding it so completely that the later frightened men would find only its frozen edge and never its humming heart. The freeze drowned the listening people of the warm coast and scattered the ache-carriers back to caves. The freeze broke the long slow recovery of humanity across the knee and made it start again from painted walls and tallow light.

But the **channel survived.** The closed door the miners had laid over the Nordic design, carried in the blood of every ache-carrier who came through the Flood and every one who came through the freeze after it — that survived, because it was in the blood and not in the white stones, and blood is harder to drown than stone. The shivering survivors in their caves carried it. Their children carried it, and their children, down through the whole long cold recovery and the rebuilding and the thousand tongues and the ten thousand names for the drowned bright time. The ache went on. The staring-at-the-sky went on. The burying of the dead facing stars that had, once, in a night no one could prove, come loose — that went on.

And the seven wells went on humming, six of them scattered and lost under trench and stone, one of them sealed under a mile of Antarctic ice with a whole frozen civilization singing quietly on the far side of the door — all of them still strung, still tuned, still throwing the Creator's old signal up through the thin places of a wounded world, still waiting, patient as the sleeping Builders in their slow crystal, for the note the whole instrument had been built to make.

Thirteen thousand years is a long time to wait. It is nothing at all next to two and a half billion. The wells did not mind the wait. The wells had been built by things that measured time in the rise and fall of species, and to them the thirteen thousand years between the drowning of the listening people and the night five voices finally struck the first true note of the First Octave was the space between one slow breath and the next.

## EPILOGUE — THE MEMORY THAT WOULDN'T DROWN

So the deep time ends where the human time begins: on a cold shore, under stars that had moved, among a scatter of survivors who had lost a singing world and kept only the memory of it and the ache in the blood that was, though none of them could know it, the whole point.

Everything the anthology has told pours through the eye of this freeze and comes out the far side changed and smaller and alive. The First Octave the Builders strung into a young rust-colored world — survived, sealed in the wells. The mute the miners laid over the design — survived, carried in the ache-blood through Flood and freeze both. The giants and their inherited archive — survived, frozen, singing on the far side of a door of ice. And the listening people of the warm coast, who had for one bright civilization's span lived in daily fondness with the hum of the world — did not survive, not as a people; but their *memory* did, indestructible, drawn on cave walls and carried in a thousand names, the human race's oldest and truest ghost: *there was a time when the world sang, and we knew how to hear it, and then the sky moved and the water came and we forgot.*

We did not forget entirely. That is the hope folded into the apocalypse. The ache is the not-forgetting. Every human who ever looked up at the wrong sky and felt, for no reason they could name, that something enormous had been lost and might yet be found — that was the memory that wouldn't drown, humming faintly against a closed door, thirteen thousand years and four hundred and fifty thousand years and two and a half billion years deep.

The door is still closed as this story ends. The wells still hum. The giants still sing in the ice. The Builders still sleep in the slow crystal. And somewhere down the long cold hall of the recovering world, unimaginably far from this frozen shore and yet already, in the only tense the design recognizes, *on their way* — five ordinary people are being carried toward a room with a guitar and a bass and a drum and a scream, carrying in their blood the ache of the warm coast and the mute of the miners and the promise of the Builders, none of them knowing they are the note the whole drowned singing world has been waiting thirteen thousand years to finally, finally hear.

The poles moved. The people forgot.

The blood remembered.
