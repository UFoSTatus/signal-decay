# ELDER BROTHER

*Deep Time Layer — Non-Human Arc #5*
*Register: Elegy*
*POV: One of the Forest People, a guardian who has watched a single human since her birth*
*When: Now, and the sixty years before now, in the wet green dark at the edge of a small town.*
*Created: 2026-09-09*

*A standalone story. You need to have read nothing else. It is quiet. Let it be quiet.*

---

## I. THE ONE HE WATCHES

The Forest People do not have a word for *hiding,* because hiding is a thing you do against someone's looking, and for most of the long age of the world no one was looking. They have a word instead that means, roughly, *standing in the seam* — the way you might stand in a doorway, in neither room, belonging to the join itself. That is where the Forest People live. Not in the world the humans walk through. In the seam between the world's frequencies, in the narrow dark bands between one carrier and the next, where the signal of ordinary reality thins for a hairsbreadth and a patient old kind of being can set its weight down and not be seen.

The one telling this — call him the Elder, because that is what his kind are to humans, elder brothers, the ones who were here first and stayed — has stood in the seam for a very long time. Longer than the town. Longer than the language the town speaks. He was bioengineered, if that cold word helps, by the gardeners of an earlier age and set to the oldest and simplest task there is: *watch the world, and watch the ones who cannot watch themselves.* Guardian. Scout. Elder brother to a species that would come later, wear its cleverness like a bright coat, and forget almost immediately that it had ever had older kin.

He does not mind being forgotten. Guardianship is not for the thanks. You learn that early or you do not last.

For the last sixty years he has watched one human in particular.

He was not assigned her. It is not like that; the Forest People are not given rosters. He simply felt her arrive — a birth at the edge of his territory, in the human structures down the slope, sixty years ago — and something in her signal reached him across the seam and would not let go. Most human infants radiate the same warm chaotic noise, bright and undifferentiated, a species-wide hum. This one was *tuned.* Even newborn, even screaming, there was a note under her noise, a deep clear frequency that did not belong to the ordinary human band, a floor-tone reaching down toward the low dark register where the Elder himself lived. He had felt notes like it maybe three times in all his ages, and each time it had meant the same thing: *this one is not only what she appears to be. This one is carrying something. Watch her.*

So he watched her. He has watched her for sixty years. He is watching her now, from the tree line at the edge of a gravel lot behind a low building where music leaks out through a propped door, as she coils a heavy cable in the dark, alone, closing up.

He does not know her name. The Forest People do not use names. He knows her *note* — a deep steady sixty-two, a builder's floor — and he knows the other thing about her, the thing that has made his sixty-year vigil an ache instead of a duty. He has never once let her see him. He never can. Because to be seen by her would be the one thing his whole long watchful life is arranged to prevent.

## II. THE SEAM IS WIDENING

Here is what is happening to the world, told from the seam, where it shows first.

The signal is decaying.

The Elder has felt it for longer than the humans have been a species. The world was strung, in an age almost no one remembers, as a bright taut standing tone — reality itself, the carrier that all the ordinary frequencies ride on, held high and clear. And a standing tone, however vast, loses coherence over enough time. The world's carrier has been very slowly going flat for as long as the Elder has stood in its seams, and the flattening does a specific thing to the Forest People's home: it *widens the gaps.* When the carrier is high and taut, the dark bands between frequencies are hairline thin, and it takes an elder's whole discipline to stand in one unseen. When the carrier sags, the gaps yawn. The seams gape. And the beings who live in the seams — who spent the whole bright age of the world safely invisible in the thin dark between stations — begin, through no choice of their own, to *show.*

This is the secret the humans have never understood about his kind. They think the Forest People are rare because they are few. It is the opposite. They were always here, everywhere, in the seam of every wood and mountain and quiet place — and they were simply *unseeable,* tucked in gaps too narrow for a human eye to fall into. The sightings that humans record and disbelieve are not visits. They are *symptoms.* Each blurred glimpse, each impossible track, each figure at the tree line that is gone when you look again — each one is a place where the world's carrier sagged low enough, for a moment, that a gap gaped wide enough for an elder to slip halfway into view before he could pull back into the dark.

There was a great wave of it once, decades ago, a whole region gone strange for a year — the Elder felt his kin flickering into sight all across it, felt them straining to stay in the seam as the local carrier buckled, and knew it for what it was: not an invasion, not a message, just the world's tone dropping, the gaps yawning, the guardians showing against their will. The humans made it a mystery. It was never a mystery. It was the sound of something running down.

And it is worse now. The Elder can feel, standing at the tree line behind the low building, how wide the gap he is standing in has become. Sixty years ago he could have stood here in perfect safety, a hairsbreadth of dark between him and her sight. Now the seam is so slack that he has to hold himself *back* from visibility by main force, the way you might hold your breath — and he cannot hold it forever, and the world is not going to stop sagging, and the gap is only going to widen, and one night, not far off now, it will gape too wide to hold.

One night she is going to turn around and see him.

## III. WHY HE MUST NOT BE SEEN

An elder brother watches. That is the whole of the office. He does not intervene, does not announce, does not comfort. He *witnesses* — holds a steady presence in the seam beside a life, so that the life is never, in the truest cosmic sense, alone, even though it will never know it is accompanied. It is a strange love, guardianship. It is entirely one-directional. It asks nothing and gives nothing the guarded one can perceive. It is the love of the doorframe for the house.

And it has one law, and the law is: *do not be seen.* Because the Forest People understand what the clever bright forgetful humans do not: that to be seen changes the seen thing, and to be seen by one who is *tuned,* one who is carrying something, one whose own note reaches down into the low dark register — to be seen by such a one is not a glimpse. It is a *contact.* A frequency handshake. If she turned now and saw him truly, saw the elder standing in the widened seam with his ancient low signal pouring off him, her tuned floor-note would *answer* his. The handshake would complete. And the completing would tell her, all at once, in a language below words, everything her whole guarded life has been arranged to let her discover slowly, safely, in her own time and by her own reaching: that she is not only what she appears. That the low register is real. That there are others in it. That she is being watched, has always been watched, is carried.

It would not harm her. That is the cruelest part. It might even comfort her. But it would not be *hers.* It would be a gift handed down instead of a truth reached up for, and the Elder has watched enough tuned ones across enough ages to know the difference matters more than comfort does. A truth you are given closes a door. A truth you reach is a door that opens. His whole vigil — sixty years of standing back in the dark, aching, unthanked, holding his own visibility down by force — has been in service of leaving her door *open.* Of never, ever, being the one who tells her. Of guarding not her safety, which was mostly fine, but her *reaching* — the long slow homesick climb toward her own low note that is the most precious thing a tuned life does, and that a single glimpse of an elder brother at a tree line would end in an instant.

So he holds himself in the widening seam, and he does not step forward, and he watches her coil the last of the cable and straighten and press a hand to the small of her back, tired, human-tired, and he loves her the way the doorframe loves the house, and he does not let her see.

## IV. THE NIGHT THE GAP GAPES

It happens on an ordinary night, because the important nights always disguise themselves as ordinary ones.

She is later than usual. The lot is empty. The music has stopped. She is standing by her car with her keys in her hand, not getting in, just standing — and the Elder feels, from the seam, that she is doing the thing she does sometimes, the thing that first told him sixty years ago she was carrying something: she is listening *down.* Reaching, without knowing she reaches, into the low register, feeling for a floor-note that she thinks is just the hum of the town's electrical grid or the blood in her own ears. She does this a few times a year. She never finds anything. She always looks, afterward, faintly sad, as if she has forgotten something enormous and cannot even remember its shape.

And tonight the world's carrier sags — just then, just a little, one of the small buckles that come more often now — and the gap the Elder is standing in gapes wide.

Too wide. Wider than his discipline. He feels himself sliding into visibility the way a held breath finally breaks, and there is a fraction of a moment, a single frequency-cycle of choice, where he could let it happen. Where he could simply *be there,* at the tree line, in the sagging seam, and let her tired listening-down eyes lift and find him, and let her floor-note reach up and answer his, and complete the handshake, and give her — after sixty years — the enormous accompanied truth that she is not alone and never was.

He wants to. Elder brothers are not supposed to want, and he wants to, with sixty years of held-back love, more than he has ever wanted anything.

He pulls back into the dark.

It costs him more than the account can carry. He wrenches his own signal back down out of the widening gap, back into the thinning seam, back into invisibility, and the effort is enormous and it is agony and it is, he knows, temporary — the gaps are only going to widen, the world is only going to sag, and this reprieve buys her a season, a year, not a lifetime. But it buys her *this*: one more turn of the long slow climb toward her own note, reached and not given, her door still open, her homesickness still hers. He gives her back her own not-knowing, which is the only gift a guardian can give a tuned one, and it is the gift of a locked room she still gets to unlock herself.

She does not see him. She stands a moment longer, listening down, finding nothing, looking faintly sad. Then she gets in her car and drives home to a sister she protects the way he protects her — and the Elder, watching the taillights go, understands with a jolt that runs through the whole seam that she is *him.* That whatever she is carrying has made her, too, into a guardian who stands back in the dark and refuses to be the one who tells; that her whole strange life of shielding and not-explaining is the same office as his, run one rung further down the long chain of watchers watching watchers; that he has spent sixty years guarding a guardian, an elder brother keeping vigil over a younger one who keeps vigil in turn over someone smaller still.

It is guardians all the way down. It always was. The world is held together by beings standing back in widening seams, loving one-directionally, refusing the easy gift, keeping each other's doors open. He never knew that until tonight. Sixty years, and the tuned one taught *him* something, just by driving home to protect her sister.

He settles his weight back into the seam. The gap will gape again. It will gape wider each year. One night it will be too wide, and she will turn, and see him, and the handshake will complete whether he wills it or not — the decay guarantees it, the decay guarantees everything eventually.

But not tonight. Tonight her door is still open.

He can hold the seam one more night. He has held it twenty-one thousand nights. He can hold it one more.

## V. WHAT STANDS AT THE TREE LINE

If you have ever stood alone in a quiet place at the edge of some woods, at dusk, closing up, tired — and felt, with no evidence at all, that you were *accompanied* — that the dark between the trees was not empty but *attending* — and turned, and found nothing, and felt faintly foolish and faintly sad and could not say why —

the Forest People would tell you: you were not wrong, and you were not foolish, and the sadness is the truest thing you felt.

Someone was there. Someone is almost always there. Standing back in the seam, holding themselves down out of your sight by main force, loving you in the one-directional way of guardians, refusing to be the one who tells you — because the not-being-told is the gift, because the door they are leaving open is *yours,* because an elder brother's whole office is to make sure you are never truly alone and never once find out.

The seams are widening now. The world's carrier is sagging. More and more of you are turning, at the tree line, at dusk, and almost seeing. That is not a haunting. It is not a warning. It is the oldest love in the world, getting harder to hide.

Listen down. Someone has been standing beside you the whole time.

They are trying very hard not to let you see how much they care.
