# THE WARNING

*Main-Plot Story · Agent Zhao (Corinne) → Signal Decay · ~2026*
*Register: the turn — watcher becomes warner*
*Created: 2026-09-23*

*This is a main-plot beat, not a side-story. Agent Zhao has watched the Five since they converged. She voted with her silence when the Seven Rests moved toward deploying the Dampener. She has been coming apart since São Paulo — since Ana Lucia, the Resonant whose gift she watched the machine erase forever. This is the night she stops being the Legacy's eyes and becomes the band's warning. Everything the season has built toward turns here.*

---

## THE MEMO

The memo came down through the Third Rest on an ordinary Tuesday, in the flat bureaucratic language the Legacy used for the things that mattered most:

**RE: Subject Cluster SD-5 ("The Frequency" convergence). Escalation to Tier One. The Seven Rests have reviewed field agent reports and authorize preparation for Neutralization. Dampener logistics to commence. Assessment window: 30 days. Recommend continued passive surveillance pending deployment order.**

Corinne Zhao read it in her van, in the dark, outside The Frequency, with the directional mic still warm from a practice she'd just listened to — Kael's small careful voice, Jude's clamped low end, Oren's relentless tick, Mira laughing at something, Zara saying almost nothing, and under all of it a warm tired voice she'd deleted from the record three weeks ago describing the whole thing by ear.

Thirty days. Then the machine.

She'd known it was coming. She'd written the reports that made it possible — *five bright signatures, converging, unprecedented composite carrier* — because that was the job, and she was good at the job, and she'd told herself for two years that reporting was not the same as pulling the trigger.

She thought about Ana Lucia.

---

## WHAT THE MACHINE DOES

Corinne had been to São Paulo in 2020. A welfare check. Confirm the suppression held.

It held. That was the horror of it. Ana Lucia Dos Santos — who at nineteen had sung to the rain and made it *dance* — was thirty-one when Corinne met her, and she was fine. Employed. Married. Ordinary. And when Corinne, off-script, had asked her gently whether she ever missed it, whether she ever felt the old thing stir, Ana Lucia had looked at her with a confusion so total it was its own answer.

"Missed what?" she'd said.

She didn't remember. The Dampener hadn't just switched off her gift. It had reached into the place where the gift *lived* — the place that was inseparable from her — and it had taken the whole room, and sealed the door, and painted over where the door had been. Ana Lucia didn't grieve her music because Ana Lucia no longer contained the part of herself that had ever known there was music to grieve.

*That* was neutralization. Not death. Worse. A person walking around in the shape of someone who'd been amputated from herself and didn't know the limb was gone.

And the Seven Rests had just authorized it. Five times. For Kael, who thought his voice was theft. For Jude, who thought his was a bomb. For Oren, who guarded a door in the floor with a rhythm. For Mira, who'd finally, after a lifetime of being called crazy, found people who saw what she saw. For Zara, who was older than any of them knew and had been waiting, Corinne suspected, for something for a very long time.

*They found each other,* Corinne had written, in the report that started her cracking. *They don't even know what they are and they found each other.*

Thirty days. Then five Ana Lucias.

---

## THE THING SHE COULDN'T UNKNOW

She sat in the van a long time.

The levee. That was Vought's word for what they were — *we are the levee that holds back the flood until the valley is ready.* Corinne had believed it, once. That Resonants, uncontrolled, were a danger — to themselves, to the barrier, to the fragile quiet that kept the worse things from noticing Earth. That the Dampener was mercy. Containment. Levee, not weapon.

But she'd sat outside this venue for months and the flood had never come. What came instead was five broken people slowly becoming less broken by being in a room together. What came was a girl with no signature who told each of them, one at a time, that they weren't monsters — that the door was an instrument, the bomb was a foundation, the clock was a guard. What came was the least dangerous thing Corinne had ever surveilled: people, healing, through music, in a room that sat on an old buried note.

The Legacy's whole justification was that the flood was coming. Corinne had watched for two years and seen no flood. She'd seen a band.

You cannot unknow a thing like that. She'd tried. It was why she didn't sleep. It was why she'd deleted Ami's entry. It was why, when the memo said *thirty days,* the thing that broke in her was not her faith — that had been breaking for two years — but her *paralysis.* The luxury of watching. The lie that observing wasn't acting. Thirty days took that away. In thirty days there would be no more neutral ground. There would only be the people who let it happen and the people who didn't.

Corinne Zhao decided, in a van, in the dark, which one she was going to be.

---

## THE APPROACH

She didn't do it cleverly. There was no clever way.

She waited three more days — long enough to copy what she'd need, quietly, the small careful thefts of someone who'd spent eleven years learning exactly how the Legacy watched its own: the deployment timeline, the Dampener logistics, the assessment protocol, the names of the Seven Rests, the thing about the barrier and the wells and why the machine was more dangerous than they admitted. She put it on a drive that didn't exist in any system. Then she did the most dangerous thing an agent of the Legacy could do: she turned off her own porch light. Went dark. Stopped reporting.

And on a Thursday night, after practice, when the others had gone and only the sound tech remained coiling cable in the upstairs room, Corinne Zhao climbed the side stair of The Frequency — not with a mic, not with a camera, not as a watcher — and knocked on the door.

Zara Kovač opened it. And the two of them looked at each other, and Corinne understood, from the stillness in the other woman's ancient eyes, that Zara had known she was out there the whole time. Had heard the van. Had felt the watcher. Maybe for months.

"You're the one who's been listening," Zara said. Not a question.

"Yes," Corinne said.

"You're one of them. The ones who find us." Still not a question. No fear in it — something older than fear.

"I was," Corinne said. Her voice did not shake, which surprised her. "I'm here because in twenty-seven days they're going to bring a machine to this city and do to all five of you what they did to a woman in São Paulo who used to sing to the rain. And I can't —" the word caught, the first crack in the flat agent's calm "— I can't watch it happen again. I know what it takes out. I've met what's left afterward. And I would rather burn than be the reason there are five more of her."

Silence. The room-tone hummed in its corner — the well under the floor, the note Zara had helped tune longer ago than Corinne could imagine.

Then Zara stepped back and opened the door the rest of the way.

"Come in," she said. "Sit down. Start at the beginning." A pause, and something that was almost, almost a smile. "We've been waiting for one of you to break for a very long time."

---

## THE TURN

Corinne Zhao walked into the upstairs room at The Frequency and sat down in a folding chair and became, in the space of one conversation, the most valuable defector in the Legacy's history — and a traitor whose life expectancy, from that night, could be measured in the time it took the Seven Rests to notice she'd gone dark.

She told Zara everything. The Dampener. The timeline. The Rests. The barrier and the wells. What the machine did and what it cost. And Zara listened the way Zara listened to everything — completely, permanently, filing it into that endless perfect memory — and asked, at the end, only one question:

"Why us? Why warn us and not just run?"

And Corinne, who had spent eleven years being the levee, who had watched the machine erase a woman who sang to rain, who had sat outside a venue and slowly fallen in love from a distance with five people healing each other with sound, gave the answer that was the whole turn of the season:

"Because you're not the flood," she said. "I was told my whole career that you were the flood and the machine was the levee. I've watched you for two years. You're not the flood." She looked toward the corner, where the note hummed, where the buried well sang under the floor of a bar in an ordinary city. "I don't know what's coming. Maybe something is. But it isn't you. You're the first beautiful thing I've ever been ordered to destroy, and I'm done being the levee. I'd rather find out what you actually are."

Outside, the city went on, ordinary, unaware. Inside, an ancient soul and a broken agent sat in a room that sat on a note, twenty-seven days from a machine, and began — quietly, without any idea whether it would be enough — to plan.

The watcher had become the warning. The season had turned. And somewhere in the building, faint and buried and patient, the note that had been waiting longer than any of them had been alive hummed on, as if it had known all along that this was how it started: not with a flood, but with one person deciding not to look away.
